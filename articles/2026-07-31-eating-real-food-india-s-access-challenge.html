Eating Real Food: India's Access Challenge

📅 2026-07-31 📂 Health Original source ↗
Key points
  • Health advice alone won't solve India's nutrition crisis; access to real food is the real issue.
  • Affordability and availability of fresh, unprocessed foods are major barriers for many Indians.
  • Policy interventions must focus on improving supply chains and making nutritious food accessible to all.

The Advice Gap

Nutritionists and health experts have long urged Indians to ditch processed foods and embrace 'real food' — fresh vegetables, whole grains, and pulses. The advice is sound, but for millions, it rings hollow. The real challenge isn't knowledge; it's access.

In urban centres, organic stores and farmers' markets cater to the affluent. In rural areas and low-income neighbourhoods, the story is starkly different. Fresh produce is either unavailable or priced beyond reach. Processed foods, with their long shelf life and low cost, often become the default.

Affordability: The Silent Barrier

The cost of a healthy diet is a significant hurdle. A recent analysis suggests that a nutritious meal can cost nearly twice as much as a calorie-dense, nutrient-poor alternative. For a family living on a tight budget, the choice is often made by the wallet, not the waistline.

This isn't just about individual choices; it's a systemic issue. Agricultural policies, supply chain inefficiencies, and market distortions all play a role in making real food expensive. Subsidies often favour cash crops over nutritious staples, skewing what's grown and what's affordable.

Availability: Beyond the Metro

Even when people can afford fresh food, it isn't always there. Cold storage gaps mean that a significant portion of produce rots before reaching markets. In smaller towns and villages, the variety of fresh foods available is often limited to whatever is grown locally and in season.

This means that even motivated individuals find it hard to follow dietary guidelines. The problem isn't a lack of willpower; it's a lack of infrastructure. Without reliable access to markets that stock a range of fresh foods, the 'eat real food' advice becomes a privilege, not a prescription.

Policy Over Preaching

The solution, experts argue, lies not in more public health campaigns but in structural changes. Investing in cold chain logistics, improving rural road connectivity, and supporting smallholder farmers can all help make fresh food more available. On the demand side, targeted subsidies or price stabilisation mechanisms could make healthy food more affordable.

Some states have experimented with fortified foods and school meal programmes, but these are stopgaps. The long-term goal should be to create an environment where the healthy choice is the easy choice. That requires moving beyond advice and into action — on infrastructure, markets, and income support.

What to Watch

As India's public discourse on nutrition grows, the focus is slowly shifting from individual responsibility to systemic solutions. Watch for policy announcements on agricultural support and food distribution in the coming months. The test will be whether these measures actually put real food on every plate.
