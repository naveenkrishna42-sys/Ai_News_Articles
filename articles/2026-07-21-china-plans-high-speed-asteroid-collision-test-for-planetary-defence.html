China plans high-speed asteroid collision test for planetary defence

📅 2026-07-21 📂 Science Original source ↗
Key points
  • China plans to hit a near-Earth asteroid with a projectile moving at Mach 26 (9 km/s).
  • The test targets a smaller and less predictable asteroid than the one used in NASA's DART mission.
  • A second spacecraft will observe the impact to collect data on the collision's effects.

China is preparing for a landmark space mission: crashing a spacecraft into an asteroid at hypersonic speeds. The test, part of the country's planetary defence programme, aims to see if a kinetic impact can alter the path of a near-Earth object.

The projectile will hit the asteroid at Mach 26 — roughly 26 times the speed of sound, or 9 kilometres per second. A second spacecraft will trail the impactor to observe the collision and gather data.

A different target from NASA's DART

The mission differs from NASA's Double Asteroid Redirection Test (DART) in a key way. China is targeting a smaller asteroid that is less predictable in its orbit. DART hit the moonlet Dimorphos, which orbited a larger asteroid called Didymos. China's target is not tethered to a larger body.

Scientists say smaller asteroids are harder to track and more numerous. Successfully deflecting one would prove the technique works against a wider range of threats.

How the mission will work

The plan involves two spacecraft launched together. The first is the impactor — a high-speed projectile designed to slam into the asteroid's surface. The second is an observer craft that will hang back, recording the moment of impact and measuring any change in the asteroid's trajectory.

China has not announced a launch date or named the target asteroid. Details remain classified as the mission is still in the planning phase. The China National Space Administration has said it will release more information closer to the launch window.

Why Mach 26?

The extreme speed is deliberate. At 9 km/s, the impactor carries enormous kinetic energy. The goal is to transfer enough momentum to nudge the asteroid off its course, even if only by a few millimetres per second. Over time, that small change can add up to a significant shift in the asteroid's path.

A slower impact would require a much heavier spacecraft to achieve the same effect. Speed, in this case, is efficiency.

Global interest in planetary defence

China's test comes amid growing international attention on asteroid defence. NASA's DART mission in 2022 successfully altered the orbit of Dimorphos, proving the concept works. China's test aims to validate the approach on a more challenging target.

The European Space Agency is also planning its own follow-up mission, Hera, which will study the aftermath of DART's impact in detail. Together, these missions form a patchwork of planetary defence capabilities.

No known asteroid currently poses a threat to Earth. But space agencies argue that the only way to be prepared is to test the technology before it is needed.

China's test will be closely watched by astronomers and defence planners worldwide. A successful deflection of a smaller, fast-moving asteroid would mark a significant step forward. For now, the world waits for a launch date and a target name — details that will turn this plan into a countdown.
