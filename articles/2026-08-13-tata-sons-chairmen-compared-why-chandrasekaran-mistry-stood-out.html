Tata Sons Chairmen Compared: Why Chandrasekaran, Mistry Stood Out

📅 2026-08-13 📂 Business Original source ↗
Key points
  • N Chandrasekaran's tenure as Tata Sons chairman ends, marked by a focus on digital and consumer businesses.
  • Cyrus Mistry's ouster in 2016 remains a defining, contentious chapter in Tata Group history.
  • The incoming chairman faces three major cash-losing businesses and a shifting power structure.
  • Noel Tata's influence is seen as a key factor in the leadership transition.

The Tata Group is set for a historic transition as N Chandrasekaran prepares to step down as chairman of Tata Sons. His departure marks the end of a nearly decade-long tenure that reshaped the conglomerate's portfolio, but it also brings into sharp focus the contrasting leadership styles of the two most talked-about chairmen in recent memory — Chandrasekaran and his predecessor, Cyrus Mistry.

The announcement, confirmed by a board member to The Times of India, signals a new chapter for India's largest business house. But to understand what lies ahead, one must look back at how these two leaders navigated the group through very different storms.

The Marathoner's Method

N Chandrasekaran, often described as a marathoner technocrat, took the helm in 2017 after a long stint at Tata Consultancy Services (TCS). His leadership was defined by a relentless push into digital businesses and a consolidation of the group's sprawling interests. He streamlined operations, bet big on consumer tech, and worked to clean up the balance sheets of several subsidiaries.

His style was markedly different from Mistry's. Where Mistry was seen as a methodical, no-nonsense professional who focused on corporate governance and debt reduction, Chandrasekaran brought a growth-oriented, almost entrepreneurial energy to the chairman's office. He championed ventures like super-app Tata Neu and pushed for scale in electronics manufacturing, aligning the group with the government's 'Make in India' push.

Yet, his tenure was not without turbulence. The group's airline venture, Air India, has been a persistent challenge, and other businesses have struggled to turn profitable. The incoming chairman will inherit a portfolio that still carries three major cash-losers, a burden that will test the group's famed resilience.

The Mistry Years: A Contentious Legacy

Cyrus Mistry's chairmanship, though brief, remains one of the most debated corporate episodes in India. Appointed in 2012, he was ousted in 2016 in a boardroom coup that led to a bitter legal battle. His approach was to cut through the conglomerate's complexity, focusing on cash flow and exit from non-core sectors. That approach, however, clashed with the old guard's vision, leading to his dramatic removal.

Mistry's legacy is often framed as a cautionary tale about the limits of professional management within a family-owned empire. His attempts to rein in spending at certain group companies were viewed by some as necessary discipline, but by others as a threat to the group's traditional ethos. The Supreme Court's eventual ruling in favour of Tata Sons in 2021 closed the legal chapter, but the shadow of that battle still lingers over the group's governance.

What Sets Them Apart

The difference between the two men is not just one of tenure but of temperament. Chandrasekaran was a consensus-builder, often working behind the scenes to align the powerful Tata Trusts, which hold a majority stake in Tata Sons. He understood that survival in the role required managing relationships as much as managing businesses. Mistry, by contrast, was seen as more direct, willing to make unpopular calls without the same political finesse.

That distinction is crucial now. As Chandrasekaran exits, the group's power structure is visibly shifting. The Indian Express notes that his departure signals the tightening grip of Noel Tata, the half-brother of the late Ratan Tata. Noel's growing influence suggests that the group is moving toward a more family-centric leadership model, even as it grapples with professional management demands.

The Road Ahead

The next chairman will walk into a complex situation. The three cash-losing businesses — likely including Air India and some consumer ventures — require quick decisions. There is also the question of how the new leader will balance the group's philanthropic legacy with the need for profitability in a competitive market.

For now, the Tata Group stands at a crossroads. The board's choice of successor, expected in the coming weeks, will signal whether the group leans further into the family's vision or returns to the professional management model that Chandrasekaran embodied. What is certain is that the next leader will be compared, fairly or not, to both the marathoner who ran a steady race and the disruptor who dared to challenge the status quo. The group's ability to navigate this transition will define its next decade.
