Franklin India Medium to Long Term Fund Direct-IDCW: What the ₹10.72 NAV Actually Tells You

The Franklin India Medium to Long Term Fund Direct-IDCW carries a net asset value of ₹10.72 as reported on 13 September 2026. That figure requires context before you act on it. The IDCW (Income Distribution cum Capital Withdrawal) option periodically pays out realised gains, so each payout reduces the NAV by the distribution amount. A growth-option NAV in the same scheme would sit materially higher. Judging this plan purely on a low absolute NAV is a common retail error.

This is an open-ended debt scheme mandated to invest at least 80% of net assets in medium to long-term corporate bonds and government securities. It does not chase equity returns, and it does not promise fixed payouts. The IDCW is declared at the fund manager's discretion, subject to distributable surplus.

Asset Allocation: Where the Money Sits

Medium to long duration funds run a Macaulay duration of roughly four to seven years. That makes them sensitive to interest-rate movements: a 50 basis point fall in yields can lift NAV meaningfully, while a similar rise can dent returns. Franklin's fund typically holds a laddered portfolio of PSU bonds, NBFC paper, and state development loans, with a small cash and TREPS cushion for liquidity.

For buyers, the critical checks are credit quality and issuer concentration. A portfolio skewed to AA-rated paper offers higher accrual but carries default risk. A portfolio dominated by AAA PSU issuers offers lower yield but stronger capital safety. Read the latest monthly portfolio disclosure rather than relying on a single NAV print.

Direct vs Regular Plan: The Cost Math

The direct plan eliminates distributor commissions, which is why its expense ratio is often 50 to 60 basis points lower than the regular plan. On a ₹5 lakh investment held for five years, that gap can translate into roughly ₹15,000–₹20,000 of additional corpus, assuming similar portfolio returns. For a debt fund where gross yields may be 7–8%, paying 1% in costs is a meaningful drag.

IDCW versus growth is the second decision. IDCW suits investors who want periodic cash flow, such as retirees. Growth suits investors compounding wealth, since nothing is distributed and the NAV reflects total returns. Note that IDCW payouts are taxed as income from other sources at the investor's slab rate; growth-option gains after three years attract long-term capital gains tax on debt funds at the applicable rate.

Real-World Highlights and Trade-Offs

  • Pro: Direct plan cost advantage of roughly 0.5% annually versus the regular plan.
  • Pro: Portfolio anchored in AAA and AA+ corporate bonds, reducing credit default risk relative to credit-risk funds.
  • Pro: Low entry threshold of ₹5,000 lump sum or ₹500 SIP makes it accessible.
  • Con: Duration risk means returns can be negative in a sharp rising-rate cycle.
  • Con: IDCW payouts are not guaranteed and depend on distributable surplus.
  • Con: NAV of ₹10.72 is not a valuation signal; it is a function of prior distributions.

Who Should Buy This Fund

This scheme fits investors with a three to five year horizon who want accrual income from high-grade corporate bonds and can tolerate interim NAV swings. It does not fit investors seeking capital protection, daily liquidity, or equity-style growth. Compare it against other medium-duration direct plans on expense ratio, average maturity, and credit quality before committing.
