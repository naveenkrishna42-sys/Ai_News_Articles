NASA-ISRO Radar Satellite Captures First Image of Mount Desert Island

📅 2026-08-07 📂 Space Original source ↗
Key points
  • NISAR captures debut radar image of Mount Desert Island, Maine
  • Joint NASA-ISRO mission aims to monitor Earth's surface changes
  • First images validate satellite's radar systems for global coverage

First Light for NISAR

The NASA-ISRO Synthetic Aperture Radar (NISAR) satellite has transmitted its first radar image, capturing Mount Desert Island off the coast of Maine. The image, released by NASA's Jet Propulsion Laboratory, marks a significant milestone for the joint US-Indian mission.

Mount Desert Island, known for its rugged coastline and Acadia National Park, serves as a test target for calibrating the satellite's advanced radar instruments. The debut image confirms that NISAR's systems are functioning as intended.

What NISAR Will Do

NISAR is designed to scan nearly all of Earth's land and ice surfaces every 12 days. Its dual-frequency radar can detect movements as small as a few centimetres, making it ideal for tracking earthquakes, landslides, and glacial shifts.

The mission is a collaboration between NASA and the Indian Space Research Organisation (ISRO). It carries an L-band radar from NASA and an S-band radar from ISRO, allowing scientists to observe a wide range of surface changes.

Why Mount Desert Island?

The island offers a mix of forested terrain, rocky shores, and urban areas, making it a useful calibration site. Radar signals reflect differently off each surface, helping engineers fine-tune the satellite's data collection.

Initial results show clear distinctions between land and water, with the island's coastline sharply delineated. This bodes well for future observations of coastal erosion and sea-level impacts.

Global Monitoring Ahead

Once fully operational, NISAR will provide critical data on natural hazards, groundwater depletion, and polar ice dynamics. Scientists in both countries have already identified priority regions, including the Himalayas and the Antarctic ice sheet.

The satellite's ability to penetrate clouds and work in darkness ensures continuous monitoring, regardless of weather or time of day.

For India, NISAR also holds promise for agriculture and disaster management. The S-band radar can detect soil moisture and crop health, supporting better water-use planning.

Next Steps for the Mission

Over the coming weeks, engineers will continue to calibrate NISAR's instruments using other known targets. The satellite is expected to begin routine science operations shortly after.

Data from the mission will be made freely available to researchers worldwide, a key objective for both space agencies.

Officials have not yet confirmed an exact date for full operations, but the successful first image brings that moment closer.

As NISAR settles into its orbit, the focus now shifts to the wealth of data it will deliver—offering an unprecedented view of a changing planet.
