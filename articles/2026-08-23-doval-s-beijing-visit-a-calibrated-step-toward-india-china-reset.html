Doval's Beijing visit: A calibrated step toward India-China reset

📅 2026-08-23 📂 Top Stories Original source ↗
Key points
  • 25th Special Representatives round signals continuity in India-China diplomatic channels despite military standoff
  • Talks likely to focus on border stability, de-escalation, and laying groundwork for a possible Xi Jinping visit to India
  • Analysts see 'early harvest' agreement on patrolling as a realistic near-term goal, but structural trust deficit remains
  • Domestic political pressures in both countries complicate any substantive breakthrough

A familiar face, a familiar impasse

When National Security Advisor Ajit Doval lands in Beijing for the 25th round of Special Representatives (SR) talks, he walks into a room where the furniture has not moved much since 2020. The Ladakh standoff that began with the Galwan clash has cooled into a tense, managed equilibrium, but the core dispute over the Line of Actual Control (LAC) remains unresolved. This meeting, set against reports of a potential Xi Jinping visit to India, is less about a breakthrough and more about calibrating expectations.

The SR mechanism, revived after a five-year gap in December 2024, is the only high-level channel that has kept the bilateral relationship from freezing entirely. Doval's counterpart, Wang Yi, knows the script; the two have met multiple times, often in third-country venues. But the subtext this time is different: both governments are signalling that they want a functional, if not friendly, relationship.

What 'early harvest' actually means

The phrase 'early harvest' has been floated by diplomatic watchers, but its practical scope is narrow. It likely refers to an agreement on disengagement in the remaining friction points, particularly in the Depsang and Demchok areas, where troops still face off. A mutual commitment to avoid new patrols in contested zones, or a protocol for rapid de-escalation, could be signed without conceding on sovereignty claims.

That would be a win for both sides. India gets a reduction in military tension, freeing resources for other fronts. China gets a stable western flank as it manages economic headwinds and a more assertive US. But an 'early harvest' that goes beyond tactical measures—say, on trade or visas—is unlikely. The trust deficit, built over five years of standoff, cannot be repaired in one meeting.

Why the Xi visit matters more than the talks

The buzz around a Xi Jinping visit to India—his first since the 2019 Mahabalipuram summit with Modi—has given this round of SR talks an unusual urgency. A visit would be a diplomatic coup for New Delhi, signalling that the world's two most populous nations can manage their differences. For Beijing, it would demonstrate that India is not irrevocably aligned with the US-led Indo-Pacific strategy.

But the visit is not a given. Chinese diplomacy often links high-level engagements to concrete deliverables. If Doval returns without a clear framework on border stability, Beijing may postpone the visit, citing 'lack of conditions'. The ball is effectively in India's court to offer something palatable—perhaps a commitment to resume talks on the LAC's southern sector, or a joint statement on maintaining peace.

Domestic constraints on both sides

Neither leader has much room to compromise. In India, the political opposition and nationalist media would pounce on any concession seen as surrendering territory. In China, the 'wolf warrior' stance and the party's legitimacy tied to sovereignty claims make even minor adjustments risky. This is why the SR talks are structured as 'consultations', not negotiations—they allow both sides to save face.

The domestic angle also explains why Doval, not the foreign minister, is leading. The NSA's role is to convey firmness, while the MEA handles optics. It is a classic division of labour in Indian diplomacy, but it also means that any breakthrough will require coordination across ministries, which is slow.

What a realistic outcome looks like

Expect a joint statement that stresses 'positive and constructive' discussions, with a commitment to maintain dialogue. The 'early harvest' may be a set of confidence-building measures, such as reopening certain border trade points or resuming the Kailash Mansarovar Yatra, which was suspended in 2020. These are low-cost, high-symbolism steps.

Yet, the structural issues remain. The LAC's undefined nature, India's infrastructure build-up along the border, and China's military modernisation are not going away. This is a relationship of managed rivalry, not friendship. The best case is that Doval's visit prevents a relapse into crisis, while the Xi visit, if it happens, becomes a photo-op with limited substance.

  • Pros: Sustained diplomatic channel; potential for CBMs; reduced risk of accidental escalation; possible Xi visit boosts bilateral optics.
  • Cons: No resolution on core boundary issues; domestic politics may hinder implementation; China may use talks to buy time; India's strategic autonomy under pressure.

The tragedy of the India-China relationship is that both sides know the other's red lines, yet neither can move. Doval's trip to Beijing is an exercise in crisis management, not peacemaking. And that is okay—sometimes the best outcome is that nothing worse happens.
