Raja Ravi Varma: The Controversy Behind India's Most Iconic Artist

📅 2026-07-18 📂 Culture & Arts Original source ↗
Key points
  • Raja Ravi Varma is celebrated for popularising Indian art through oleographs of Hindu deities.
  • Critics argue his Westernised style distorted traditional iconography and objectified women.
  • His works remain widely reproduced, sparking ongoing debates about cultural representation.

Raja Ravi Varma is a name that resonates across India. His paintings of Hindu gods and goddesses adorn millions of homes, calendars, and temples. He is often called the father of modern Indian art. But behind the reverence lies a simmering controversy. Many now question whether his work truly represents Indian tradition or if it imposed a Westernised gaze on sacred imagery.

Born in 1848 in the princely state of Travancore, Ravi Varma mastered European oil painting techniques. He blended realism with mythological themes, producing works like 'Shakuntala' and 'Lady in the Moonlight'. His prints, mass-produced through his own press, made art accessible to the common man. For decades, he was seen as a democratiser of culture.

The Critique: Westernisation of Hindu Iconography

Modern art historians and cultural critics have turned a sharper eye on his legacy. They argue that Ravi Varma's gods look too human, too European. His figures often have fair skin, soft features, and poses reminiscent of classical Greek statues. Traditional Indian temple art, with its stylised, symbolic forms, was replaced by a literal, almost photographic style.

This shift, critics say, altered how Indians visualise their deities. The goddess Lakshmi in his paintings appears more like a Victorian-era lady than a celestial being. Some scholars call this a form of cultural colonisation, where Western artistic norms quietly supplanted indigenous aesthetics.

The Depiction of Women

Another layer of controversy involves his portrayal of women. Ravi Varma's female figures are often draped in sheer fabrics, with exposed midriffs and suggestive glances. While celebrated as beautiful, many now see this as objectification. His 'Nair Lady Adorning Her Hair' and 'The Galaxy of Musicians' show women in passive, ornamental roles.

Feminist art critics point out that these images catered to a male, colonial gaze. They argue that his work reinforced stereotypes of Indian women as either sensual or saintly. This dual representation, they say, continues to influence popular culture today, from cinema to advertising.

The Defenders: Art in Its Time

Supporters of Ravi Varma insist that he must be understood in his historical context. The late 19th century was a period of cultural flux. Indian artists were experimenting with new techniques brought by the British. Ravi Varma's work was groundbreaking precisely because it bridged two worlds.

His oleographs, they argue, gave ordinary Indians a way to connect with their faith. Before him, religious art was mostly confined to temples and wealthy homes. His prints were affordable and widely distributed, fostering a shared visual language across regions and castes. For many, this was a form of cultural empowerment, not erosion.

Art historian Partha Mitter has noted that Ravi Varma's style was a deliberate choice to appeal to a broad audience. The realism made the gods relatable. The controversy today, he suggests, reflects our own anxieties about identity and authenticity rather than the artist's intent.

The Legacy Today

Ravi Varma's works remain ubiquitous. They are printed on everything from wedding invitations to political posters. Yet, younger artists are now reimagining his subjects. Some subvert his imagery to critique caste and gender norms. Others reclaim traditional iconography using indigenous styles.

In 2021, a series of exhibitions in Delhi and Mumbai sparked fresh debate. Some attendees praised his technical skill; others walked out, calling his work 'offensive' and 'outdated'. The split is generational, with older viewers often defending him and younger ones demanding a more critical appraisal.

The controversy shows no sign of fading. As India grapples with questions of cultural ownership and representation, Ravi Varma remains a lightning rod. His art is both a national treasure and a contested symbol. What is clear is that the conversation about his legacy is far from over.

What happens next? Museums and collectors are now rethinking how to present his work. Some are adding contextual labels that address the critiques. Others are commissioning contemporary responses. The debate is likely to intensify as new generations ask: whose gods, whose art, whose India?
