Typewriters keep clacking in Chennai despite digital age

📅 2026-08-08 📂 Chennai Original source ↗
Key points
  • Typewriter repair shops in Chennai continue to operate despite digital dominance
  • Loyal customers, including courts and government offices, sustain the demand
  • Repair technicians note a niche but steady market for refurbished machines

In an age of instant messaging and cloud storage, the rhythmic clack of typewriter keys still echoes through a few narrow lanes of Chennai. While most of the world has moved to sleek laptops and smartphones, a handful of repair shops and enthusiasts are keeping the mechanical workhorses alive.

The machines, once ubiquitous in every office and newsroom, now occupy a niche but stubborn presence. From legal documents to wedding invitations, the demand has not vanished—it has simply thinned to a dedicated clientele.

A craft that refuses to die

Walk into any of the city's old typewriter repair shops, and you will find shelves stacked with dusty machines, ribbons in faded colours, and tools worn smooth by decades of use. The technicians here are mostly elderly, having learnt the trade in their youth when typewriters were the backbone of official communication.

They speak of a time when waiting lists stretched for months, and every government office had a row of clattering machines. Today, the footfall is sparse, but it is steady. Many customers arrive with machines inherited from parents or grandparents, seeking repairs that modern technicians cannot offer.

Who still uses typewriters?

The surviving users are a mixed lot. Courts and government departments, bound by procedural rules, still rely on typewriters for certain forms and affidavits. Some lawyers prefer typed documents over printed ones, citing authenticity and tradition.

Small businesses and individual users also keep the trade alive. Wedding card designers, for instance, use typewriters to add a vintage touch to invitations. Students of design and history occasionally seek them out for projects. The repair shop owners note that while the volume has dropped, the loyalty of these customers remains strong.

The economics of a dying trade

Repairing a typewriter is not cheap. A full service can cost anywhere from a few hundred to a couple of thousand rupees, depending on the machine's condition. Spare parts are increasingly hard to find, forcing technicians to cannibalise old machines for components.

Yet, the trade survives. Some shops have adapted by selling refurbished typewriters online, tapping into a global market of collectors and nostalgia buffs. Others offer maintenance contracts to the few institutions that still use them regularly.

The technicians themselves are aware of their precarious position. There are few young apprentices willing to learn the craft. The knowledge is passing with the older generation, and the shops that remain are often run by people who have spent their entire lives in this profession.

A symbol of endurance

For the regulars, a typewriter is more than a tool. It is a symbol of patience and precision, a counterpoint to the fleeting nature of digital content. The act of typing on a physical machine forces a certain deliberation—each key press is deliberate, each word carries weight.

Users often speak of the sensory experience: the resistance of the keys, the bell that signals the end of a line, the smell of ink and oil. These are things a keyboard cannot replicate.

In Chennai, the typewriter's survival is not a rebellion against technology but a quiet insistence on continuity. As long as there are people who value the craft, the clacking will continue.

What remains to be seen is whether this niche will attract new practitioners or fade with the current generation of technicians. For now, the machines keep working, one key at a time.
