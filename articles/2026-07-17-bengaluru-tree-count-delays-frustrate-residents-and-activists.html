Bengaluru tree count delays frustrate residents and activists

📅 2026-07-17 📂 Bengaluru Original source ↗
Key points
  • Bengaluru's tree census, meant to map and protect urban trees, is years behind schedule.
  • Residents and activists blame bureaucratic delays and lack of coordination between agencies.
  • The delay has hampered conservation efforts and angered citizens who fear loss of green cover.

Bengaluru's ambitious tree census, a project meant to count and protect the city's green cover, is moving at a glacial pace. What was conceived as a quick, scientific mapping of every tree in the city has become a bureaucratic quagmire that is frustrating residents and environmental activists alike.

A project stuck in paperwork

The census, launched in 2022 by the Bruhat Bengaluru Mahanagara Palike (BBMP) in collaboration with the Indian Institute of Science, aimed to count every tree in the city. The goal was to create a digital database to monitor tree health, prevent illegal felling, and plan new plantations.

But three years later, only a fraction of the city's estimated 1.5 crore trees have been counted. Officials cite delays in procurement of equipment, shortage of trained personnel, and lack of coordination between the BBMP and the forest department.

"The counting is taking longer than the trees themselves take to grow," said a BBMP official who did not want to be named. "We are stuck in a cycle of tenders and approvals."

Residents and activists lose patience

For residents of neighbourhoods like Jayanagar and Indiranagar, the delay has real consequences. Without an accurate tree count, they say, the BBMP cannot effectively prevent the felling of trees for road widening or construction projects.

"We have seen over 200 trees cut in our locality in the last two years," said Meena Rajan, a resident of Koramangala. "The census was supposed to stop this. But nothing has changed."

Environmental groups have also voiced frustration. "The government keeps talking about increasing green cover, but they can't even count what they have," said a member of the Bengaluru Environment Trust. "This is basic governance."

What is causing the holdup?

BBMP officials admit the project has faced multiple hurdles. The initial plan to use GPS-enabled tablets for data collection was delayed due to a failed tender. Then, the training of field staff took longer than expected.

There is also a lack of clarity on which trees are being counted. The census was supposed to include all trees on public land—parks, roadsides, and government properties. But trees on private land, which make up a significant portion of the city's green cover, were left out. This has led to criticism that the data will be incomplete.

"If you don't count private trees, you are missing half the picture," said urban ecologist Dr. S. R. Nair. "This census will be outdated the day it is finished."

A race against time

Climate change and rapid urbanisation have made the need for accurate data even more urgent. Bengaluru's green cover has shrunk from over 70% in the 1970s to less than 15% today, according to some estimates.

The BBMP now says it hopes to complete the census by the end of 2026. But activists are sceptical. "Every year they give a new deadline. We have heard this before," said Rajan.

What happens next depends on whether the BBMP can break the bureaucratic logjam. If it fails, the city's trees—and its residents—will pay the price. For now, the counting continues, slower than the trees grow.
