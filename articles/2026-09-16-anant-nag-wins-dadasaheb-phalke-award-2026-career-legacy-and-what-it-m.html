Anant Nag to Receive Dadasaheb Phalke Award on September 22, 2026

The Indian film fraternity is set to honour one of its most versatile actors, Anant Nag, with the Dadasaheb Phalke Award, the country's highest cinematic honour. The ceremony will take place on September 22, 2026, in New Delhi, where President Droupadi Murmu will present the award. Prime Minister Narendra Modi congratulated Nag on social media, praising his contributions to Indian cinema and noting the actor's enduring appeal across generations.

Nag, who turns 78 this year, has been a fixture in Kannada, Hindi, and English films since the early 1970s. His selection was announced by the Ministry of Information and Broadcasting, following a recommendation by a jury chaired by filmmaker Shekhar Kapur. The award recognises his exceptional body of work, which spans over 300 films and includes critically acclaimed performances in Ankur, Godhi Banna Sadharana Mykattu, and the iconic television series Malgudi Days.

Why the Dadasaheb Phalke Award Matters

Instituted in 1969, the Dadasaheb Phalke Award is India's most prestigious film honour, named after the father of Indian cinema, Dhundiraj Govind Phalke. It is presented annually at the National Film Awards ceremony to a film personality for their outstanding contribution to the growth and development of Indian cinema. The award carries a Swarna Kamal (Golden Lotus) medal, a shawl, and a cash prize of ₹10 lakh—a modest sum compared to commercial film fees, but the prestige is immeasurable.

For buyers and industry watchers, the award often boosts the commercial value of an actor's past filmography. Streaming platforms and television networks frequently acquire or re-run classic films of the awardee, capitalising on renewed public interest. In Nag's case, his extensive catalogue—from art-house gems to blockbuster Kannada comedies—offers a rich library for OTT platforms seeking premium regional content.

Anant Nag's Five-Decade Journey: Key Milestones

Born in 1948 in Shimoga, Karnataka, Anant Nag began his career in theatre before making his film debut in 1973 with Sankalpa. His breakthrough came with Shyam Benegal's Ankur (1974), a landmark in parallel cinema. He went on to balance mainstream and art films with remarkable ease, becoming a household name in Karnataka.

From Parallel Cinema to Mainstream Stardom

Nag's versatility is his hallmark. In the 1970s and 80s, he was a fixture in Benegal's socially relevant films, including Nishant and Manthan. Simultaneously, he ruled the Kannada box office with commercial hits like Mungarina Minchu and Beladingala Baale. His portrayal of the affable, often sardonic common man resonated with audiences across India, especially through Malgudi Days, based on R.K. Narayan's stories.

For collectors and enthusiasts, physical media of Nag's films—especially rare DVDs of his parallel cinema work—often see price spikes following major awards. A quick scan of online marketplaces shows that a limited-edition DVD of Ankur can command upwards of ₹2,500, while out-of-print Kannada VCDs may fetch even higher sums among diaspora collectors.

PM Modi's Congratulations and the Public Participation Angle

Prime Minister Modi's congratulatory message on X (formerly Twitter) highlighted Nag's "timeless performances" and noted that the actor's journey reflects the richness of Indian regional cinema. In a statement, Nag expressed gratitude, saying the recognition validates the power of storytelling that transcends language barriers. He also remarked that PM Modi has changed the award selection system by encouraging public participation—a reference to recent government initiatives inviting citizen input for national awards.

This shift toward transparency and public engagement could influence how future awardees are chosen, potentially making the process more democratic but also more susceptible to popularity contests. Industry veterans argue that while public participation is welcome, the Dadasaheb Phalke Award must remain a peer-driven honour to preserve its artistic integrity.

    • Elevates the profile of Kannada cinema on the national stage.

  • Cons:

    • Public participation in award selection risks turning artistic merit into a popularity metric.
    • Limited theatrical re-release opportunities for older films due to poor print quality.

Buyer's Guide: What to Watch and Collect

If you're new to Anant Nag's work, start with Ankur (1974) for its raw, neo-realist power. For Kannada cinema enthusiasts, Godhi Banna Sadharana Mykattu (2016) showcases his late-career brilliance. Malgudi Days remains a nostalgic favourite and is available on select OTT platforms. Collectors should look for the 2003 DVD box set of Malgudi Days, which includes a bonus interview with Nag—prices for sealed copies have risen by 40% over the past year.

As the September 22 ceremony approaches, expect a surge in digital rentals and merchandise. For those looking to invest in physical media, authenticated copies of Nag's parallel cinema films are the safest bet, with steady appreciation among niche buyers. Avoid bootleg compilations; they lack restoration quality and hold no resale value.

The Dadasaheb Phalke Award to Anant Nag is more than a lifetime achievement honour—it is a reminder that versatility and longevity are the true markers of a cinematic legend. As the industry celebrates, viewers have a rare chance to rediscover a body of work that defines the best of Indian storytelling.
