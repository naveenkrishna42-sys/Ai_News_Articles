Sukumara Kurup Case: Why a 42-Year-Old Fugitive Saga Still Haunts Kerala

  • The case exposes gaps in tracking fugitives across borders

The Brunei False Alarm

For a few tense days, Kerala thought it had finally cracked one of its most baffling cold cases. A photograph circulating online appeared to show Sukumara Kurup—a man who has eluded capture since 1984—living in Brunei. The state’s Crime Branch swung into action. Within 72 hours, the excitement evaporated. Police confirmed the man in the photo was Damodara Menon, an unrelated individual with a passing resemblance to the fugitive. The case, once again, went cold.

This latest twist reveals something troubling: four decades of investigative advances, international cooperation mechanisms, and modern surveillance have failed to locate a man whose face was once printed on lakhs of posters across Kerala.

What Made This Case Infamous

Sukumara Kurup’s crime was calculated and chilling. He allegedly murdered his own nephew Chithrasena, burned the body, and presented the charred remains as Chithrasena’s to claim insurance money. When the deception unraveled, Kurup vanished. He was charged with murder, cheating, and criminal conspiracy. Courts have tried him in absentia multiple times, convicting him in at least one case.

The case became a cultural phenomenon in Kerala. It spawned folklore, jokes, and endless speculation about Kurup’s whereabouts. Every few years, a "sighting" surfaces—usually in the Gulf countries where many Keralites work. None have panned out.

Legal Vacuum on Presumed Death

One critical question emerges: why hasn’t Kurup been declared legally dead? Under Indian law, particularly the Indian Evidence Act, a person can be presumed dead if missing for seven years. Yet no such petition appears to have been filed by Chithrasena’s family. Legal experts suggest this hesitation stems from practical concerns.

"Declaring someone legally dead isn’t just paperwork," explains a senior advocate familiar with the case. "It triggers inheritance rights, closes insurance claims, and requires family consensus. In cases involving foul play, families often resist because they want the perpetrator to remain legally alive—and therefore liable for prosecution."

This creates an absurd situation. Kurup exists in legal limbo—neither dead enough for civil closure nor captured enough for criminal justice.

Why International Fugitive Tracking Fails

The Brunei episode exposes systemic weaknesses. India lacks bilateral extradition treaties with several Gulf nations. Even when treaties exist, bureaucratic delays stretch for years. Interpol notices help, but without local law enforcement cooperation, physical apprehension remains difficult.

The Kerala police have handled this case reasonably well by domestic standards—they followed up leads promptly and issued quick clarifications. But reactive investigation alone cannot solve a four-decade-old vanishing act.

The Bigger Picture

Cases like Kurup’s expose how India’s criminal justice system struggles with modern fugitive challenges. The law assumes the state will eventually catch offenders. When it doesn’t, the system offers no graceful exit for victims’ families or society.

Without reforms—faster extradition processes, better inter-agency coordination, and clearer presumed-death provisions—Kerala will likely see more false alarms. Kurup may already be dead himself, living quietly under an assumed name somewhere untraceable. Or he may be laughing at four decades of failed manhunts.

Until someone finds definitive proof either way, this case remains a mirror held up to law enforcement limitations—and a wound that refuses to heal for Chithrasena’s family.

  • What worked: Police responded quickly to the Brunei lead and prevented misinformation from spreading
  • What failed: No coordinated international mechanism to track Gulf-based Indian fugitives
  • Legal gap: No presumed death petition filed, keeping Kurup in legal limbo
  • Public impact: The case erodes faith in the justice system’s ability to deliver closure
