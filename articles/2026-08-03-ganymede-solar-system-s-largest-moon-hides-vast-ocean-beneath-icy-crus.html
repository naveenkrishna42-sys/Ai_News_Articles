Ganymede: Solar System's Largest Moon Hides Vast Ocean Beneath Icy Crust

📅 2026-08-03 📂 Science Original source ↗
Key points
  • Ganymede is the largest moon in the Solar System, larger than the planet Mercury.
  • Beneath its thick icy crust, scientists believe a salty ocean exists, possibly holding more water than all of Earth's oceans.
  • The moon's unique magnetic field and icy surface make it a prime target for future exploration.

A Giant Among Moons

When it comes to moons, Ganymede stands alone. Orbiting Jupiter, this icy world is the largest moon in the Solar System — bigger than the planet Mercury and even larger than the dwarf planet Pluto. Its diameter stretches about 5,268 kilometres, making it a true giant in the cosmic neighbourhood.

But size isn't the only thing that makes Ganymede remarkable. Beneath its frozen, cratered exterior, scientists believe a vast ocean of liquid water sloshes silently. This hidden sea, tucked miles below the surface, has turned Ganymede into one of the most tantalising targets in the search for habitable worlds beyond Earth.

The Hidden Ocean

The idea of an underground ocean on Ganymede isn't new, but recent observations have strengthened the case. Spacecraft data, including from NASA's Galileo mission and the Hubble Space Telescope, have hinted at a salty ocean sandwiched between layers of ice. The water may hold more volume than all of Earth's oceans combined.

What keeps this ocean liquid? Scientists point to tidal heating — the gravitational pull from Jupiter and its other moons flexes Ganymede's interior, generating warmth. That heat, combined with natural radioactive decay in the moon's rocky core, could maintain a stable subsurface ocean despite the frigid temperatures at the surface.

An Icy World With a Magnetic Surprise

Ganymede is also the only moon in the Solar System known to generate its own magnetic field. This magnetic bubble, embedded within Jupiter's gigantic magnetosphere, creates dazzling auroras at the moon's poles. The way these auroras wobble has given astronomers indirect clues about the ocean below — the magnetic field interacts with the salty water, causing subtle shifts.

The surface itself is a patchwork of ancient dark regions and lighter, grooved terrains, suggesting a history of tectonic activity. Craters and ridges tell a story of impacts and ice flows, while possible salt deposits hint at briny water that may have once reached the surface.

Why Ganymede Matters

For planetary scientists, Ganymede is more than just a big rock in space. It represents a class of ocean worlds — moons with liquid water beneath icy shells — that could potentially host microbial life. Europa, another Jovian moon, often steals the spotlight, but Ganymede's larger size and unique magnetic field make it equally compelling.

The European Space Agency's JUICE (Jupiter Icy Moons Explorer) mission, launched in 2023, is currently on its way to the Jupiter system. It is expected to arrive in the early 2030s and will conduct detailed flybys of Ganymede, eventually entering orbit around the moon. The spacecraft will map the surface, probe the ice shell, and search for evidence of the subsurface ocean.

What Lies Ahead

Scientists are also keen to understand whether Ganymede's ocean ever interacts with the rocky mantle below. Such interactions could create chemical gradients — potential energy sources for life. Future missions, possibly including landers or ice-penetrating probes, could one day sample the ocean directly.

For now, Ganymede remains a world of questions. But with JUICE closing in and new telescopes peering deeper into space, answers may not be far off.

The coming decade will be crucial for unlocking the secrets of this icy giant. If Ganymede's ocean is confirmed to be warm and chemically rich, it could reshape our understanding of where life might exist in the Solar System.
