India Braces for 100% US Tariff Threat Over Russian Oil Purchases

✍️ By TIVRA News Editorial Team 📅 2026-09-17 📂 World Original source ↗
Key points
  • The US House has advanced a sanctions bill that could slap tariffs of up to 100% on imports from countries buying Russian crude, directly targeting India and China.
  • India's MEA says it is monitoring developments and will take all necessary measures to protect trade and economic interests.
  • Indian refiners have leaned on discounted Russian crude to cushion import bills, so any tariff shock would transmit to fuel prices, exports and the rupee.

What the US House Vote Actually Changes

The US House of Representatives has advanced a Russia sanctions bill that empowers Washington to impose tariffs of up to 100% on goods from countries that continue buying Russian crude. India and China are the two economies most directly in the crosshairs, given their share of discounted Russian barrel purchases since 2022.

The measure is not an automatic levy. It creates a legal trigger the US President can use against trading partners, which means the real story is diplomatic leverage rather than an immediate customs order. New Delhi has responded cautiously, with the Ministry of External Affairs stating it is monitoring developments and will protect India's trade and economic interests.

Why India Is the Immediate Target

Russian crude has become a structural pillar of India's import basket, sourced largely by refiners in Gujarat and on the west coast. The discount to Brent has narrowed over time but remains material enough to justify continued purchases despite Western pressure.

US senators have framed the bill as a "clean up your act" warning to both India and China. For Indian buyers, the practical question is whether the tariff is a negotiating threat or a policy that actually lands on Indian exports.

What It Means for Indian Exporters and Consumers

A 100% tariff would effectively price many Indian goods out of the US market overnight. Sectors with thin margins, such as textiles and gems and jewellery, would absorb the sharpest hit because they cannot easily pass on a doubling of landed cost.

Pharmaceutical and IT services exporters face a different problem. Drugs and software are harder to substitute, but US buyers would still demand renegotiated contracts or shift sourcing over time. Any sustained disruption would pressure the rupee and complicate the RBI's inflation management.

Fuel Prices and the Refining Chain

If Indian refiners are forced to cut Russian crude, they must replace it with Brent-linked barrels from West Asia, Africa or the Americas. That raises the import bill, and some of it flows into petrol, diesel and jet fuel pricing. The cushion Indian consumers have enjoyed from discounted crude would erode.

Pros and Cons of India's Current Strategy

  • Pro: Discounted Russian crude has saved India tens of thousands of crores in foreign exchange since 2022.
  • Pro: India retains strategic autonomy by buying from multiple suppliers rather than aligning with one bloc.
  • Pro: New Delhi has kept channels open with both Washington and Moscow, avoiding a hard rupture.
  • Con: Exposure to secondary sanctions and tariff threats raises risk premiums for Indian exporters.
  • Con: A 100% tariff would hit labour-intensive MSME clusters hardest, risking jobs.
  • Con: Prolonged uncertainty discourages long-term US buyers from signing fresh Indian contracts.

Buyer Guide: How to Read This Headline

For Indian businesses with US exposure, the sensible move is scenario planning, not panic. Review contract clauses on tariff pass-through, hedge currency where possible and diversify buyer geographies into the EU, Gulf and Southeast Asia.

For consumers, the near-term impact depends on whether the bill becomes an operational tariff or remains a bargaining chip. Watch three signals: the US Senate's next step, the President's stance on invoking the measure, and whether Indian refiners quietly taper Russian volumes.

India's response so far signals negotiation rather than confrontation. The bill's passage through the House is a pressure point, not a verdict, and the coming weeks of diplomacy will determine whether Indian exporters face a tariff wall or a negotiating table.
