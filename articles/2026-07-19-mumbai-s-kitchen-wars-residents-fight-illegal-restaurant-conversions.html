Mumbai's kitchen wars: Residents fight illegal restaurant conversions

📅 2026-07-19 📂 Mumbai Original source ↗
Key points
  • Illegal restaurant and cloud kitchen conversions in Mumbai residential buildings are sparking resident protests.
  • Residents cite safety hazards, noise pollution, and traffic congestion as major concerns.
  • Local authorities have issued notices but enforcement remains a challenge.

From homes to commercial kitchens

Across Mumbai, a quiet war is being fought in residential buildings. Residents are locking horns with neighbours who have turned their flats into commercial kitchens for restaurants and cloud kitchens. The issue has escalated in areas like Bandra, Andheri, and Powai, where narrow staircases and limited parking are poorly suited for heavy footfall and delivery traffic.

Residents complain of constant noise, strong odours, and fire risks. Many buildings lack the infrastructure to support gas cylinders, exhaust systems, or waste disposal on a commercial scale. Yet, the conversions continue.

Safety and nuisance concerns

Fire safety is a primary worry. In many cases, kitchens operate without proper ventilation or extinguishers. Residents allege that building society approvals are bypassed, and kitchens run late into the night, disturbing peace. One resident in a Bandra cooperative housing society told Mumbai Mirror: “We don’t know when a cylinder might leak. It’s a ticking bomb.”

Traffic congestion from delivery bikes is another flashpoint. Locals say two-wheelers clog entry gates and pavements, making it hard for elderly residents to walk. Garbage from packaging often piles up, attracting pests.

Regulatory gaps

Mumbai’s municipal corporation has issued notices to several properties, but enforcement is patchy. Many kitchens operate without trade licences or fire NOCs. The Brihanmumbai Municipal Corporation (BMC) has said it is conducting inspections, but residents claim complaints often go unanswered for weeks.

Real estate lawyers point out that converting a residential flat into a commercial kitchen violates the Maharashtra Ownership Flats Act. However, proving misuse in court is slow, and many operators simply shift to another flat when caught.

Residents fight back

In some buildings, residents have formed committees to monitor and report illegal kitchens. They film delivery activity and document noise levels. Some have approached local police and the BMC’s nuisance complaint cell. But the battle is uphill.

One Powai resident said: “Every week a new kitchen opens. We can’t sleep before 1 am because of the noise. The society executive committee is divided, so nothing gets done.”

Civil society groups have called for stricter zoning rules and regular inspections. They want the BMC to de-register properties found violating norms and impose heavy fines.

Cloud kitchen boom adds pressure

The rise of cloud kitchens—delivery-only restaurants operating from residential addresses—has worsened the problem. These kitchens require no storefront and can start with minimal investment. Operators often lease multiple flats in the same building, turning entire floors into commercial cooking zones.

Industry estimates suggest Mumbai has over 5,000 cloud kitchens, many unregistered. The state government has proposed a policy to regulate them, but it is yet to be enforced.

What happens next

With residential anger growing, the BMC is under pressure to act decisively. A new task force to inspect and seal illegal commercial kitchens is reportedly being considered. Residents hope for faster action, but until regulations catch up with the rapid growth of food delivery, the war on Mumbai’s kitchens is far from over.
