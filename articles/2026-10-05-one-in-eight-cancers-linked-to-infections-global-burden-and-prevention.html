One in Eight Cancers Linked to Infections: Global Burden and Prevention

Key points

  • Vaccination, screening and antimicrobial treatment provide proven, cost‑effective avenues to prevent a substantial share of these cancers, especially when deployed equitably across income levels.

Understanding the Global Burden of Infection‑Related Cancers

According to a 2026 analysis published in the European Medical Journal, roughly one in eight cancer diagnoses worldwide can be traced back to an infectious agent. This proportion translates to about 12.5 % of all new cancer cases each year, highlighting a preventable slice of the global oncology burden. The estimate draws on data from the International Agency for Research on Cancer (IARC) and the World Health Organization (WHO), which track cancer incidence and attributable risk factors across regions.

Key Infectious Agents and Their Associated Cancers

Several pathogens have been classified as carcinogenic to humans (Group 1) by IARC. The most prevalent contributors are human papillomavirus (HPV), Helicobacter pylori, hepatitis B and C viruses (HBV/HCV), and Epstein‑Barr virus (EBV). Each agent is linked to specific cancer types, and together they account for the majority of infection‑related malignancies.

The percentages represent the share of all cancer cases globally that can be attributed to each pathogen, based on IARC’s 2020 global cancer burden assessment. When combined, these agents explain roughly half of the 12.5 % infection‑related fraction, with the remainder arising from other infectious factors such as HIV‑associated Kaposi sarcoma and liver flukes.

Prevention Strategies: Vaccination, Screening and Treatment

Vaccination offers the most direct route to curb infection‑driven cancers. HPV vaccines, administered to adolescents before exposure, have demonstrated up to 90 % efficacy against the viral strains responsible for the majority of cervical cancers. HBV vaccination, part of routine infant immunisation in many countries, reduces liver cancer incidence markedly.

Screening and early treatment further lower risk. HPV‑based cervical screening identifies precancerous lesions, while HBV/HCV antiviral therapy suppresses viral replication and cuts hepatocellular carcinoma risk. Eradication regimens for H. pylori, using proton‑pump inhibitors and antibiotics, have shown a 30‑40 % reduction in gastric cancer development in high‑risk populations.

Policy Implications and Future Directions

Translating scientific evidence into public health action requires sustained financing, equitable vaccine delivery, and strengthened laboratory capacity. Low‑ and middle‑income countries bear a disproportionate share of infection‑related cancers due to limited access to vaccination and screening programmes.

Experts recommend integrating cancer prevention into existing infectious disease control frameworks, such as expanding HPV vaccination alongside maternal health services and scaling up HBV birth‑dose vaccination in endemic regions. Continued surveillance, supported by global cancer registries, will be essential to monitor trends and assess the impact of interventions over the coming decade.
