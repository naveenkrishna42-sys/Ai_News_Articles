BRICS Trade Finance System: What It Means for Global Buyers

Why BRICS Is Building Its Own Trade Finance Rails

For three decades, cross-border trade settled through a narrow set of dollar-clearing channels dominated by SWIFT messaging and US correspondent banks. That architecture worked smoothly until sanctions turned it into a chokepoint. The freezing of Russian reserves in 2022 was the inflection point: every non-Western central bank realised that holding dollar assets carried political risk, not just currency risk. BRICS nations have since moved from rhetoric to plumbing, assembling payment, settlement, and credit infrastructure that runs parallel to the incumbent system.

The effort is deliberately quiet. There is no single launch date, no grand treaty. Instead, a patchwork of bilateral swap lines, local currency settlement agreements, and development bank credit is being stitched together. For Indian importers and exporters, this matters far more than summit communiqués suggest.

The Building Blocks of the New System

Local Currency Settlement and Swap Lines

India already settles a slice of its crude oil and defence imports with Russia in rupees and dirhams, routed through UAE intermediaries. Similar arrangements exist with the UAE for gold and with several African nations for pharma exports. These are not dollar replacements; they are dollar bypasses for specific corridors where both sides hold mutual trade surpluses.

The New Development Bank as Anchor

The NDB, headquartered in Shanghai, has approved loans denominated in local currencies rather than dollars. That shifts currency risk from borrowers to the bank and, by extension, to member states. For infrastructure buyers, this can mean cheaper effective borrowing costs, though the NDB's lending book remains small relative to the World Bank.

Payment Messaging Alternatives

Russia's SPFS and China's CIPS have expanded membership among BRICS and partner nations. These systems do not yet match SWIFT's reach or reliability, but they provide a fallback for sanctioned or sanction-exposed entities.

Buyer Implications: Cost, Speed, and Risk

The headline benefit for Indian businesses is cost. A mid-sized exporter invoicing in rupees to a Russian buyer avoids two dollar conversions, saving roughly 1–2% per transaction. On a ₹10 crore annual export book, that is ₹10–20 lakh directly to the bottom line. Settlement also speeds up, which improves working capital cycles.

The risks are equally concrete. Rupee balances accumulate in partner countries with limited avenues to spend them, creating the classic imbalance problem India faced with Russian oil. Currency convertibility is shallow for the ruble, real, and rand, so hedging costs can erase the savings. And compliance teams at Indian banks remain cautious, wary of secondary sanctions exposure.

  • Pro: Lower transaction costs, often 0.5–2.5% per trade.
  • Pro: Faster settlement, improving cash flow for SMEs.
  • Pro: Reduced exposure to dollar sanctions and US policy shifts.
  • Con: Currency imbalance and limited convertibility of partner currencies.
  • Con: Thin liquidity and weak hedging markets outside the RMB.
  • Con: Bank compliance caution slows adoption.

What to Watch Over the Next 24 Months

Commodity and energy corridors will lead adoption because volumes are large, buyers are state-linked, and price benchmarks can be renegotiated. Defence trade, already politically insulated, will follow. Consumer goods and services trade will remain on dollar rails for years because supplier bases are fragmented and dispute resolution frameworks are still dollar-centric.

Indian exporters should start by piloting rupee invoicing with one or two counterparties in Russia, the UAE, or Africa, measuring actual landed savings against hedging costs. Importers of crude, gold, and fertiliser should ask their banks directly whether CIPS or SPFS routing is available for their corridor. The system is not yet a replacement for the dollar order. It is, however, becoming a genuine option for specific trades, and buyers who understand its plumbing will negotiate better terms than those who ignore it.
