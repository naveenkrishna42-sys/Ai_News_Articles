SpaceX Falcon 9 Rocket Stage to Crash into Moon on August 5

📅 2026-08-01 📂 Science Original source ↗
Key points
  • An abandoned SpaceX Falcon 9 rocket stage is on course to hit the Moon on August 5.
  • The impact will occur at seven times the speed of sound, leaving a fresh crater.
  • Visibility of the crash depends on lunar lighting and the observer's location.
  • Scientists are uncertain about the exact effects of the collision.

A Wayward Rocket's Final Destination

An abandoned SpaceX Falcon 9 rocket stage, drifting in space for years, is set to crash into the Moon on August 5. The spent booster, which helped launch a satellite in 2015, has been tumbling in an uncontrolled orbit since then. Now, gravity has sealed its fate.

The impact will occur at roughly seven times the speed of sound, carving out a fresh crater on the lunar surface. Scientists, however, remain unsure what exactly to expect from the collision.

Who Will See the Crash?

Visibility depends on which side of the Moon faces Earth at the moment of impact. If the crash occurs on the near side, observers in parts of Asia, Europe, and Africa might catch a brief flash through telescopes. If it hits the far side, no one on Earth will see it directly.

Space agencies and amateur astronomers are tracking the rocket's trajectory closely. Officials have not yet confirmed the precise coordinates of the impact site, but they expect the crash to leave a crater several metres wide.

Why Scientists Are Watching

This is not a planned mission. The Falcon 9 stage was never designed to land on the Moon. Its uncontrolled descent offers a rare, albeit accidental, opportunity to study how a large object impacts the lunar surface.

Experts are particularly interested in the plume of dust and debris the crash will kick up. By analysing that material, they hope to learn more about the Moon's subsurface composition. The event could also help refine models for future lunar missions, including those aimed at mining resources.

What Happens Next

In the days leading up to August 5, tracking teams will refine their predictions. Amateur astronomers with decent telescopes may want to point them at the Moon around the expected time, though a clear view is not guaranteed.

After the impact, scientists will compare before-and-after images from lunar orbiters to confirm the crater's size and shape. The data could inform how space debris is managed in Earth's orbit as well.

The crash is a reminder that even discarded hardware can have scientific value. As more rockets are launched, such accidental impacts may become more frequent, and each one offers a chance to learn.
