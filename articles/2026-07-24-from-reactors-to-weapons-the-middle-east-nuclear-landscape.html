From reactors to weapons: The Middle East nuclear landscape

📅 2026-07-24 📂 Top Stories Original source ↗
Key points
  • Iran is the only Middle East nation with a domestic nuclear fuel cycle and near-weapons capability.
  • Israel has an undeclared nuclear arsenal, built with French and US help in secret.
  • Saudi Arabia seeks a US civilian nuclear deal, but Trump links it to normalisation with Israel.
  • UAE and Egypt operate civilian reactors under strict international safeguards.
  • Trump's proposed Saudi deal has sparked fears of a regional arms race in West Asia.

Who has nuclear power in the Middle East?

Several nations in the Middle East operate nuclear programmes, but their origins and ambitions vary widely. Iran stands out as the only country in the region that has built a complete domestic nuclear fuel cycle, from uranium mining to enrichment.

Israel is widely believed to possess the region's only nuclear arsenal, though it has never officially confirmed it. Saudi Arabia, the United Arab Emirates and Egypt have civilian nuclear energy projects, while Turkey is constructing its first power plant.

Iran: The domestic enrichment path

Iran's nuclear programme began in the 1950s under the US-backed Atoms for Peace initiative. It later expanded covertly, with key facilities at Natanz and Fordow built without international oversight.

Tehran now enriches uranium to up to 60% purity, close to weapons-grade. The 2015 JCPOA deal capped its enrichment at 3.67%, but the US withdrawal in 2018 pushed Iran to accelerate its work. The IAEA has confirmed Iran is the only non-weapons state with a full enrichment cycle.

Israel: The undeclared arsenal

Israel built its nuclear capability in secret during the 1960s with help from France, which supplied a reactor, and the US, which turned a blind eye. The Dimona reactor in the Negev desert is believed to have produced enough plutonium for an estimated 90 warheads.

Israel maintains a policy of 'nuclear ambiguity' — neither confirming nor denying its arsenal. This has allowed it to avoid international sanctions while deterring regional adversaries. The US has never officially pressured Israel to sign the NPT.

Saudi Arabia: The sticking point with Trump

Saudi Arabia has long sought a civilian nuclear programme, but its plans hit a roadblock under the Trump administration. In July 2026, Trump announced a landmark nuclear deal with Riyadh, but linked it to the kingdom joining the Abraham Accords — normalising ties with Israel.

Trump said, 'Saudis must recognise Israel for nuclear deal.' The proposal has alarmed Israelis, who fear it could ignite a West Asia arms race. Saudi officials have not confirmed whether they will accept the condition, but the deal would allow the kingdom to enrich uranium domestically — a step that could eventually enable weapons production.

UAE, Egypt and Turkey: The civilian route

The UAE took a different approach. It built the Barakah nuclear plant with South Korean help and agreed to forgo domestic enrichment and reprocessing — the gold standard for non-proliferation. The plant began operations in 2020.

Egypt has an older Soviet-built research reactor and is now constructing its first commercial plant at El Dabaa with Russian assistance. Turkey is building the Akkuyu plant, also with Russia, and has hinted at future enrichment ambitions. Both countries are NPT signatories with full IAEA safeguards.

What to watch: Trump's Saudi deal faces opposition from both Israeli security hawks and US non-proliferation advocates. If Riyadh accepts the enrichment clause, it could trigger a cascade of similar demands from other Arab states. The IAEA and Congress will scrutinise every clause.
