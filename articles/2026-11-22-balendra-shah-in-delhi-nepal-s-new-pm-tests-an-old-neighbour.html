Balendra Shah in Delhi: Nepal's New PM Tests an Old Neighbour

✍️ By TIVRA News Editorial Desk 📅 2026-11-22 📂 Top Stories Original source ↗
Key points
  • Air entry points and power purchase deals are the two deliverables Nepal can actually bank, and both are stuck on Indian operational clearances rather than principle.
  • The 1950 Treaty of Peace and Friendship remains the structural irritant; the EPG report submitted in 2018 has still not been made public by either side.
  • Kalapani, Lipulekh and Susta are politically non-negotiable for any Nepali prime minister, so expect a framework for talks rather than a settlement.

Balendra Shah lands in New Delhi on a two-day visit carrying an unusual combination of assets: a large urban mandate, a small country's leverage, and a neighbour that has outlasted every Nepali government since 1950. The schedule will look ceremonial. The files are not.

Why This Visit Matters More Than the Optics

Shah's political capital comes from outside the traditional Kathmandu elite. A structural engineer and rapper who won the capital's mayoralty in 2022 as an independent, he built his reputation on delivery audits and public confrontation with contractors, not on foreign policy apprenticeship.

That profile cuts both ways in Delhi. Indian officials deal in predictable, slow-moving institutional relationships; Shah's mandate is impatient and impatient governments in Kathmandu have historically found New Delhi's file system harder to move than its rhetoric.

The visit is therefore a test of whether a genuinely new style of Nepali politics can extract operational concessions rather than another joint commission communique.

The Four Files That Define Shah's India Test

Air Routes: The Cheapest Win on the Table

Nepal has pushed for years to operationalise additional air entry points beyond the corridors in regular use, arguing that longer routings add fuel burn, crew hours and ticket prices on flights to Delhi, Mumbai and the Gulf. India has accepted the principle; airlines still fly the old tracks.

This is the lowest-cost concession available to Delhi and the most visible to Nepali passengers, who pay a premium on almost every international sector.

Power: Where Nepal Finally Has Leverage

Nepal now exports surplus monsoon hydropower to India and imports in the dry season. The 2024 long-term agreement for India to import up to 10,000 MW over a decade gave Kathmandu a commercial relationship it did not have a generation ago.

The unfinished work is transmission capacity and payment certainty. Without dedicated cross-border lines, generation that exists on paper cannot be scheduled, and developers cannot close financing.

The 1950 Treaty: A Report Nobody Has Published

The Treaty of Peace and Friendship underpins the open border, reciprocal citizen rights and the security relationship. The Eminent Persons' Group submitted its recommendations on revision in 2018. Neither capital has made the text public.

Shah cannot reopen the treaty in one visit. He can insist on a published timetable, which would itself be a departure from three decades of managed ambiguity.

The Border: Kalapani, Lipulekh, Susta

These are not negotiable for any Nepali prime minister, and no Indian government has shown appetite for third-party mediation. Expect a mechanism for technical-level boundary work, not a settlement.

What This Means in Practice

For Indian travellers, the measurable outcome is airfare and route time. Every additional entry point shaves fuel cost off Kathmandu–Delhi and Kathmandu–Gulf sectors, which is where Nepali carriers compete hardest.
