RBI’s Rupee View: Why the Central Bank Sees the Currency as ‘Undervalued’

📅 2026-07-29 📂 Business Original source ↗
Key points
  • The RBI has indicated the rupee is undervalued, likely to manage export competitiveness.
  • The central bank's intervention in forex markets aims to prevent excessive volatility.
  • An undervalued rupee can boost exports but makes imports more expensive.
  • The RBI's stance is influenced by India's trade deficit and foreign exchange reserves.

The Reserve Bank of India (RBI) has been sending a clear signal through its market operations: it believes the rupee is undervalued. This position, while not explicitly stated in every policy document, is evident in the central bank's persistent intervention in the foreign exchange market.

For months, the RBI has been buying dollars, building up India's foreign exchange reserves. This action prevents the rupee from appreciating too quickly. The logic is straightforward: a cheaper rupee makes Indian exports more competitive globally.

What 'Undervalued' Means in Practice

An undervalued currency is one whose exchange rate is lower than what market forces alone would determine. The RBI's actions suggest it believes the rupee's 'fair value' is higher than its current trading level.

By absorbing dollars, the central bank effectively keeps the rupee weaker than it might otherwise be. This is a deliberate strategy to support domestic manufacturing and export-oriented sectors, such as textiles, IT services, and pharmaceuticals.

The Trade-Off: Exports vs. Imports

The benefits of an undervalued rupee come with clear costs. Indian importers, especially those buying crude oil, edible oils, and machinery, pay more. This can fuel inflation, as the cost of imported inputs rises.

For Indian students studying abroad or families planning foreign holidays, a weaker rupee means higher expenses. The RBI's stance, therefore, is a balancing act between supporting exporters and controlling imported inflation.

Impact on the Trade Deficit

India runs a persistent trade deficit—it imports more than it exports. An undervalued rupee can help narrow this gap by making exports cheaper and imports costlier. However, the immediate effect is often a higher import bill, particularly for oil.

By maintaining a large forex reserve buffer, the RBI can also manage sudden capital outflows. This provides a cushion against global shocks, such as a sudden rise in US interest rates or geopolitical turmoil.

The RBI’s Dual Mandate

The central bank’s primary job is to control inflation and maintain financial stability. Its exchange rate policy is a tool to serve these ends. An undervalued rupee, if managed carefully, can support growth without triggering runaway inflation.

Analysts note that the RBI rarely comments directly on the rupee’s 'fair value.' Instead, it lets its actions speak. The consistent pattern of dollar buying indicates a clear preference for a competitive exchange rate.

This approach is not unique to India. Many emerging-market central banks, particularly in Asia, have historically managed their currencies to support export-led growth. The RBI’s strategy fits this broader pattern.

The central bank's stance also reflects a pragmatic view of global capital flows. With foreign investors often moving money in and out of Indian markets, a managed rupee reduces volatility. It gives businesses greater certainty for planning.

Looking ahead, the RBI’s policy will depend on global cues. If the US dollar weakens or foreign inflows surge, the central bank may allow the rupee to appreciate gradually. For now, the message is clear: the RBI sees the rupee as undervalued and is acting on that belief.
