Every disease is a policy failure: Why public health is a governance issue

📅 2026-08-11 📂 Health Original source ↗
Key points
  • The essay argues that preventable diseases are largely the result of policy decisions, not just biological factors.
  • It calls for a shift from treating symptoms to addressing root causes like housing, pollution, and food systems.
  • Experts say this framing could reshape how governments allocate health budgets and design urban infrastructure.

When a person falls ill, the immediate instinct is to look for a pathogen, a genetic flaw, or an unlucky roll of the biological dice. But a provocative new essay from Works in Progress Magazine flips that assumption on its head. It argues that virtually every disease, from diabetes to tuberculosis, is at its core a policy failure, a predictable outcome of decisions made in government chambers, not in hospital wards.

The piece, published this week, contends that the most powerful determinants of health are not stethoscopes or surgical instruments but housing codes, traffic laws, agricultural subsidies, and tax structures. The framing is deliberately blunt, and it is already stirring debate among public health experts and policymakers in India and beyond.

The case for systemic causes

The essay advances a simple, uncomfortable thesis: when a society builds cities without safe walking paths, it engineers obesity. When it prices nutritious food beyond the reach of poor families, it manufactures malnutrition. When it allows factories to spew particulate matter into the air, it writes a prescription for chronic lung disease.

None of these outcomes are accidents. They are the accumulated results of choices, or the lack of them, made by people with the authority to allocate resources and set rules. The authors argue that medical professionals are effectively mopping the floor while the tap is still running, treating individual patients while ignoring the structural currents that made them sick in the first place.

This is not a new idea. The social determinants of health have been studied for decades. But the essay sharpens the argument by removing the comfortable cushion of inevitability. It insists that diseases are not natural disasters visited upon passive populations. They are governed, budgeted, and legislated into existence.

Why the framing matters now

The timing is significant. India is wrestling with a double burden of disease. Infectious illnesses persist in many regions, while non-communicable diseases like hypertension, diabetes, and cardiovascular conditions are rising sharply in urban and semi-urban areas. The country's health spending, though increasing, still leans heavily toward curative care rather than prevention.

If the essay's thesis gains traction, it could shift the conversation from how many hospital beds a district needs to how its streets, markets, and schools are designed. It would place the onus on ministries of urban development, food processing, and environment, not just the Ministry of Health and Family Welfare.

Officials have not yet responded to the essay's arguments, and no policy changes have been announced. But the piece has already generated significant discussion in public health circles, with some calling it a much-needed corrective to a medical system that profits from treating illness rather than preventing it.

Critics and caveats

Not everyone is convinced. Some researchers caution that the policy-failure framing risks oversimplifying complex biological realities. Genetic predispositions, pathogens that evolve faster than regulations, and individual behaviours that defy rational planning all play a role. A person can make every sensible choice and still fall ill.

Others worry that the argument could be weaponised politically, used to blame governments for diseases that are partly shaped by forces beyond their control, such as climate patterns or global supply chains. The essay anticipates this pushback, acknowledging that some diseases are more amenable to policy intervention than others.

Still, the core challenge stands. If diseases are policy failures, then they can also be policy successes. The tools to prevent illness already exist. What is lacking, the essay argues, is the collective will to use them.

What to watch next

The debate is unlikely to remain confined to academic journals. As India prepares its next round of health budget allocations, the question of where the money flows, into hospitals or into cleaner streets, will become increasingly political. Whether the essay changes any budget lines remains to be seen. But it has already changed the terms of the argument.
