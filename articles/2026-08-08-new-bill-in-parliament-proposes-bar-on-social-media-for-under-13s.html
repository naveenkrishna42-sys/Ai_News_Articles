New Bill in Parliament Proposes Bar on Social Media for Under-13s

📅 2026-08-08 📂 Top Stories Original source ↗
Key points
  • Bill proposes barring children under 13 from creating social media and gaming accounts without verified parental consent.
  • It mandates annual child safety audits for platforms to identify and mitigate risks to minors.
  • The bill is set to be introduced in Parliament by Baijayant Panda.

What the Bill Proposes

A private member's bill set to be introduced in Parliament by MP Baijayant Panda could change how children interact with digital platforms. The proposed legislation seeks to bar children under 13 from creating accounts on social media and gaming platforms without verified parental consent. If passed, platforms would be required to implement age-gating mechanisms and obtain verifiable permission from a parent or guardian before allowing minors to sign up.

The bill also mandates annual child safety audits for social media and gaming companies. These audits would assess risks such as exposure to harmful content, cyberbullying, and data exploitation. Platforms would need to submit findings to a designated authority, though the specifics of enforcement and penalties have not yet been detailed in public summaries of the bill.

Why This Matters

India has one of the largest youth populations online, and concerns about children's safety on digital platforms have grown steadily. Recent incidents of online grooming, gaming addiction, and exposure to inappropriate content have pushed child safety up the legislative agenda. The bill's focus on parental consent brings India in line with regulations like the UK's Age Appropriate Design Code and the US Children's Online Privacy Protection Act, though it goes further by explicitly covering gaming platforms.

Industry watchers note that implementing age verification at scale is technically challenging. Platforms would likely rely on document-based verification or parental ID checks, raising questions about privacy and data security. The bill's approach to parental consent—requiring verification rather than a simple checkbox—could set a precedent for how digital identity is managed in India.

Reactions and Concerns

Child rights advocates have broadly welcomed the move, calling it a necessary step to protect minors in an increasingly digital world. However, some experts have expressed caution, arguing that a blanket ban on under-13s could push children to misrepresent their age or use platforms unofficially. They suggest that a combination of education, parental controls, and platform design changes might be more effective than a hard age barrier.

The bill also raises questions about enforcement. India's digital ecosystem is vast, with numerous small and medium platforms operating alongside global giants. Regulators would need a robust mechanism to ensure compliance, and it remains unclear whether the proposed audits would apply uniformly to all platforms or only to those above a certain user threshold. Officials have not yet confirmed the scope or the timeline for implementation.

What Happens Next

The bill is expected to be introduced in Parliament in the coming session, where it will face debate and potential amendments. Its fate as a private member's bill means it has limited chance of becoming law without government backing, but it signals growing political will to address child online safety. Watch for the government's response—if it adopts similar provisions in its own digital regulations, the bill's core ideas could become reality sooner than expected.
