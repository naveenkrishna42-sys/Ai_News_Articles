IndiGo Pune–Guwahati Flight Returns After Landing Gear Fails To Retract

✍️ By TIVRA News Editorial Desk 📅 2026-11-15 📂 Analysis Original source ↗
Key points
  • The fault was a failure to retract, not to extend — a configuration the aircraft is certified to land in
  • All 192 passengers and crew are reported safe, with no injuries in early accounts
  • Engineering inspection and DGCA reporting will determine cause, aircraft downtime and passenger entitlements

What happened at Pune

An IndiGo flight bound for Guwahati turned back and landed safely at Pune after the landing gear reportedly failed to retract following take-off. Reports cite 192 passengers and crew on board, all accounted for safe. The aircraft returned to its departure airport, the standard conservative choice when a crew cannot confirm a normal gear retraction.

The distinction matters: this is a failure to retract, not a failure to extend. A gear that stays down leaves the aircraft in a configuration it is certified to land in, which sharply narrows the risk profile.

Why a retraction failure still triggers an emergency

Landing gear is retracted hydraulically, sequenced by proximity sensors and held by mechanical uplocks. A fault can sit anywhere in that chain, from a sensor and actuator to a hydraulic issue or mechanical jam, and crews train for it repeatedly in simulators.

Standard practice is to run the quick reference handbook, attempt corrective steps, then decide whether to continue or return. With the gear down, drag and fuel burn rise while range shrinks, so pilots typically stay near the departure airport, burn fuel to reach landing weight, and request emergency handling so rescue crews stand by. Ground teams then inspect the runway and the aircraft for tyre or brake damage.

What this means for flyers

A return-to-base decision reflects procedure, not improvisation. Declaring an emergency unlocks priority handling, longer runway margins and rescue cover, and it is made early and deliberately. Expect the aircraft to sit out of service while engineers confirm the fault, which is why a morning departure can turn into an overnight delay.

Practical steps for affected travellers: stay seated until the crew instructs otherwise, keep boarding passes and documents handy, and rely on the airline app and official statements rather than unverified social posts for rebooking and compensation details.

Technical deep dive: why a gear that stays down is a serious but manageable problem

On a typical narrowbody such as the A320 family, which IndiGo operates in large numbers, the landing gear is retracted by hydraulic actuators, locked up by mechanical uplocks, and monitored by proximity sensors that tell the crew whether each strut is truly up and locked. If any one of those signals disagrees, the system will often refuse to move at all, which is a design choice: an ambiguous indication is treated as a fault rather than as a command to be overridden.

Flying with the gear down is possible but constrained. Drag rises sharply, fuel burn climbs, and speed is limited to the gear-extended maximum, which for a typical A320 is in the region of 280 knots or about Mach 0.67. None of those limits threaten the airframe; they simply make continuing to the destination uneconomical and, in many cases, unnecessary when the departure airport is close. That is the arithmetic behind the return to Pune.

Crews also train for the opposite failure, a gear that will not extend, which is far more serious because it can lead to a gear-up landing. A retraction failure, by contrast, usually ends with the aircraft landing in its normal configuration, tyres and brakes intact, followed by engineering inspection rather than a runway closure.

The maintenance side: what happens after the aircraft parks

Once on the ground, engineers typically inspect the tyres and brakes for heat or damage, check hydraulic systems for leaks, and pull maintenance logs to see whether the fault is a sensor, a switch, an actuator or a genuine mechanical jam. The aircraft is then either repaired and released, or held under the airline's Minimum Equipment List, which defines what can be deferred and under what conditions. A defect that affects gear retraction is rarely deferrable for long, so an overnight or multi-day grounding is common, and passengers are usually re-accommodated on later services or rival carriers depending on seat availability.

Compensation under the DGCA's passenger rights framework is triggered by the cause and length of the delay, so a dramatic-sounding return to base does not automatically mean a payout, while a long wait on a fault within the airline's control might. The airline's written reason for the delay is therefore the document that matters most.
