Saturn may produce 1,000 tonnes of diamonds annually, scientists say

📅 2026-07-23 📂 Science Original source ↗
Key points
  • Saturn's atmosphere may generate about 1,000 tonnes of diamonds annually.
  • Diamonds form when lightning breaks methane into carbon, which then falls and hardens.
  • Scientists have not yet confirmed these estimates with direct observation.

Diamond factory in the sky

Saturn may be the solar system's most unexpected gem mine. Scientists now believe the ringed planet could produce roughly 1,000 tonnes of diamonds every year deep within its thick atmosphere.

The estimate comes from new analysis of known chemical and physical processes inside Saturn. Researchers have long suspected that the planet's extreme pressure and heat could turn carbon into diamonds. The latest calculations put the annual yield at a staggering figure.

How Saturn's diamonds form

Lightning storms in Saturn's upper atmosphere break apart methane molecules. This releases carbon atoms, which then clump together and start falling toward the planet's core.

As the carbon particles sink deeper, the pressure rises. At a depth of roughly 6,000 kilometres, the pressure becomes intense enough to compress the carbon into graphite. A further 6,000 kilometres down, the graphite transforms into diamonds. These diamonds continue their descent, eventually melting into liquid carbon near Saturn's core.

The entire process depends on lightning frequency and methane concentration, both of which remain uncertain. Scientists have not yet measured these variables directly around Saturn.

Uncertainty remains

The 1,000-tonne figure is a theoretical projection. No probe has ever sampled Saturn's deep atmosphere. The Cassini spacecraft, which studied Saturn until 2017, operated in the upper atmosphere and could not confirm diamond formation at depth.

Some researchers caution that the actual rate could be lower or higher. Factors such as the exact methane abundance, lightning flash rates, and how efficiently carbon particles grow all affect the outcome. Until a future mission can directly observe these layers, the estimates will remain models.

Similar processes may occur on Jupiter and Uranus, though conditions there would produce different yields.

What this means

For now, these diamonds are beyond reach. They form at depths where pressure would crush any spacecraft. Retrieving even a single carat would require technology that does not exist.

The finding does, however, deepen understanding of carbon cycles on gas giants. It also raises questions about how much carbon is locked inside Saturn compared to its visible rings and atmosphere.

Scientists hope that future orbital missions equipped with atmospheric entry probes could one day test these predictions. Until then, Saturn will keep its diamond hoard hidden.
