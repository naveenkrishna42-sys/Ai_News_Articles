How to photograph the partial solar eclipse safely

📅 2026-08-12 📂 Technology Original source ↗
Key points
  • Use certified solar filters or eclipse glasses over the lens
  • Smartphone sensors can be damaged by direct sun exposure
  • Practice framing and focus before the eclipse begins

The partial solar eclipse is a rare celestial event that many will want to capture on camera. But pointing your smartphone or DSLR directly at the sun can be risky—both for your eyes and your equipment.

Experts warn that the sun's intense light can damage camera sensors, just as it can harm your retina. The key is preparation and the right gear.

Why you shouldn't just point and shoot

Smartphone cameras are not designed to stare at the sun. The concentrated light can overheat the sensor, causing permanent spots or lines in future photos.

Even with a brief glance through the viewfinder, the risk of eye injury is real. Unlike a total eclipse, where the sun is fully covered, a partial eclipse leaves a sliver of blinding light visible.

Use a certified solar filter

The safest way is to attach a solar filter to your lens. For phones, you can hold a pair of eclipse glasses in front of the camera—or use a specially designed clip-on filter.

For DSLRs and mirrorless cameras, a solar filter that fits over the front of the lens is essential. Never use a neutral density filter alone, as it doesn't block enough infrared and ultraviolet light.

Set up before the eclipse begins

Practice your framing and focus on the sun before the partial phase starts. Autofocus may struggle, so switch to manual focus and set it to infinity, then fine-tune.

Use a tripod to keep the camera steady, and consider using a remote shutter or a timer to avoid shake.

Shooting through eclipse glasses

If you don't have a filter, you can hold eclipse glasses directly in front of your phone's lens. This works best if the glasses are large enough to cover the entire aperture.

Make sure the glasses are ISO-certified and not scratched or damaged.

Post-processing and sharing

Once you've captured the shots, you can enhance them with editing apps to bring out the sun's corona or the moon's silhouette.

Share your photos online, but remember: the real experience is watching the sky, not just your screen.

As the eclipse passes, take a moment to enjoy the dimming light and the cool breeze. Future eclipses are on the horizon, and your new skills will serve you well.
