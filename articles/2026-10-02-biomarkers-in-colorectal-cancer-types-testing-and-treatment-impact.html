Biomarkers in Colorectal Cancer: Types, Testing, and Treatment Impact

Introduction

Colorectal cancer (CRC) treatment has shifted from a one‑size‑fits‑all approach to precision medicine driven by molecular biomarkers. These genetic and molecular alterations help oncologists predict response to specific drugs, avoid ineffective therapies, and enroll patients in clinical trials. Understanding the main biomarkers and their testing pathways is essential for both clinicians and patients navigating care decisions.

Major Biomarker Categories

Microsatellite Instability‑High / Mismatch Repair Deficiency (MSI‑H/dMMR)

MSI‑H/dMMR results from defects in DNA mismatch repair genes, leading to high mutation loads. It is observed in roughly 15 % of early‑stage and 5 % of metastatic colorectal cancers, often linked to Lynch syndrome. Patients with MSI‑H tumors derive significant benefit from immune checkpoint inhibitors such as pembrolizumab or nivolumab, either alone or in combination with ipilimumab.

RAS Mutations (KRAS and NRAS)

Activating mutations in KRAS (exons 2, 3, 4) and NRAS are present in about 40 % of colorectal cancers. These mutations render tumors resistant to anti‑EGFR monoclonal antibodies (cetuximab, panitumumab). Consequently, RAS status must be determined before initiating EGFR‑targeted therapy; only RAS‑wild‑type patients are candidates for these agents.

BRAF V600E Mutation

The BRAF V600E alteration occurs in approximately 8‑12 % of metastatic colorectal cancers and is associated with aggressive disease and poorer prognosis. Standard chemotherapy shows limited efficacy. Targeted regimens combining a BRAF inhibitor (encorafenib) with an EGFR inhibitor (cetuximab) have demonstrated improved survival in this subgroup.

HER2 Amplification

HER2 gene amplification is found in 2‑3 % of metastatic colorectal cancers, more frequently in left‑sided tumors. HER2‑positive disease may respond to HER2‑directed therapies such as trastuzumab plus pertuzumab or the antibody‑drug conjugate trastuzumab deruxtecan (T‑DXd), especially after progression on standard treatments.

NTRK Gene Fusions

NTRK1‑3 fusions are rare (<1 % of CRC) but produce constitutively active TRK proteins driving tumorigenesis. Patients harboring NTRK fusions are eligible for TRK inhibitors larotrectinib or entrectinib, which can produce durable responses irrespective of tumor location.

Clinical Implications and Treatment Selection

Biomarker testing directs the first‑line and later‑line therapy algorithm. For metastatic CRC, the workflow typically begins with RAS and BRAF testing to decide on EGFR inhibitors or BRAF/EGFR combos. MSI‑H/dMMR testing is performed early to identify candidates for immunotherapy upfront. HER2 and NTRK assessments are reserved for patients who have exhausted standard options or show clinicopathologic clues suggestive of these alterations.

Testing platforms include polymerase chain reaction (PCR)‑based assays, next‑generation sequencing (NGS) panels, and immunohistochemistry (IHC) for MMR proteins. NGS offers the advantage of simultaneous detection of multiple biomarkers, reducing tissue consumption and turnaround time.

Testing Guidelines and Patient Considerations

Major societies such as NCCN and ESMO recommend reflex MSI‑H/dMMR testing for all colorectal cancers at diagnosis, and RAS/BRAF testing for metastatic disease prior to initiating systemic therapy. Adequate tissue preservation—either from biopsy or resection specimen—is critical; when tissue is limited, liquid biopsy (circulating tumor DNA) can serve as an adjunct for detecting KRAS, NRAS, BRAF, and certain resistance mutations, though it is less reliable for MSI‑H detection.

Patients should discuss the implications of biomarker results with their oncology team, including potential eligibility for clinical trials, expected benefits, and possible side effects of targeted agents. Insurance coverage for comprehensive genomic profiling varies, and prior authorization may be required.

Emerging Biomarkers and Future Directions

Research continues to identify novel predictive markers such as tumor mutational burden (TMB), specific fibroblast growth factor receptor (FGFR) alterations, and epigenetic signatures. Early‑phase trials explore antibody‑drug conjugates targeting CEA or guanylyl cyclase C, and combination regimens pairing immunotherapy with targeted agents. As these data mature, biomarker panels will likely expand, further personalizing colorectal cancer management.
