Tumbbad to Raincoat: 7 Hindi Films Where Rain Steals the Show

📅 2026-08-07 📂 Movies Original source ↗
Key points
  • Rain is used as a narrative device in Hindi cinema, setting mood and driving plot.
  • Films like Tumbbad, Raincoat, and others showcase rain as a character in itself.
  • The list highlights how directors use weather to enhance storytelling and emotion.

Hindi cinema has long had a love affair with rain. From romantic duets in the hills to dramatic confrontations in the middle of a storm, the monsoon has been a recurring motif. But in some films, rain is more than just a backdrop — it becomes the very soul of the story, shaping characters, moods, and fates.

A recent feature by News18 spotlights seven Hindi films where rain is, arguably, the real main character. These are not just films where it happens to rain; they are stories where the downpour is integral to the narrative itself. Here's a look at why these films stand out.

The Unrelenting Downpour of Tumbbad

Tumbbad, the 2018 fantasy horror, is perhaps the most striking example. Set in a rain-soaked village, the film's relentless monsoon is not just an atmospheric choice — it is a plot driver. The rain floods the ruins, exposes hidden paths, and creates the haunting, claustrophobic feel that defines the movie.

Director Rahi Anil Barve uses the downpour to mirror the greed and decay at the heart of the story. The constant drizzle and thunderous clouds become a character that watches over every secret. Without the rain, the myth of the golden treasure would lose its ominous weight.

Rain as a Metaphor in Raincoat

Rituparno Ghosh's Raincoat, starring Aishwarya Rai and Ajay Devgn, is a masterclass in quiet melancholy. The film unfolds over a single rainy day in Kolkata, where two former lovers meet by chance. The rain outside mirrors the tears unshed, the words unspoken.

The constant drizzle outside the characters' conversations is not a distraction but a companion to their loneliness. It isolates them in their shared past and makes their present connection feel fleeting. Here, rain is the gentle, persistent pressure that forces a confession — or at least, a moment of honesty.

Weather That Shapes Fate

The list includes other films where weather dictates outcomes. In some, a rainstorm delays a journey, leading to a chance encounter. In others, a flood washes away a family's home, propelling the plot into new, uncertain territory. These films use rain as a narrative tool that characters cannot control, making their struggles feel more helpless.

Directors like Sanjay Leela Bhansali and Imtiaz Ali have also used rain to heighten emotional peaks. In Devdas, the rain-soaked climax at the gates of a mansion is as much about the storm as it is about the characters. In Rockstar, the rain during the final concert scene is a release of pent-up anger and love.

Why Rain Works in Cinema

Cinema is a visual medium, and rain offers instant texture. It adds a layer of sound, movement, and light that a dry scene cannot. Rain can make a city look romantic, a village look tragic, or a character look vulnerable. It is a cheap, effective way to say something without dialogue.

In Indian cinema, where melodrama is often embraced, rain amplifies the heightened reality. It allows actors to perform with more intensity, and audiences to feel the weight of every scene.

The List That Sparks Debate

The News18 list, which includes both iconic and lesser-known titles, has already sparked debate among film buffs. Some argue that other films — like Megha or Barsaat — deserve a spot. Others feel the chosen films perfectly capture the essence of rain as a character.

What is clear is that this list is not exhaustive. It is a conversation starter, a way to revisit films with a new lens. The next time you watch a rain-soaked scene, ask yourself: is the rain just there, or is it telling the story?

As the monsoon season continues across India, this list offers a good reason to revisit these films. Whether you prefer the horror of Tumbbad or the tender sorrow of Raincoat, there is no denying that rain, when used well, can be the most memorable character of all.
