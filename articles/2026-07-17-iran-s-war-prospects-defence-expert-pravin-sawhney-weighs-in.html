Iran's War Prospects: Defence Expert Pravin Sawhney Weighs In

📅 2026-07-17 📂 Wars & Conflicts Original source ↗
Key points
  • Pravin Sawhney outlines Iran's asymmetric warfare capabilities and strategic depth.
  • Expert notes weaknesses in Iran's air defence, logistics, and economic resilience.
  • Analysis suggests Iran faces significant challenges in a protracted conflict scenario.

Strategic Depth vs. Military Reality

Defence expert Pravin Sawhney has offered a sobering assessment of Iran's war-fighting capacity. In an analysis for Frontline Magazine, he argues that while Tehran possesses considerable asymmetric warfare tools, its conventional military weaknesses could prove decisive in a prolonged engagement.

Sawhney points to Iran's reliance on proxy forces and missile technology as key elements of its deterrence posture. However, he questions whether these assets can compensate for gaps in air defence, logistics, and force modernisation.

Air Power and Logistics: Critical Gaps

The expert highlights Iran's ageing air fleet as a major vulnerability. Many of its combat aircraft date back to the pre-1979 era, with limited access to spare parts and upgrades. This severely restricts Tehran's ability to establish air superiority or conduct sustained offensive operations.

Logistical constraints are another concern. Sawhney notes that Iran's defence industry, though innovative, struggles with production capacity and supply chain resilience. Sustaining a high-intensity conflict would place enormous strain on these systems.

Economic and Social Factors

Beyond military hardware, Sawhney examines Iran's economic resilience. Years of sanctions have battered the economy, limiting the government's fiscal space for war. Inflation and unemployment remain high, and public morale is fragile. A protracted war could exacerbate these internal pressures.

The expert also notes that Iran's strategic depth—its ability to absorb punishment and continue fighting—is not unlimited. While Tehran has prepared for asymmetric warfare, the cost of a full-scale conventional conflict could be crippling.

Asymmetric Capabilities: A Double-Edged Sword

Iran's strength lies in its network of proxies across the Middle East—from Hezbollah in Lebanon to militias in Iraq and Yemen. These groups allow Tehran to project power and open multiple fronts without committing its own forces directly.

But Sawhney warns that over-reliance on proxies comes with risks. These groups have their own agendas and may not always act in lockstep with Iranian interests. Moreover, a direct attack on Iran could trigger a regional conflagration that Tehran cannot control.

What the Expert Does Not Say

Sawhney stops short of predicting an outright Iranian defeat. His analysis is more nuanced: Iran can fight, but its capacity to win a conventional war is limited. The outcome would depend heavily on the nature of the conflict—whether it remains a limited confrontation or escalates into a full-scale invasion.

He also notes that Iran has invested heavily in cyber warfare and naval mines, which could complicate enemy operations. These capabilities could buy time and impose costs, but they are unlikely to change the strategic balance.

Officials in Tehran have not responded to Sawhney's analysis, and independent verification of Iran's military readiness remains difficult. The expert's views reflect one school of thought among defence analysts, not a consensus.

What happens next depends on whether diplomacy can de-escalate tensions or whether both sides are preparing for a longer, bloodier confrontation. The world will be watching.
