Total solar eclipse Aug 12, 2026: One week to go, how to watch safely

📅 2026-08-05 📂 Science Original source ↗
Key points
  • The total solar eclipse on August 12, 2026 is one week away, with a path crossing parts of Spain and other regions.
  • Skywatchers are advised to use proper eye protection, as the same day brings the peak of the Perseid meteor shower.
  • NASA and other agencies have released viewing tips for August, including two eclipses this month.

With exactly one week to go before the total solar eclipse on August 12, 2026, skywatchers across the globe are making final preparations. The celestial event, which will sweep across parts of Europe and elsewhere, has already generated significant buzz among astronomers and casual observers alike. But as excitement builds, experts are urging caution: looking directly at the Sun without proper protection can cause permanent eye damage.

Where and when to watch

The eclipse's path of totality will cross northern Spain and other regions, offering a rare daytime spectacle. For those outside the path, a partial eclipse will be visible in many parts of Europe, Africa, and the Americas. The exact timing varies by location, so local astronomy groups and online resources are providing detailed schedules.

In India, while the eclipse won't be visible, enthusiasts can follow live streams from space agencies and observatories. The Indian Space Research Organisation (ISRO) has not yet announced any specific programming, but international feeds are expected to cover the event extensively.

Safety first: protecting your eyes

Eye safety is the single most important concern for eclipse viewers. Regular sunglasses, even very dark ones, are not sufficient. Only certified eclipse glasses or handheld solar viewers that meet the ISO 12312-2 international standard should be used. These filters block harmful ultraviolet and infrared radiation.

For those using telescopes or binoculars, solar filters must be attached to the front of the optics — never the eyepiece. Alternatively, pinhole projectors offer a simple, safe way to observe the eclipse indirectly. The European Space Agency and NASA have both published step-by-step guides for building these devices at home.

A rare double feature: Perseid meteor shower

Adding to the excitement, the Perseid meteor shower — often considered the best of the year — peaks on the same night as the eclipse. This means that after the Sun sets, observers can turn their attention skyward again to catch meteors streaking across the dark sky. The coincidence is a reminder of how dynamic our solar system is.

While the two events are unrelated, their timing offers a full day of astronomical wonder. However, the bright Moon during the eclipse might interfere with meteor viewing, so patience and a dark location will be key for meteor enthusiasts.

What to expect on August 12

The eclipse will begin in the morning local time in Spain, with totality lasting just a few minutes — a fleeting moment that leaves many viewers speechless. During totality, the Sun's corona will be visible, and the sky will darken dramatically, with stars appearing in the daytime.

Weather is always a wildcard. Forecasts are still uncertain, but cloud cover could obscure the view in some areas. Back-up plans, such as traveling to a different location or relying on live streams, are advisable for those who have made the journey.

Looking ahead

After August 12, the next total solar eclipse will occur in 2027, crossing parts of Africa and the Middle East. For now, all eyes are on the coming week. As the countdown continues, remember to enjoy the event responsibly — and share the experience with fellow sky gazers.
