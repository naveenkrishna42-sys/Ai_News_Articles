NISAR satellite to aid disaster prediction, says NASA-ISRO

📅 2026-08-03 📂 Space Original source ↗
Key points
  • NISAR is a joint NASA-ISRO Earth observation satellite.
  • It will monitor ground movements to provide early warnings for natural disasters.
  • The satellite uses advanced radar to detect subtle changes in the Earth's surface.

In a significant leap for disaster management, the NISAR satellite—a collaborative project between NASA and the Indian Space Research Organisation (ISRO)—is set to transform how scientists predict natural calamities. With its advanced radar imaging capabilities, NISAR will track minute changes in the Earth's surface, offering a potential early warning system for earthquakes, landslides, and other geophysical hazards.

The satellite, whose name stands for NASA-ISRO Synthetic Aperture Radar, is designed to scan nearly the entire planet every 12 days. By measuring shifts in the ground with remarkable precision—down to centimetres—it can detect the subtle deformations that often precede a major event. For a country like India, where seismic activity and monsoon-triggered landslides pose recurring threats, this capability could prove invaluable.

How NISAR Works

At the heart of NISAR lies a dual-frequency radar system that penetrates clouds and works day or night. It uses two radar bands: the L-band, which can see through vegetation and dry soil, and the S-band, ideal for observing rough terrain and urban areas. Together, they create a high-resolution map of ground movement.

This continuous monitoring allows scientists to spot 'fault creep' along earthquake fault lines or the slow slide of an unstable hillside. When these movements accelerate, it could signal an impending rupture or landslide, giving authorities crucial lead time to evacuate vulnerable communities.

Unlike traditional satellite imagery, which captures static snapshots, NISAR's interferometric radar compares images over time. This technique, known as InSAR, reveals even the slightest vertical or horizontal displacement of the land surface.

Implications for India and Beyond

For the Indian subcontinent, nestled on the tectonically active Indo-Australian plate, the benefits are immediate. Regions like the Himalayas, which face constant seismic pressure, and the Western Ghats, prone to landslides during heavy rains, stand to gain immensely. Local agencies could use NISAR data to update hazard maps, design safer infrastructure, and plan disaster response more effectively.

Globally, the satellite will contribute to a broader understanding of earthquake cycles and volcanic activity. Scientists hope that by correlating surface changes with seismic events, they can develop predictive models that work across different geological settings.

The satellite also promises to assist in monitoring groundwater depletion, glacier dynamics, and even the health of forests—making it a versatile tool for environmental management.

Challenges and the Road Ahead

While the potential is vast, translating raw data into actionable warnings is no small task. It requires robust computational models and close collaboration between space agencies, geologists, and disaster management authorities. Officials have not yet confirmed the exact timeline for full operational use, but the data is expected to flow within months of launch.

There are also limitations. Predicting the exact time and magnitude of an earthquake remains a scientific challenge, even with better data. NISAR can indicate where stress is building, but it cannot yet say when it will release. Still, any advance in early warning could save thousands of lives.

As the satellite begins its mission, scientists at NASA and ISRO will be watching closely. The first year of data will be crucial for calibrating instruments and validating models, with the scientific community eagerly awaiting the first global maps of surface deformation.

In the coming months, expect more details on NISAR's initial findings and its integration into operational disaster warning systems. For now, the satellite stands as a testament to what international collaboration can achieve in the service of humanity.
