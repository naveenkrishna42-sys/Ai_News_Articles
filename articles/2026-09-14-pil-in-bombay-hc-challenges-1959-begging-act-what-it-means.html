A Constitutional Challenge to a Colonial-Era Law

A public interest litigation has been moved in the Bombay High Court challenging the constitutional validity of the Bombay Prevention of Begging Act, 1959. The petition argues that the legislation, which remains in force in Maharashtra, criminalises poverty instead of addressing its structural causes. The matter has been listed before the court, and the petition seeks a declaration that key provisions of the Act violate fundamental rights.

The Bombay Prevention of Begging Act is a legacy statute enacted when Bombay was a bilingual state. It empowers authorities to detain persons found begging and place them in certified institutions. The challenge centres on whether such detention and criminalisation can survive constitutional scrutiny in contemporary India.

What the Bombay Prevention of Begging Act, 1959 Actually Says

The Act defines "begging" broadly, covering soliciting alms in public places, exposing wounds or deformities to obtain charity, and using children for the purpose of begging. It allows a magistrate to order detention of a person found begging in a certified institution for periods that can extend up to several years, depending on the classification of the offence.

Crucially, the law treats repeat offenders more harshly and permits institutional detention without the procedural safeguards typical of criminal trials. This architecture is central to the petitioner's argument that the statute is punitive rather than rehabilitative.

The Constitutional Arguments at the Heart of the Petition

Article 21: Right to Life and Personal Liberty

The petition contends that detaining individuals for the act of begging amounts to deprivation of personal liberty without the due process guarantees read into Article 21. In 2018, the Delhi High Court struck down several provisions of the Bombay Prevention of Begging Act as extended to Delhi, holding that the state cannot criminalise poverty. The Bombay petition draws on that reasoning.

Article 14: Equality Before Law

Because the Act targets visible poverty while leaving systemic deprivation untouched, the petitioner argues it is arbitrary and unreasonable classification. Similar laws have been repealed in Delhi, Karnataka, Punjab and Haryana, creating what the petition describes as a discriminatory regime within the Union.

Article 19(1)(a): Freedom of Expression

Begging as a means of survival, the petition argues, can involve expressive conduct protected under free speech jurisprudence. The state has not demonstrated a compelling interest that justifies a blanket prohibition.

Real-World Implications for Maharashtra

Maharashtra operates a network of beggar homes and remand institutions under the Act. If the Bombay High Court strikes down the statute or its key provisions, the state will need to redesign its rehabilitation framework. Urban local bodies, which currently rely on the Act for clearance drives, would lose their primary legal tool.

For civil society organisations and legal aid groups, the petition represents a test of whether courts will continue the trajectory set by the Delhi High Court. For residents and businesses, the outcome will shape how visible homelessness is managed in Mumbai, Pune and other cities.

Key Takeaways

  • Constitutional challenge: The PIL directly questions whether a 1959 statute can criminalise survival in a rights-based constitutional order.
  • Judicial precedent: The Delhi High Court's 2018 ruling provides a strong template for striking down similar provisions.
  • State impact: Maharashtra may need to replace detention with welfare-based rehabilitation if the Act is struck down.
  • Timeline: No final hearing date has been confirmed; the petition is at an early stage.
  • Broader signal: The case will influence how other states with similar colonial-era laws respond to legal challenges.

What Comes Next

The Bombay High Court will hear the petition and likely seek the Maharashtra government's response. The state may defend the Act as a welfare measure rather than a punitive one. The outcome will determine whether one of India's oldest begging laws survives, or whether Maharashtra joins the states that have already repealed comparable legislation.
