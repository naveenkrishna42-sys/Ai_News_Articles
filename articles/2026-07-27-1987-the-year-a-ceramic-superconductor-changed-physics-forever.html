1987: The Year a Ceramic Superconductor Changed Physics Forever

📅 2026-07-27 📂 Science Original source ↗
Key points
  • The 1987 discovery of yttrium barium copper oxide (YBCO) superconductor at 93 Kelvin.
  • First superconductor to work with inexpensive liquid nitrogen instead of costly liquid helium.
  • Enabled practical applications like magnetic levitation, MRI, and power transmission.
  • Triggered a global race in condensed matter physics and materials science.

A Ceramic That Defied Expectations

In 1987, a year after the breakthrough reignited interest in high-temperature superconductivity, physicists achieved something extraordinary. They cooled a ceramic made of yttrium, barium, and copper oxide to 93 Kelvin—about minus 180 degrees Celsius. Then they watched it levitate a magnet indefinitely.

This was the first superconductor cold enough to run on liquid nitrogen. Before this, superconductors required expensive liquid helium, which limited their use. The discovery opened the door to practical applications that had been purely theoretical.

Why 93 Kelvin Mattered

Liquid nitrogen boils at 77 Kelvin. The new material—nicknamed YBCO—operated well above that threshold. Suddenly, scientists could cool a superconductor with a cheap, abundant coolant instead of liquid helium, which costs roughly 10 times more per litre.

Researchers at the University of Houston and the University of Alabama reported the find. The result was immediate: labs around the world began reproducing the experiment within days. The race to understand and improve YBCO was on.

Levitation and the Meissner Effect

When a superconductor expels all magnetic fields from its interior—a phenomenon called the Meissner effect—it can make a magnet hover in midair. YBCO demonstrated this perfectly. The magnet stayed suspended above the ceramic disc without any visible support.

That single image of a floating magnet became iconic. It showed the public that superconductors weren't just laboratory curiosities. They could defy gravity and potentially reshape technology.

The Fallout: A New Era in Physics

The 1987 discovery was not the first high-temperature superconductor. That honour belongs to a lanthanum-based ceramic discovered in 1986 by Georg Bednorz and Alex Müller, who won a Nobel Prize the following year. But YBCO was the first to cross the liquid-nitrogen threshold.

It triggered a flood of research. Scientists searched for materials that could superconduct at even higher temperatures—ideally at room temperature. That search continues today. Meanwhile, YBCO found its way into prototypes for power cables, magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) machines, and maglev trains.

A Long Road to Commercial Use

YBCO is brittle and difficult to manufacture in long lengths. That has slowed its adoption. But progress in thin-film deposition and wire fabrication has made it viable for niche applications. Superconductor cables using YBCO now operate in power grids in the United States and South Korea.

The material also powers some of the world's most powerful magnets, including those used in particle accelerators and fusion reactors. The levitation effect, once a party trick, is now a design principle for frictionless bearings and energy storage flywheels.

What Comes Next

Researchers continue to push the boundaries. In 2023, a team claimed to have observed superconductivity at room temperature in a nitrogen-doped lutetium hydride, though that result remains contested. The hunt for an affordable, room-temperature superconductor is still the holy grail of condensed matter physics.

For now, YBCO remains the benchmark. More than three decades after that floating magnet, it still defines what is possible without helium.
