Darwin's 150-year-old prediction confirmed by insect-eating alpine flower in China

📅 2026-08-08 📂 Science Original source ↗
Key points
  • Chinese scientists identify carnivory in Saxifraga, an alpine flowering plant
  • Multidisciplinary evidence confirms Darwin's 150-year-old prediction
  • Discovery suggests many more carnivorous plants may exist undiscovered

For 150 years, Charles Darwin's prediction about a carnivorous plant remained a botanical curiosity. Now, scientists in China have confirmed his theory with the discovery of an insect-eating alpine flower.

The plant, belonging to the genus Saxifraga, was found growing in the high-altitude regions of China. It has a sticky surface that traps and digests insects, a trait that Darwin had hypothesised about in the 19th century but could never prove.

A Sticky Secret Revealed

Researchers used a multidisciplinary approach to confirm the carnivorous nature of the plant. They analysed its morphology, conducted field observations, and performed biochemical tests to detect digestive enzymes on its leaves.

The evidence was conclusive. The plant not only traps insects but also absorbs nutrients from them, a hallmark of true carnivory. This places Saxifraga in an exclusive club of plants that feed on animals.

Darwin's Visionary Insight

Darwin, the father of evolution, had speculated that certain plants with sticky glandular hairs might be capable of carnivory. He even corresponded with botanists about the possibility, but lacked the tools to verify it.

His prediction was based on careful observation of plant morphology and behaviour. The new discovery vindicates his foresight, showing that his understanding of plant adaptations was far ahead of his time.

Expanding the Carnivorous Plant Roster

Carnivorous plants are rare, with only a few hundred known species worldwide. They include the Venus flytrap, pitcher plants, and sundews. The addition of Saxifraga is significant because it belongs to a genus previously considered non-carnivorous.

This finding suggests that carnivory may have evolved independently in multiple plant lineages. It also raises the possibility that other plants with sticky surfaces might be secretly carnivorous, waiting to be discovered.

Why So Many More Could Exist

The discovery was made in an alpine environment, where nutrient-poor soils make carnivory advantageous. Such habitats are often understudied, and scientists believe many more carnivorous plants could be hiding in plain sight.

The researchers note that their methods could be applied to other suspected carnivorous plants, potentially revealing a larger number of killer plants than previously thought. This could reshape our understanding of plant ecology and evolution.

While the discovery is exciting, officials have not yet confirmed the full extent of the plant's range or its conservation status. Further studies are needed to understand its ecological role and how it fits into the broader picture of carnivorous plant evolution.

As scientists continue to explore remote alpine regions, Darwin's legacy lives on. His 150-year-old theory, once a footnote in botanical history, has now become a cornerstone of modern plant science. The discovery of this insect-eating flower in China is a reminder that nature still holds many secrets, and that some of the most profound insights come from paying attention to the smallest details.

Moving forward, researchers will likely expand their search to other sticky-leaved plants in similar habitats. The next confirmation of carnivory might be just around the corner, and with it, a deeper understanding of how plants adapt to extreme environments.
