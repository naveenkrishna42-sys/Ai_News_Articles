Understanding Lung Health Basics

Lung function determines how efficiently oxygen enters the bloodstream and carbon dioxide is removed. In healthy adults, the forced expiratory volume in one second (FEV1) should be at least 80 percent of the predicted value based on age, height and sex. Knowing these reference points helps you interpret simple measurements you can take at home.

Practical Self‑Check Methods You Can Do at Home

Resting Respiratory Rate

Count the number of breaths you take in one minute while sitting quietly. A normal range for adults is 12‑20 breaths per minute. Rates consistently above 20 may signal early airway irritation or anxiety‑related hyperventilation.

Peak Flow Measurement

Using an inexpensive peak flow meter (≈ ₹800‑₹1,200), exhale as hard and fast as possible after a full inhalation. Record the best of three attempts. Values should fall within 80‑100 percent of your personal best, which you establish over two weeks of healthy days.

Pulse Oximetry

A fingertip SpO₂ sensor (≈ ₹1,500‑₹2,500) shows oxygen saturation. Readings of 95‑100 percent are considered normal at sea level; values below 92 percent warrant prompt medical review, especially if accompanied by shortness of breath.

Symptom Questionnaires

The COPD Assessment Test (CAT) or the modified Medical Research Council (mMRC) scale consist of a few questions about breathlessness and impact on daily activities. Scores above certain thresholds (CAT ≥ 10 or mMRC ≥ 2) suggest worsening lung disease and should trigger a clinician visit.

Red Flags That Warrant a Doctor’s Visit

Persistent cough lasting more than three weeks, coughing up blood, or unexplained weight loss are urgent signs. Wheezing that does not improve with a bronchodilator or worsening shortness of breath on minimal exertion also need evaluation.

If your peak flow drops below 80 percent of your personal best on two consecutive days, or SpO₂ stays under 92 percent at rest, schedule an appointment promptly. These objective changes often precede noticeable symptoms.

Preventive Actions and When Screening Tests Are Worth It

Avoiding tobacco smoke, limiting exposure to indoor pollutants, and getting vaccinated against influenza and pneumococcal disease reduce lung injury. For smokers over 40 years, low‑dose CT screening annually can detect early cancer, though cost (₹6,000‑₹9,000) and radiation exposure must be weighed.

Routine spirometry in a clinic (≈ ₹1,200‑₹1,800) provides the most accurate FEV1 and FVC values and is recommended every two years for those with risk factors such as occupational dust or chronic bronchitis.

Pros and Cons of Home Lung Self‑Checks

  • Pros: Immediate feedback, low recurring cost, empowers patients to spot trends.
  • Cons: User technique affects accuracy; cannot replace spirometry or imaging for definitive diagnosis.
  • Pros: Early detection of exacerbations can reduce hospital admissions.
  • Cons: False reassurance if device is faulty or readings misinterpreted.
