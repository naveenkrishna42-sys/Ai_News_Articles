Record US Cyclosporiasis Outbreak: Doctors Told to Watch for Symptoms

📅 2026-07-16 📂 Health Original source ↗
Key points
  • Over 2,100 confirmed cases reported across 38 states, the highest ever recorded
  • CDC issued a Health Alert Network advisory urging clinicians to test for Cyclospora
  • Outbreak linked to cuts in CDC parasite monitoring programs, say reports
  • Source of contamination remains unidentified; investigation ongoing

The United States is grappling with its largest recorded outbreak of cyclosporiasis, a parasitic infection that causes severe gastrointestinal distress. The Centers for Disease Control and Prevention (CDC) has issued a Health Alert Network (HAN) advisory, urging clinicians across the country to remain vigilant and test patients presenting with symptoms.

As of mid-July 2026, more than 2,100 confirmed cases have been reported across 38 states. This figure already surpasses the previous record set in 2018, when 2,299 cases were documented over an entire year. Health officials say the true number may be higher, as many mild cases go undiagnosed.

What Is Cyclosporiasis and Why Is It Spreading?

Cyclosporiasis is caused by the microscopic parasite Cyclospora cayetanensis. People typically get infected by consuming food or water contaminated with the parasite. The most common vehicle is fresh produce — particularly imported berries, herbs, and salad greens — that has been washed or sprayed with contaminated water.

Symptoms include watery diarrhoea (often described as explosive), loss of appetite, bloating, stomach cramps, nausea, and low-grade fever. The illness can last for weeks if untreated and may relapse in immunocompromised patients. The CDC notes that the infection is not transmitted directly from person to person.

The current outbreak appears to be domestically acquired, meaning patients did not travel to endemic regions. This has puzzled investigators, as Cyclospora is rarely found in US-grown produce. The source of contamination has not yet been identified.

CDC Alert and Clinical Guidance

The CDC's HAN advisory, published on 15 July 2026, directs healthcare providers to consider cyclosporiasis in any patient with prolonged diarrhoea, especially if they have not responded to standard antibiotic therapy. Stool testing specifically for Cyclospora is required, as routine ova and parasite exams often miss the organism.

Treatment involves a course of trimethoprim-sulfamethoxazole (TMP-SMX), an antibiotic that is effective against the parasite. For patients with sulfa allergies, alternative therapies are limited and must be managed by an infectious disease specialist. The CDC warns that delayed diagnosis can lead to prolonged illness and unnecessary hospitalisations.

Monitoring Cuts Linked to Outbreak Severity

Reports indicate that the scale of this outbreak may be linked to earlier cuts in federal monitoring programs. Under the leadership of Health and Human Services Secretary Robert F. Kennedy Jr., the CDC's foodborne illness surveillance infrastructure was reportedly scaled back. According to a USA Today investigation, the agency reduced staff and resources dedicated to tracking parasites like Cyclospora.

Critics argue that these cuts left public health officials blind to the early signs of an outbreak. The CDC has not directly commented on the connection, but the timing has raised concerns among infectious disease experts. "When you stop looking, you don't find things until they become big problems," one epidemiologist told reporters.

What Patients Should Know

Health officials advise anyone experiencing diarrhoea lasting more than three days, especially if accompanied by fatigue or stomach pain, to see a doctor and request a Cyclospora test. The infection is treatable, but early diagnosis is key.

To reduce risk, the CDC recommends washing all fruits and vegetables thoroughly under running water. However, because the parasite can survive on produce even after washing, consumers are urged to cook food when possible. Imported items, particularly from regions with poor water sanitation, carry higher risk.

The Food and Drug Administration (FDA) has launched an investigation to identify the contaminated food source. Traceback efforts are underway, but officials have not yet announced any product recalls or specific items to avoid.

As the outbreak continues to grow, health agencies are racing to contain it. The CDC has called for enhanced surveillance and faster reporting. The FDA is working with importers and distributors to trace the supply chain. For now, the source remains a mystery — and the case count keeps climbing.
