AI Turns Existing MRI Scans Into a Prognostic Tool

Routine MRI scans already performed for staging multiple myeloma may hold far more information than radiologists currently extract. A new AI-driven analysis, reported by Oncodaily, automatically segments skeletal muscle and adipose tissue from these scans and correlates the results with patient outcomes. The approach repurposes imaging that hospitals in India already generate, potentially avoiding the cost of dedicated body-composition studies such as DEXA or CT-based analysis.

Multiple myeloma is the second most common haematological malignancy in India, with roughly 15,000 to 20,000 new cases annually across urban registries. Prognosis is currently guided by cytogenetics, beta-2 microglobulin, albumin and the Revised International Staging System (RISS). Body composition has long been suspected as a modifier, but manual measurement was too labour-intensive for routine practice.

What the AI Measures and Why It Matters

The model applies convolutional neural networks to T1-weighted and STIR sequences, isolating paraspinal, psoas and abdominal wall musculature alongside subcutaneous and visceral fat. Outputs are expressed as skeletal muscle index (SMI), visceral adipose tissue (VAT) area and subcutaneous fat ratio. Low SMI, or sarcopenia, has emerged as the dominant signal, associated with inferior progression-free survival and higher rates of treatment-limiting toxicity.

Excess visceral fat behaves differently from subcutaneous fat. VAT is metabolically active, secreting adipokines and inflammatory cytokines that may promote plasma cell proliferation and reduce chemotherapy tolerance. This distinction matters clinically: two patients with identical BMI can have radically different risk profiles, and BMI alone has repeatedly failed as a prognostic marker in myeloma.

Buyer and Clinician Implications in India

For hospital procurement teams, the appeal is straightforward. No new hardware is required. A software layer integrated into existing PACS infrastructure can extract body-composition data from scans already billed to the patient. Private centres in metros charge Rs 8,000 to Rs 15,000 for a myeloma MRI panel; adding AI analytics at Rs 1,000 to Rs 2,500 per study is a modest increment that could influence treatment intensity decisions.

Oncology chains evaluating such tools should demand three things: published validation on Indian patient cohorts, seamless DICOM integration without workflow disruption, and clarity on whether the software is a regulated medical device under CDSCO rules. A tool that merely reports muscle area without actionable thresholds offers limited clinical value.

Key Real-World Highlights

  • No additional imaging cost: analytics run on scans already acquired for staging.
  • Sarcopenia and high visceral fat independently predict worse outcomes.
  • BMI alone misses the risk signal; tissue-level measurement is more granular.
  • Intervention potential: nutritional support, resistance training and physiotherapy could be targeted at high-risk patients.
  • Adoption barrier: retrospective data, limited Indian cohort validation and unclear reimbursement pathways.

Practical Benchmarks Before Adoption

Clinicians should ask vendors for sensitivity and specificity figures for detecting sarcopenia against a DEXA reference standard, plus reproducibility across 1.5T scanners that dominate tier-2 and tier-3 Indian hospitals. Ask whether the model was trained on ethnically diverse data; muscle mass norms derived from Western cohorts can misclassify Indian patients, who tend to have lower baseline skeletal muscle index.

The larger opportunity lies in prehabilitation. If AI flags a patient as sarcopenic before autologous stem cell transplant, a structured 4 to 6 week programme of protein supplementation and supervised resistance training, costing Rs 15,000 to Rs 30,000, may improve tolerance and reduce hospital stay. That cost is trivial against a transplant package exceeding Rs 15 lakh.

AI-derived body composition will not replace cytogenetics or RISS. It adds a modifiable, imaging-based variable to a risk model that has long been static. For Indian oncology centres, the sensible next step is a pilot integration with prospective data collection, not wholesale adoption on retrospective evidence alone.
