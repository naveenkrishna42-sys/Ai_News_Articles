Global defence spending surpasses peace investment, raising concerns

📅 2026-07-17 📂 Wars & Conflicts Original source ↗
Key points
  • Global military expenditure has reached record highs
  • Peace-building and conflict prevention funding lags behind
  • Experts argue this imbalance undermines global security

Global military spending has reached unprecedented levels, outpacing investments in peace-building and conflict prevention. This trend, according to analysts, is self-defeating, as it prioritises arming for war over fostering stability.

Data from leading think tanks indicates that nations collectively spent nearly $2.4 trillion on defence in the past year, a sharp rise from previous decades. In contrast, funding for diplomatic missions, conflict resolution programmes, and humanitarian aid has stagnated or declined.

The numbers tell a stark story

Defence budgets have swelled across major powers, with the United States, China, and India leading the surge. European nations, spurred by regional tensions, have also hiked spending. Meanwhile, the United Nations peacekeeping budget remains underfunded, and international development aid for conflict-prone regions has shrunk.

This disparity is not just about money, experts say. It reflects a mindset where immediate threats overshadow long-term solutions. “You cannot fight fire with fire forever,” said a senior analyst at a New Delhi-based security forum. “Eventually, you need to invest in fire prevention.”

Why this matters for India

India’s own defence budget has climbed to over ₹5.94 lakh crore, making it one of the world’s top military spenders. This is driven by perceived threats from neighbours and internal security challenges. Yet, New Delhi’s allocation for peace-building—whether in Kashmir or through diplomatic outreach—remains a fraction of that amount.

Experts argue that this imbalance could perpetuate cycles of conflict. “Military strength is necessary, but without parallel investment in dialogue, development, and trust-building, it only escalates tensions,” said a former Indian diplomat familiar with the data.

The global context

Globally, the trend mirrors domestic spending patterns. Countries like Saudi Arabia and Russia have ramped up defence outlays, while contributions to multilateral peace initiatives have fallen. The World Bank reports that aid to fragile states hit a decade low last year.

Critics point out that this approach often backfires. High military spending can fuel arms races, divert resources from social welfare, and alienate potential allies. “When you spend more on weapons than on schools or hospitals, you are building a fortress, not a society,” said a researcher at a Geneva-based think tank.

What is being done?

Several international bodies have called for a rebalancing. The UN Secretary-General recently urged member states to allocate at least 10% of their defence budgets to peace-building. So far, fewer than a dozen nations have signed on.

Civil society groups are also pushing for transparency. Campaigns demanding that governments publish a “peace index” alongside military expenditure reports have gained traction in Europe and parts of Asia. But implementation remains patchy.

In India, voices from academia and the non-profit sector have urged a similar shift. They argue that the country’s soft power—its diaspora, cultural influence, and democratic credentials—could be better leveraged to reduce conflict risks.

What to watch

Whether nations can break this cycle remains uncertain. The next round of UN budget negotiations in Geneva could be a litmus test. If major powers resist reallocating funds, the gap between defence and peace spending will likely widen. For now, the world seems locked in a paradox: spending more to be safe, while undermining the very foundations of lasting peace.
