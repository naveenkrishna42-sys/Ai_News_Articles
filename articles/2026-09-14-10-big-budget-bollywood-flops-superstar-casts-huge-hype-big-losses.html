10 Big-Budget Bollywood Flops: Superstar Casts, Huge Hype, Big Losses

Key points

  • Streaming revenue and satellite rights now decide whether a tentpole recovers its money.
  • Content and word-of-mouth, not star power, drive opening-weekend-to-lifetime multipliers.

Why Star Power No Longer Guarantees a Hit

Bollywood's biggest names once carried a film to a safe opening. That equation has broken down. Over the past decade, a string of tentpoles backed by A-list casts and nine-figure marketing spends have collapsed at the box office, leaving distributors and exhibitors to absorb heavy losses.

The pattern is consistent: a record opening day, a steep fall by Monday, and a lifetime total that barely covers the print and advertising bill. For buyers of theatrical and satellite rights, the warning is clear — budget discipline and script quality now matter more than the billing order of the cast.

The 10 Big-Budget Flops, Ranked by Scale of Loss

1. Thugs of Hindostan (2018)

Aamir Khan and Amitabh Bachchan in a Yash Raj Films period epic, made on an estimated ₹200–240 crore budget. It opened to a record ₹50 crore-plus day one, then dropped more than 80% within a week. Lifetime domestic earnings stayed well under ₹150 crore.

2. Zero (2018)

Shah Rukh Khan, Anushka Sharma and Katrina Kaif headlined a VFX-heavy romance reportedly budgeted at ₹200 crore-plus. Domestic lifetime collections settled around ₹90 crore, making it one of SRK's costliest misses.

3. Kalank (2019)

Karan Johar's Dharma Productions assembled Varun Dhawan, Alia Bhatt, Aditya Roy Kapur, Sonakshi Sinha, Madhuri Dixit and Sanjay Dutt. The ₹150 crore-plus period drama failed to cross ₹80 crore domestically.

4. Panipat (2019)

Ashutosh Gowariker's Maratha war epic, with Arjun Kapoor, Sanjay Dutt and Kriti Sanon, was mounted at roughly ₹100 crore. It finished its run near ₹35 crore in India.

5. Dabangg 3 (2019)

Salman Khan's return to the franchise, plus Sonakshi Sinha and Kichcha Sudeep, was made at about ₹100 crore. Domestic collections hovered around ₹150 crore gross, but high costs and weak overseas numbers hurt returns.

6. 83 (2021)

Ranveer Singh, Deepika Padukone and an ensemble cast recreated India's 1983 World Cup win. Budgeted at an estimated ₹250 crore including marketing, it earned roughly ₹110 crore net in India.

7. Laal Singh Chaddha (2022)

Aamir Khan and Kareena Kapoor Khan in the official Forrest Gump remake, with a reported ₹180 crore budget. Domestic net collections were around ₹60 crore.

8. Samrat Prithviraj (2022)

Akshay Kumar and Manushi Chhillar in a Yash Raj Films historical, budgeted near ₹150 crore. It managed only about ₹68 crore net in India.

9. Brahmastra: Part One – Shiva (2022)

Ranbir Kapoor, Alia Bhatt and Amitabh Bachchan fronted a trilogy-launch reportedly costing ₹400 crore. Despite a strong ₹225 crore-plus Hindi net, the film's overall recovery was stretched across a three-part plan.

10. Kisi Ka Bhai Kisi Ki Jaan (2023)

Salman Khan headlined a ₹100 crore-plus Eid release that ended near ₹110 crore net in India — a thin margin once marketing and P&A were added.

What Buyers and Investors Should Take Away

  • Opening day is not a verdict. Several of these films posted record day-one numbers before collapsing over the first weekend.
  • Marketing is now a fixed cost. Print and advertising budgets of ₹30–50 crore routinely push a ₹100 crore film into loss territory.
  • Streaming and satellite rights cushion, but rarely rescue. A weak theatrical run also depresses digital and TV valuations.
  • Genre fatigue is real. Period epics and remake-heavy slates underperformed consistently between 2018 and 2023.
  • Ensemble billing does not equal footfalls. Six-star casts in Kalank and 83 could not overcome weak word-of-mouth.

The Commercial Reality

For exhibitors and distributors, these ten titles are a cautionary set. Theatrical recovery typically needs a 2.5x-to-3x multiple of the opening weekend to break even once P&A is counted. Most of the films above finished well short of that threshold.

Producers are responding with tighter budgets, revenue-share deals with stars, and faster digital windows. The lesson for the trade is straightforward: a superstar cast sells tickets on Friday, but only a strong script sells them on Monday.
