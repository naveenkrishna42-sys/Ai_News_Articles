SpaceX rocket stage on course to crash into Moon tonight

📅 2026-08-04 📂 Science Original source ↗
Key points
  • A SpaceX rocket stage weighing 9,000 pounds is expected to crash into the Moon tonight.
  • The impact will create a new crater on the lunar surface.
  • NASA will attempt to observe the lunar impact to study the event.

An Uncontrolled Descent

A discarded SpaceX rocket stage is on a collision course with the Moon, and impact is expected to occur tonight. The 9,000-pound piece of hardware has been drifting in space since its launch years ago, its orbit slowly decaying until lunar gravity took over. Now, it is set to slam into the far side of the Moon, creating a fresh crater in the process.

This is not a controlled landing. There is no guidance system, no thrusters firing to adjust the trajectory. The stage is simply following the laws of celestial mechanics, and the Moon is in its path.

What Will Happen on Impact

When the rocket stage hits the lunar surface, it will be travelling at several kilometres per second. The kinetic energy released will carve out a crater that could be tens of metres wide, kicking up debris and dust. Unlike Earth, the Moon has no atmosphere to slow the object down, so the impact will be abrupt and violent.

Astronomers and space enthusiasts have been tracking the object for weeks, refining predictions of where and when it will strike. The exact impact site has been narrowed down, but officials have not yet confirmed the precise coordinates.

NASA's Observational Effort

NASA has announced it will attempt to observe the lunar impact. The space agency plans to use its Lunar Reconnaissance Orbiter, which has been circling the Moon since 2009, to monitor the event. The orbiter may be able to capture images of the crash site, providing valuable data on how such impacts alter the lunar surface.

This is not just a spectacle. Studying the impact could help scientists understand the mechanics of crater formation, which has implications for planetary defence and future lunar missions. The data could also shed light on the composition of the Moon's subsurface material, which gets exposed during such events.

Visibility From Earth

Will you be able to see it from Earth? The short answer is no. The impact is expected to occur on the far side of the Moon, which is permanently turned away from our planet. That means the flash of impact will be hidden from direct view. However, some observatories may detect the event through reflected light or secondary effects, though such detections are uncertain.

Space agencies and amateur astronomers alike will be watching the data streams, but for the average skygazer, the event will pass without a visible trace.

A Growing Problem of Space Debris

This incident highlights a growing issue: the accumulation of space debris around Earth and the Moon. Rocket stages, defunct satellites, and other hardware are left in orbit, and some eventually fall back to Earth or crash into celestial bodies. In this case, the Moon gets a new crater, but such events also pose risks to active missions and future lunar exploration.

As more countries and private companies plan missions to the Moon, the need for responsible disposal of spacecraft components becomes more pressing. The SpaceX stage's impact is a stark reminder that what goes up must come down—or crash into something.

What Happens Next

After the impact, scientists will analyse the data from NASA's observations to determine the exact size and shape of the new crater. The findings will be compared with models of crater formation, refining our understanding of lunar geology. Meanwhile, the broader conversation about managing space debris will likely gain momentum, as the international community grapples with the consequences of decades of space exploration.

For now, all eyes are on the Moon, waiting for the silent crash that will leave a permanent mark on its surface. The event is a reminder of humanity's reach into space—and the mess we sometimes leave behind.
