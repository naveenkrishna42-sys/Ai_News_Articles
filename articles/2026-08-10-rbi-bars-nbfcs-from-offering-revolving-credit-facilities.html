RBI bars NBFCs from offering revolving credit facilities

📅 2026-08-10 📂 Banking Original source ↗
Key points
  • RBI has barred NBFCs from offering revolving credit facilities.
  • The move covers products such as credit cards and overdraft lines.
  • Existing customers are expected to be grandfathered until maturity.

The Reserve Bank of India (RBI) has barred non-banking financial companies (NBFCs) from offering revolving credit facilities, a move that will reshape the consumer lending market. The directive, issued on Friday, prohibits NBFCs from launching or renewing products that allow customers to draw, repay, and redraw funds up to a sanctioned limit, such as credit cards and overdraft lines.

Industry executives said the decision catches many lenders off guard. Until now, revolving credit was seen as a growth engine for NBFCs, particularly those targeting small-ticket personal loans and card-linked products. The RBI has not yet detailed the rationale, but sources close to the development suggest concerns over unsecured credit build-up and customer indebtedness.

What the new rule covers

The ban applies to all fresh sanctions of revolving credit facilities. Existing customers, however, are likely to be allowed to continue until the expiry of their current tenure, a senior banker familiar with the matter told TIVRA News on condition of anonymity. "The regulator wants to prevent new exposure, not disrupt ongoing relationships," the banker said.

Products in the crosshairs include credit cards issued by NBFCs, credit lines linked to prepaid instruments, and overdraft facilities against fixed deposits or securities. Loans that are disbursed in a single tranche and repaid on a fixed schedule remain unaffected. The distinction is crucial: it spares traditional term loans, which dominate NBFC balance sheets.

Impact on NBFCs and borrowers

The affected companies include several mid-sized and large NBFCs that have aggressively pushed card-based products in recent years. These firms had positioned revolving credit as a convenient alternative to bank cards, often targeting first-time borrowers with lower credit limits. The ban could force them to pivot towards term loans or co-lending arrangements with banks.

Borrowers, on the other hand, may find fewer options for flexible credit. Banks, which are not covered by this directive, continue to offer credit cards and overdraft lines. Still, customers who relied on NBFC products for small-ticket, short-term needs may face a gap, at least until banks expand their reach.

Regulatory context and industry reaction

The RBI has been tightening norms around unsecured lending for over a year. It has raised risk weights on consumer credit, nudged lenders to strengthen underwriting, and now this. The pattern points to a broader push: curtail any credit product that can silently accumulate debt without visible collateral.

Industry bodies are expected to seek clarifications in the coming days. Questions remain on whether the ban extends to co-branded cards where an NBFC partners with a bank, and how existing limits that are partially utilised will be treated. The RBI has not yet issued a detailed circular; Friday's announcement was a press release.

What happens next

NBFCs will now scramble to redesign product suites. Some may push more aggressively into secured lending, while others could deepen ties with banks to offer credit cards under the bank's licence. The market will also watch whether the RBI follows up with similar restrictions on payment apps or fintech lenders that operate as NBFCs.

For the regulator, the move signals a hardening stance on consumer leverage. With credit growth already slowing in some segments, the next few quarters will reveal whether this curbs over-indebtedness or simply pushes borrowers to less regulated channels. The RBI's detailed circular, expected within weeks, will provide the fine print that lenders desperately need.
