The Rambola Title Collision, Explained

Two Hindi film productions have landed on the same title, Rambola. One is associated with director Vishal Chaturvedi, the other with Chandraprakash Dwivedi, the filmmaker behind Pinjar and the television epic Chanakya. Reports from NDTV confirm the duplication, but neither production has issued a formal clarification on release dates or ownership of the title.

In Indian film trade practice, a title is not property in the way a trademark is. Producers register titles with associations such as the Indian Motion Picture Producers Association (IMPA) and the Film Producers Guild, but registration is a first-come claim, not an absolute bar. Two films can legally hold the same name if their registration windows and release plans do not overlap in the same market.

What We Actually Know About Each Project

Chandraprakash Dwivedi's Rambola sits in the mythological and historical space he has occupied for two decades. His track record, from Chanakya on Doordarshan to Pinjar and the more recent period spectacles, gives the project instant distributor interest. Vishal Chaturvedi's Rambola, by contrast, has far less publicised scale and cast information attached to it.

The commercial gap between the two matters. A Dwivedi title can command a wider theatrical footprint and stronger satellite and digital pre-sales. A smaller production sharing that name risks being mistaken for a duplicate, a spoof, or a delayed version of the bigger film, which directly hits its OTT valuation.

Why Title Clashes Keep Happening

Hindi cinema has a long history of duplicate titles: Dil, Aashiqui, Sholay-inspired names and dozens of Ramayana-linked titles have repeated across decades. The reason is structural. Mythology and devotion-based titles draw from a finite pool of sacred names, and producers gravitate to the same recognisable words because they carry built-in audience recall.

The cost of a clash is not legal fees. It is marketing confusion. When two films share a name, search traffic splits, trailer views cannibalise, and exhibitors must clarify which film a distributor is booking. For a mid-budget film, that confusion can shave 15-25 per cent off opening weekend footfalls.

Buyer and Viewer Implications

  • Verify the filmmaker before booking: Check the director and production house on ticketing platforms, not just the title.
  • Watch for a title change: Smaller productions typically rename or add a prefix or suffix once a bigger film locks its date.
  • OTT buyers should secure written title clarity: Streaming licences are signed on exact titles; ambiguity invites renegotiation.
  • Exhibitors face programming risk: Two Rambola listings in the same weekend confuse walk-in audiences.
  • No confirmed dates yet: Neither film has announced a release window, so the clash may resolve quietly.

The Value Question

For investors, the Rambola duplication is a reminder that Indian film IP is weakly protected at the title level. Producers spend crores on production and promotion but often treat title registration as paperwork. The prudent approach is to lock a title early, register it with the relevant association, and secure a public release date so the claim is visible.

For audiences, the practical takeaway is simpler. Same name does not mean same film. Check the director, the cast and the banner before buying a ticket, because in this instance the two Rambola projects are entirely separate creative efforts sharing nothing but a word.
