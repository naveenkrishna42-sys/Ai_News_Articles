Women With PMOS Have 4 Times Higher Risk Of Heart Disease: Lancet Study

📅 2026-07-22 📂 Health Original source ↗
Key points
  • Lancet study links PMOS to four times higher risk of heart disease.
  • One in five Indian women now lives with PMOS.
  • The condition was renamed from PCOS to PMOS to reflect metabolic aspects.

A major study published in The Lancet has found that women with Polycystic Ovary Syndrome, now renamed PMOS, face a four times higher risk of developing heart disease. The research underscores a growing health crisis, particularly in India, where one in five women is affected.

What the Study Found

The study, which analysed data from hundreds of thousands of women globally, revealed that PMOS is not just a reproductive disorder. It is a metabolic condition that significantly increases the likelihood of heart attacks and strokes, especially in young women.

Researchers found that the risk of cardiovascular events was four times higher in women with PMOS compared to those without. The findings were consistent across different age groups and body weights, suggesting the condition itself is a major independent risk factor.

Why the Name Changed from PCOS to PMOS

The Lancet study also explains the rationale behind the name change from PCOS (Polycystic Ovary Syndrome) to PMOS (Polycystic Ovary Syndrome with Metabolic Syndrome). The new name better captures the full scope of the condition, which includes metabolic disturbances like insulin resistance, high blood pressure, and abnormal cholesterol levels.

Experts argue that the old name focused too narrowly on the ovaries. The shift to PMOS aims to alert both doctors and patients to the broader health risks, particularly heart disease, that accompany the syndrome.

India's Growing Burden

India Today reported that one in five Indian women now lives with PMOS. This is a startling figure, given the country's large population. The rising incidence is linked to changing lifestyles, including poor diet, lack of exercise, and increasing stress levels.

The Indian Express noted that the condition is rising rapidly, with many cases going undiagnosed. Early detection and lifestyle changes can help manage PMOS and reduce the risk of heart disease.

What This Means for Women

For women diagnosed with PMOS, the study is a wake-up call. It is not just about fertility or irregular periods. The condition demands regular monitoring of heart health, including blood pressure, cholesterol, and blood sugar levels.

Doctors are now recommending that women with PMOS undergo cardiovascular risk assessments from a younger age. Simple lifestyle interventions, such as a balanced diet and regular exercise, can significantly lower the risk of heart disease.

One in eight women globally is affected by PMOS, according to the Lancet study. The condition is now recognised as a major public health issue that requires urgent attention from healthcare systems worldwide.

Going forward, researchers will be watching to see if the name change leads to better diagnosis and management of the condition. The hope is that more women will get the comprehensive care they need, not just for reproductive health, but for their hearts too.
