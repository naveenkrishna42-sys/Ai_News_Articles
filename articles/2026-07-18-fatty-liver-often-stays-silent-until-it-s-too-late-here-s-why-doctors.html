Fatty liver often stays silent until it's too late. Here's why doctors say you shouldn't ignore it

📅 2026-07-18 📂 Health Original source ↗
Key points
  • Fatty liver disease often shows no symptoms until it progresses to advanced stages.
  • The condition is increasingly diagnosed in young adults, linked to lifestyle factors.
  • Experts urge regular screening and lifestyle changes to prevent irreversible liver damage.

The silent threat to your liver

Fatty liver disease is earning a reputation as a quiet killer. Many people walk around with the condition for years without feeling a thing. By the time symptoms appear, the liver may already be significantly damaged.

Doctors across India are raising alarms. The disease is no longer limited to older adults or heavy drinkers. A growing number of young adults are being diagnosed with non-alcoholic fatty liver disease (NAFLD).

Gastroenterologist Dr Kalayarasan Raja has warned that this “silent liver disease” is on the rise in the 20-40 age group. He says the lack of early warning signs makes it especially dangerous.

Why fatty liver stays hidden

The liver is a resilient organ. It can endure considerable damage before showing distress. In the early stages of fatty liver, fat builds up in liver cells without causing inflammation or scarring. This phase often produces no symptoms at all.

Most people only discover they have fatty liver during routine health check-ups or scans for unrelated issues. By then, the condition may have moved to a more serious stage called non-alcoholic steatohepatitis (NASH), which involves inflammation and cell damage.

If left unchecked, NASH can progress to cirrhosis — permanent scarring of the liver — and eventually liver failure. The entire process can take years, but it often goes unnoticed until it is too late for simple interventions.

Early signs that are easy to miss

Even when symptoms do appear, they are vague and easy to dismiss. Fatigue, mild pain in the upper right side of the abdomen, and unexplained weight loss are common. Some people report a feeling of fullness or discomfort after eating.

Experts point out that these signs are often mistaken for everyday health problems or stress. The Times of India recently highlighted that many patients confuse early liver issues with general tiredness or digestive troubles.

Other warning signs include yellowing of the skin or eyes (jaundice), swelling in the legs or abdomen, and itchy skin. However, these usually appear only after significant liver damage has occurred.

Rising cases in young adults

India is seeing a worrying trend. More young professionals and college students are being diagnosed with fatty liver. Poor diet, sedentary lifestyles, and rising obesity rates are key drivers.

A study cited by the Indian Express notes that one in three urban Indians may have fatty liver. Many of them are under 40. The condition is also linked to diabetes, high cholesterol, and high blood pressure — all of which are becoming common in younger age groups.

Doctors say the pandemic worsened the problem. Lockdowns led to reduced physical activity, increased screen time, and unhealthy eating habits. For many, this created the perfect environment for fat to accumulate in the liver.

What can be done

The good news is that fatty liver is reversible if caught early. Weight loss of 5-10% can significantly reduce liver fat. A balanced diet low in processed foods and sugars helps. Regular exercise, even brisk walking for 30 minutes a day, makes a difference.

Experts also advise limiting alcohol consumption and avoiding unnecessary medications that stress the liver. Routine blood tests and liver ultrasounds can detect the condition before it becomes serious.

Dr Raja emphasises that young adults should not ignore persistent fatigue or abdominal discomfort. He recommends annual health check-ups that include liver function tests, especially for those with risk factors like obesity or diabetes.

As fatty liver continues to spread silently, the message from doctors is clear: pay attention to your liver before it forces you to. Early detection and simple lifestyle changes can stop the disease in its tracks. The next step for health authorities is to spread awareness and make screening more accessible, particularly for the younger population.
