Former RBI Governor Urges Incentives to Boost Small Finance Banks

  • SFBs are seen as key channel for financial inclusion in rural and semi-urban India
  • Incentives could address capital cost, branch expansion and priority sector lending constraints

Push for More Small Finance Banks

A former RBI Governor has called for targeted incentives to create more small finance banks in India. The call reflects concern that credit penetration remains uneven despite rapid growth in formal banking. Small finance banks are positioned as a bridge between traditional commercial banks and underserved borrowers.

The argument centres on expanding last-mile access without overburdening larger lenders with social mandates. Proponents say fresh SFB licences can increase competition in districts with thin banking presence.

What Incentives Could Look Like

Policy suggestions in recent industry discussions focus on lowering the cost of doing business for new entrants. Capital adequacy pressure and high operating costs are cited as barriers to scaling SFBs profitably.

Capital and Tax Relief

Reduced tax on priority sector lending income and concessional refinance from RBI could improve return on equity for new SFBs. Lower withholding or a phased corporate tax rate for the first five years would help build reserves.

Access to long term funding at preferential rates would also reduce dependence on high cost deposits. This matters for lending to micro enterprises where ticket sizes are small and monitoring costs are high.

Regulatory Forbearance

Relaxed branch licensing norms in Tier 3 to Tier 6 centres can accelerate physical expansion. SFBs already face a 75 percent priority sector asset requirement, stricter than most commercial banks.

A calibrated reduction in minimum CASA targets in initial years, or a gradual glide path for capital raising, would give new players time to build deposit franchises.

Sector Snapshot and Performance

Small finance banks operate under RBI guidelines with a clear mandate to serve unbanked and underbanked segments. The model combines deposit taking with micro credit, small and medium enterprise lending and financial inclusion products.

Growth has been steady, with assets and loan books expanding as digital channels reduce unit costs. The sector remains capital intensive and needs patient funding to reach scale.

Real-World Implications for Borrowers and Investors

More SFBs can translate into faster credit appraisal for small businesses and farmers. Local decision making reduces paperwork and improves turnaround time for loans under Rs 10 lakh.

For investors, a larger SFB universe offers diversified exposure to financial inclusion themes. The key risk remains asset quality in segments with volatile cash flows.

  • Pros: Wider geographic reach, tailored products for micro enterprises, stronger competition for rural deposits
  • Cons: Thin margins, high operating cost per account, dependence on policy support for viability
