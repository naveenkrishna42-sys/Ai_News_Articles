Trump warns Iran: US controls your money, can strike with 'great force'

📅 2026-08-12 📂 Analysis Original source ↗
Key points
  • Trump says US controls Iran's money and Tehran is 'playing tricks' during Hormuz negotiations.
  • US attacks vessel in Gulf of Oman over alleged Iran blockade violation, raising military tensions.
  • Trump demands Iran pay compensation for deaths, flipping the table on Tehran's war damages claim.
  • Strait of Hormuz deal remains elusive as both sides set new conditions, analysts say.

Escalating rhetoric over Tehran's finances

US President Donald Trump has escalated his war of words with Iran, asserting that Washington holds the key to Tehran's financial resources and warning that the US military can strike with 'great force' if necessary. The remarks, made amid ongoing negotiations over the Strait of Hormuz, signal a hardening of the American position just as diplomatic efforts appear to be faltering.

'We control Iran's money,' Trump said, accusing Tehran of 'playing tricks' during the talks. The president's blunt language reflects a growing frustration within the White House over what it sees as Iranian intransigence, even as both sides continue to engage through intermediaries.

Gulf of Oman incident raises stakes

The tension is not merely rhetorical. The Times of India reports that US forces attacked a vessel in the Gulf of Oman over an alleged violation of the Iran blockade. The incident marks a significant uptick in military activity in the region, raising fears of a broader confrontation that could disrupt global oil supplies.

Details of the attack remain murky, with officials not yet confirming the vessel's nationality or the extent of damage. However, the strike underscores the fragile nature of the current standoff, where a single miscalculation could spiral into open conflict.

Compensation demands and diplomatic deadlock

In a surprising twist, Trump has demanded that Iran pay compensation for deaths, effectively reversing Tehran's earlier claim seeking war damages from the US. According to NDTV, the president 'flipped the table' by sending his own bill to Tehran, a move that analysts say is more about political posturing than legal reality.

The Strait of Hormuz deal remains elusive, with both sides setting new conditions that seem designed to extract maximum concessions. Al Jazeera reports that Iran and the US have introduced fresh demands during the talks, complicating any path to a negotiated settlement.

Why this matters

The stakes could hardly be higher. The Strait of Hormuz is a critical chokepoint for global energy trade, with roughly a fifth of the world's oil passing through its waters. Any sustained disruption would send shockwaves through already volatile energy markets, impacting economies from New Delhi to New York.

For India, the implications are particularly acute. The country imports a significant portion of its crude from the Gulf region, and a prolonged crisis could inflate fuel prices and stoke domestic inflation. New Delhi has historically walked a fine line between its strategic ties with Washington and its energy dependence on the Gulf, a balancing act that becomes more precarious with each escalation.

The broader regional dynamics also weigh heavily. Iran's proxies across the Middle East could respond to any US strike, opening multiple fronts and drawing in other players. The Gulf of Oman incident, while limited in scope, demonstrates how quickly the situation can move from rhetoric to action.

What to watch

Diplomatic channels remain open, but the window for a breakthrough appears to be narrowing. The coming weeks will reveal whether Trump's hardline approach forces Tehran back to the table or pushes it further into defiance. Key signals include the frequency of naval patrols in the Gulf, any new sanctions designations, and whether Iran resumes higher-grade uranium enrichment as a counter-pressure tactic.

For now, the world watches a high-stakes game of chicken, with the US insisting it holds the financial lever and Iran testing the limits of that claim. The next move, whether in a negotiating room or on the high seas, will determine whether this crisis cools or ignites.
