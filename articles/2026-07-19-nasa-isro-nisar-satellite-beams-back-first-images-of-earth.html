NASA-ISRO NISAR Satellite Beams Back First Images of Earth

📅 2026-07-19 📂 Space Original source ↗
Key points
  • NISAR satellite captures first images of Earth after launch.
  • Joint mission between NASA and ISRO aims to monitor Earth's surface changes.
  • Radar imaging reveals details of land, ice, and forests.
  • Data will help track climate change, natural disasters, and resource management.
  • Full science operations expected to begin in coming months.

The NISAR satellite, a joint venture between NASA and the Indian Space Research Organisation (ISRO), has sent back its first images of Earth. The milestone marks a significant step for a mission designed to observe the planet's surface in unprecedented detail.

The satellite, launched earlier this year, carries two radar systems — an L-band radar from NASA and an S-band radar from ISRO. Together, they can see through clouds and darkness, capturing changes in land, ice, and forests.

First Light for NISAR's Radars

The initial images show regions including parts of the Himalayas, the Amazon rainforest, and Antarctica. Scientists say the data confirms the satellite's instruments are functioning as expected.

One image reveals the rugged terrain of the Himalayan range, while another captures the dense canopy of the Amazon. A third image shows the icy expanse of Antarctica's coastline, where ice shelf movements are visible.

These early returns are part of the satellite's commissioning phase. Engineers are calibrating the instruments before full science operations begin.

Monitoring a Changing Planet

NISAR's primary goal is to observe Earth's surface every 12 days. The satellite will track subtle shifts in the ground, changes in ice sheets, and the health of forests.

Officials from both space agencies have highlighted the mission's potential for disaster management. NISAR's radar can detect ground deformation before earthquakes and landslides, and monitor floods and volcanic activity.

The data will also help scientists understand carbon cycles by measuring forest biomass. This is critical for climate change research.

A Decade in the Making

Conceived over a decade ago, NISAR represents one of the most ambitious Earth-observation collaborations between the US and India. NASA provided the L-band radar, the high-speed communication system, and the payload data subsystem. ISRO contributed the S-band radar, the spacecraft bus, and launch services.

The satellite was launched from the Satish Dhawan Space Centre in Sriharikota, India, aboard an ISRO GSLV Mark II rocket.

Both agencies have described the partnership as a model for future international scientific missions. The total cost of the mission is estimated at over USD 1.5 billion.

What Comes Next

With the first images in hand, the focus now shifts to completing calibration and validation. Scientists expect the satellite to begin routine science observations within the next two months.

The data will be made publicly available through NASA's Earthdata portal and ISRO's Bhuvan platform. Researchers worldwide are preparing to use it for studies ranging from groundwater depletion to glacier dynamics.

NISAR's first images are a promise of what is to come — a steady stream of detailed observations that will help track a planet in flux.
