Possible First Second-Generation Planet Found Around White Dwarf

✍️ By TIVRA News Science & Health Desk 📅 2026-10-06 📂 Science Original source ↗
Key points
  • First candidate of a second-generation planet detected via infrared excess and transit-like signals around a white dwarf.
  • The planet likely formed from stellar debris after the host star's asymptotic giant branch phase, challenging traditional planet formation models.
  • Preliminary estimates place the object at a close-in orbit with a period of roughly 10 hours and a size comparable to a gas giant.
  • Discovery opens a new pathway for studying planetary survival and recycling in post‑main‑sequence systems.

Discovery Overview

In early October 2026, astronomers reported a candidate object transiting a faint white dwarf located roughly 80 light‑years from the Solar System. The signal appeared as a periodic dip in the star's brightness, accompanied by an excess of infrared emission that suggests warm dust or gas in close proximity to the stellar remnant.

What Is a Second‑Generation Planet?

Traditional planet formation occurs within the protoplanetary disk of a young star. A second‑generation planet, sometimes called a Phoenix planet, would instead coalesce from material ejected during the star's asymptotic giant branch phase. As the star sheds its outer envelope, the expelled gas and dust can re‑accrete into a new planetary body orbiting the exposed white dwarf core.

Observational Evidence and Detection Techniques

The candidate was first identified in archival data from the Hubble Space Telescope's Wide Field Camera 3, where a repeating dimming pattern with a period near 10 hours stood out. Follow‑up observations using ground‑based infrared telescopes detected a modest flux excess at wavelengths around 4–5 micrometres, consistent with a heated circumstellar disk or a hot planetary atmosphere.

Physical Characteristics of the Candidate

Preliminary modelling of the transit depth and duration yields a radius estimate of about 1.1 times that of Jupiter, assuming a grazing orbit. The inferred orbital distance is roughly 0.01 AU, placing the object well within the Roche limit of a typical white dwarf, which hints at either a low‑density composition or ongoing tidal disruption.

Implications for Planet Formation and Future Research

If confirmed, this discovery would demonstrate that planetary systems can survive, or even be reborn, after the host star's death. It would also provide a natural laboratory for studying the chemistry of material processed through stellar nucleosynthesis and later re‑incorporated into a new world.
