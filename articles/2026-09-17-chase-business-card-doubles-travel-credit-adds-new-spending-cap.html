Chase Business Card Overhaul: What Changed?

Chase has announced a significant update to its flagship business credit card, doubling the annual travel credit from $300 to $600 and introducing a new spending cap for bonus earnings. According to Forbes, the changes aim to attract high-spending business owners while maintaining profitability amid shifting travel patterns. The doubled travel credit applies to a broad range of travel purchases, including airfare, hotels, and car rentals, booked directly with the provider or through Chase Travel. The new spending cap, set at $150,000 per calendar year for bonus category earnings, means that after this threshold, the earn rate drops to 1 point per dollar. This move mirrors trends seen in premium consumer cards, where issuers balance generous rewards with risk management.

Technical Specifications and Pricing Context

To assess the real-world impact, consider the card's updated specifications. The annual fee remains unchanged at $595, but the effective cost after the travel credit is now negative $5, making it a compelling proposition for frequent travellers. The card continues to offer 3 points per dollar on a broad range of business categories, including advertising, shipping, and travel, up to the new cap. Beyond the cap, the earn rate falls to 1 point per dollar. Points are worth 1.25 cents each when redeemed for travel through Chase Ultimate Rewards, or 1 cent for cash back. For a business spending $200,000 annually in bonus categories, the cap reduces potential rewards by 25,000 points compared to the previous uncapped structure. However, the doubled travel credit offsets this for those who maximise it.

Real-World Value and Buyer Implications

For a small business owner spending $10,000 per month in bonus categories, the annual spend of $120,000 stays under the cap, yielding 360,000 points. At 1.25 cents per point, that's $4,500 in travel value, plus the $600 travel credit, minus the $595 annual fee, resulting in a net gain of $4,505. Previously, with the same spend and a $300 credit, the net gain was $4,205. The doubled credit adds $300 in value, but the cap introduces a potential loss for those exceeding $150,000. A business spending $250,000 annually would earn 450,000 points on the first $150,000 and 100,000 points on the remaining $100,000, totalling 550,000 points. Without the cap, they would have earned 750,000 points, a difference of 200,000 points or $2,500 in travel value. Thus, the card is less attractive for very high spenders.

Pros and Cons

  • Pros: Doubled travel credit increases net value for frequent travellers; no change in annual fee; strong earn rate on business categories; flexible redemption options including transfer partners.
  • Cons: New spending cap reduces rewards for high-volume spenders; travel credit requires enrollment and may be split into monthly or semi-annual increments, adding complexity; effective for those who can maximise the credit.

Competitive Benchmarking and Final Verdict

Compared to the American Express Business Platinum, which offers a $695 annual fee and a $200 airline credit, the Chase card now provides a higher travel credit and a lower effective annual fee. However, Amex offers 1.5 points per dollar on purchases over $5,000 and no spending cap. The Chase card's 3 points per dollar on a wide range of business categories remains a key differentiator, but the cap may push some high spenders to Amex. For businesses spending under $150,000 annually in bonus categories, the Chase card is now more valuable than before. For those over, the calculus changes. The card remains a top choice for small to mid-sized businesses that can maximise the travel credit and stay under the cap. The changes take effect for cardholders on their next renewal after 1 January 2027, according to Forbes. Business owners should evaluate their annual spending patterns to determine if the doubled credit outweighs the cap's limitations.
