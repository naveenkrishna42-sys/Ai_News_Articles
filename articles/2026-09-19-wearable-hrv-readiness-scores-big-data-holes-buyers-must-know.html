The Hidden Flaws in Wearable HRV and Readiness Scores

Wearable devices from Apple, Garmin, Whoop, and Oura increasingly push heart rate variability (HRV) and readiness scores as key health indicators. These metrics promise to tell you whether you are recovered, stressed, or ready to train. But according to a recent analysis by Ground Truths, led by cardiologist Eric Topol, the underlying data often has significant holes. For buyers spending Rs 20,000 to Rs 50,000 on a smartwatch or fitness band, understanding these limitations is critical before trusting any readiness score.

The core issue: most wearables measure HRV using optical photoplethysmography (PPG) rather than a medical-grade ECG. PPG is sensitive to motion, skin tone, and poor blood flow, leading to inaccurate readings. A 2023 study in npj Digital Medicine found that PPG-based HRV deviated by up to 30% compared to ECG during activity. Readiness scores built on such data can misguide training and recovery decisions.

How HRV and Readiness Scores Are Calculated

HRV is the variation in time between heartbeats. Higher variability generally indicates better cardiovascular fitness and recovery. Wearables calculate HRV from PPG signals, often during sleep or rest. Readiness scores then combine HRV with heart rate, sleep, and activity data using proprietary algorithms.

The problem is that each brand uses its own formula, and none publish peer-reviewed validation. Whoop's recovery score, for instance, weights HRV heavily, while Garmin's Body Battery emphasises stress and activity. This means a "recovery score" of 80 on one device may equate to 50 on another. Without standardisation, these scores are not comparable and can create false confidence or unnecessary alarm.

Real-World Buyer Implications and Practical Benchmarks

For athletes and health-conscious consumers, relying solely on readiness scores can lead to overtraining or undertraining. A low score might prompt rest when the body is actually ready, while a high score could mask underlying fatigue. Experts recommend using HRV trends over 7–14 days rather than daily scores. Also, compare against subjective feel and performance.

From a buyer's perspective, devices with ECG capability (like Withings ScanWatch or Apple Watch Series 9) offer more reliable HRV data, though even these have limitations during movement. Chest straps (Polar H10, Garmin HRM-Pro) remain the gold standard for accuracy, but they lack the convenience of wrist-based wearables. For₹ 30,000, you can get a smartwatch with HRV, but expect to pay₹ 50,000+ for validated ECG features.

Pros and Cons of Current Wearable HRV Features

  • Pros: Convenient continuous monitoring; trend detection over time; integration with training apps; motivation for healthy habits.
  • Cons: PPG inaccuracy during motion; proprietary scores lack validation; no standardisation across brands; potential for anxiety from false alerts; high cost for reliable ECG.

What Buyers Should Look For

Prioritise devices that provide raw HRV data alongside scores. Check for published validation studies, ideally independent. If you have a heart condition, consult a doctor before relying on any wearable. For general wellness, use readiness scores as one of many signals, not the sole guide. Remember that no wearable replaces clinical assessment.

As the market grows, pressure for transparency may increase. Until then, buyers must navigate the holes in HRV and readiness scores with informed scepticism.
