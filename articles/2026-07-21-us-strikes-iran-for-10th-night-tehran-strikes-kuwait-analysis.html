US Strikes Iran for 10th Night, Tehran Strikes Kuwait: Analysis

📅 2026-07-21 📂 Analysis Original source ↗
Key points
  • US military strikes on Iranian targets have continued for a tenth consecutive night.
  • Tehran has retaliated by attacking Kuwait, widening the conflict's geography.
  • No official casualty figures or diplomatic resolution have been announced.

Context: A Decade of Air Campaigns

The United States has launched airstrikes against Iran for the tenth night in a row, marking one of the most sustained aerial campaigns in the region since the 2003 invasion of Iraq. The strikes, which began after the deaths of US service members in an earlier attack, have now expanded in scope and intensity.

Tehran, in a significant escalation, has struck back at Kuwait. This is the first time in the current cycle of violence that Iran has directly targeted a Gulf Arab state, shifting the conflict from a bilateral US-Iran confrontation to a broader regional crisis. Kuwait has not yet officially confirmed the extent of the damage or casualties.

President Donald Trump warned earlier this week of strong retaliation for the deaths of American soldiers, a promise the current bombing campaign appears to fulfill. However, the sustained nature of the strikes—ten nights without a pause—suggests a campaign designed to degrade Iran’s military infrastructure rather than a punitive, one-off response.

Background: The Path to Ten Nights of Bombing

The immediate trigger for the US strikes was a drone or missile attack on a US base in Iraq that killed multiple American service members. The White House blamed Iran-backed militias for the attack, though Tehran denied direct involvement. Within hours, US warplanes began striking targets inside Iran, including air defense systems, missile storage sites, and command-and-control centers.

Iran responded with its own missile and drone attacks on US positions in Iraq and, as seen in the past 24 hours, on Kuwaiti soil. The tit-for-tat exchanges have grown in intensity with each passing night. Unlike previous US-Iran confrontations—such as the January 2020 strike that killed Qassem Soleimani, which was a single, high-profile operation—this campaign is characterized by its relentless, nightly rhythm.

Regional powers have reacted with alarm. Gulf states, including Saudi Arabia and the United Arab Emirates, have called for restraint, but their public statements remain cautious. Iran, meanwhile, has hit out at its Gulf neighbours, accusing them of allowing US forces to use their territory for staging attacks. Kuwait, which hosts US military personnel and equipment, now finds itself a direct target.

Why It Matters: The Risk of a Regional War

The US bombing campaign on Iran entering its tenth night signals a fundamental shift in American military doctrine in the Middle East. The Obama administration relied on proxy wars and special operations; the Trump administration has opted for direct, sustained conventional strikes against a sovereign state. This approach carries immense risks.

First, the attacks on Kuwait could trigger a chain reaction. If Kuwait retaliates against Iran, or if the US uses the attack as a justification to escalate further, the conflict could draw in other Gulf states. The Strait of Hormuz, through which about a fifth of the world’s oil passes, could become a flashpoint. Global oil prices have already spiked, and a prolonged conflict could tip the world economy into a recession.

Second, the lack of a diplomatic off-ramp is worrying. Neither Washington nor Tehran has publicly outlined conditions for a ceasefire. The US has not declared an end goal beyond “degrading Iran’s ability to attack US forces.” Iran, for its part, has vowed to continue retaliating until all US forces leave the region. This is a recipe for a war of attrition, not a negotiated settlement.

Third, the human cost remains unclear. Officials have not yet confirmed casualty figures on either side. Independent reporting from inside Iran is limited due to strict media controls and the blackout of internet services in several provinces. The true scale of destruction—both military and civilian—may only emerge weeks from now.

The Domestic Angle for Washington

For President Trump, the strikes serve multiple purposes. They fulfill a promise of tough retaliation, they project strength ahead of the 2026 midterm elections, and they distract from domestic political troubles. However, a prolonged conflict with no clear victory could erode public support. The US public has historically been wary of another Middle Eastern war, and the nightly news footage of burning Iranian cities may eventually wear down that support.

In Tehran, the leadership faces a similar calculus. The Iranian regime has used the strikes to rally nationalist sentiment and crack down on internal dissent. But the cost of rebuilding damaged infrastructure and the economic toll of sanctions will test the government’s resilience.

What to Watch: The Next 48 Hours

The next two days will be critical. If the US announces a pause or a de-escalation, it could signal that the campaign has achieved its objectives. If the strikes continue without any diplomatic movement, the conflict will likely expand further. The world will be watching Kuwait’s response, oil price movements, and any surprise announcements from the White House or the Supreme Leader’s office.
