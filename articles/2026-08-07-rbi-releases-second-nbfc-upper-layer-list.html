RBI Releases Second NBFC Upper Layer List

📅 2026-08-07 📂 Banking Original source ↗
Key points
  • RBI released the second list of NBFCs in the upper layer
  • New list includes additional large non-bank lenders
  • Stricter regulations apply to upper-layer NBFCs
  • Move aims to strengthen oversight of shadow banking

The Reserve Bank of India (RBI) on Thursday released its second list of non-banking financial companies (NBFCs) categorised in the upper layer, a move that brings tighter regulatory scrutiny to some of the country's largest shadow banks. The list, part of the central bank's scale-based regulation framework, identifies lenders whose size and risk profile warrant enhanced oversight.

While the RBI did not immediately disclose the full names of all entities on the list, sources indicated that several prominent NBFCs, including those in the infrastructure and housing finance space, have been added. The central bank had first published such a list in September 2021, and this update reflects changes in the sector's composition and growth.

What the upper layer means

Under the scale-based regulation (SBR) introduced in 2021, NBFCs are classified into four layers: base, middle, upper, and top. The upper layer includes NBFCs that are systemically important and require additional regulatory attention. These firms must comply with stricter norms on capital adequacy, governance, and exposure limits, among others.

For entities in this layer, the RBI mandates a higher common equity tier-1 capital ratio and imposes limits on inter-connected lending. They are also required to maintain a board-approved policy for stress testing and have a robust internal framework for risk management.

Impact on the sector

The inclusion in the upper layer is not a penalty but a recognition of systemic significance. However, it does mean greater compliance costs and operational adjustments for the affected companies. Industry analysts note that while the additional regulations may strain resources in the short term, they ultimately contribute to a more resilient financial system.

The RBI's move is part of a broader effort to tighten oversight of the shadow banking sector, which has grown rapidly over the past decade. The central bank has been particularly cautious since the collapse of IL&FS in 2018, which exposed vulnerabilities in the NBFC space and prompted a liquidity crunch.

List composition and next steps

The second list is expected to include a mix of existing names from the first list, with some additions and possibly a few deletions. Entities that no longer meet the threshold criteria may be moved to the middle layer, while others that have grown in size could be newly included.

Analysts expect the RBI to review the list periodically, possibly annually, to reflect changes in the sector's dynamics. The central bank has also indicated that the top layer, which would contain the largest and most complex NBFCs, will be identified at a later stage.

For now, the affected NBFCs will have to align their systems and processes to meet the enhanced regulatory requirements. The RBI has given them a transitional period, but the clock is ticking for compliance.

As the financial sector evolves, the RBI's layered approach ensures that oversight keeps pace with risk. Market participants will be watching closely to see which entities make the cut in the next review, and how the upper-layer NBFCs adapt to their new regulatory reality.
