Narikurava children become puppet show commentators on environment in Chennai

📅 2026-07-19 📂 Chennai Original source ↗
Key points
  • Children from the Narikurava community in Pallavaram are involved as commentators in a puppetry production.
  • The production focuses on environmental degradation, using traditional puppet art to convey the message.
  • The initiative engages the community in a creative dialogue about ecological issues.

Voices from the community

In Pallavaram, a Chennai suburb, children from the Narikurava community are stepping into an unusual role. They are serving as commentators in a puppetry production that tackles environmental degradation. The initiative brings together traditional art and contemporary concerns.

The children, who belong to a historically nomadic tribe, are not just performers. They are narrators who guide the audience through the story, explaining the impact of pollution and deforestation on their surroundings.

Art as activism

Puppetry has long been a medium for social messaging in India. This production uses that tradition to highlight how environmental damage affects marginalised communities directly. The children’s involvement adds authenticity and urgency to the narrative.

The production does not merely entertain. It educates viewers about the link between ecological health and human well-being. The choice of children as commentators also underscores the stakes for the next generation.

Community engagement

The Narikurava community has often been on the fringes of mainstream society. By placing their children at the centre of a creative project, the initiative fosters a sense of ownership and pride. It also opens a channel for dialogue between the community and urban audiences.

Local organisers worked closely with the children to ensure their voices were authentic. The production does not script their commentary rigidly. Instead, it allows them to speak from personal experience, making the message more powerful.

Broader implications

Environmental degradation does not affect all communities equally. The Narikurava children’s participation highlights how pollution and climate change impact vulnerable groups first. Their commentary brings this reality to the forefront without academic jargon.

The production is a reminder that solutions to ecological crises must include those most affected. It also shows how traditional art forms can be repurposed for modern advocacy.

What comes next for this project is not yet clear. Organisers have not announced further performances or expansions. But the model of involving community children as commentators could inspire similar efforts elsewhere in the country.
