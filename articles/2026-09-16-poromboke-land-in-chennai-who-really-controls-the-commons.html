Poromboke Land in Chennai: Who Really Controls the Commons?

✍️ By TIVRA News Editorial Team 📅 2026-09-16 📂 Chennai Original source ↗
Key points
  • Poromboke land is government-owned 'commons' land and cannot be legally bought, sold or patta-issued.
  • Encroachment and weak enforcement, not paperwork, often decide real-world control of these parcels.
  • Buyers must verify land classification via EC, FMB sketch and CMDA/DTCP records before any transaction.

What Exactly Is Poromboke Land?

Poromboke (from the Tamil 'porombokku') refers to land reserved for public or community use, including water bodies, tank beds, grazing grounds, canal margins, roads and cart tracks. Under Tamil Nadu revenue law, this land vests with the government and is recorded as 'Government Poromboke' in village accounts.

The critical point for any buyer: poromboke land is not private property. It cannot be legally sold, bought, mortgaged or converted into a patta. Yet in and around Chennai, thousands of acres have been occupied, fenced and even built upon, creating a parallel market where transactions happen despite having no lawful title.

Why the Chennai Commons Matter

Chennai's water security depends directly on these commons. Eri (tank) beds, feeder channels and marshlands absorb monsoon runoff and recharge groundwater. When they are built over, flooding worsens and recharge falls. The 2015 and 2023 flood events exposed how encroached channels and choked wetlands turn routine rain into a crisis.

Beyond water, poromboke land hosts public amenities, burial grounds, playgrounds and road margins. Once occupied, reclaiming it requires years of litigation, eviction drives and political will. The commons are therefore not an abstract legal category; they are the physical infrastructure of urban resilience.

Encroachment, Regularisation and the Record Problem

Tamil Nadu has periodically attempted regularisation of encroachments on government land, often excluding water bodies and objectionable categories. Each such scheme creates fresh incentives to occupy land and wait for a future amnesty, while genuine public-use parcels shrink.

The deeper issue is record quality. Village accounts, 'A' register entries, FMB sketches and town survey records frequently disagree. A buyer relying only on a parent document and a tax receipt can end up holding a plot that is legally poromboke, with no enforceable title.

What This Means for Buyers and Residents

If a plot is priced 30-50% below the prevailing locality rate and the seller only offers a power of attorney, a tax receipt or a village-level 'certificate', treat it as a poromboke risk. Banks rarely finance such purchases, and resale becomes nearly impossible once the classification is flagged.

Residents already living on poromboke land face a different problem: they may receive basic services for years, then face eviction or demolition during a widening or restoration project. There is no clean title to fall back on, and compensation, if any, is discretionary.

Real-World Highlights

  • Verification is everything: A 30-year EC plus FMB sketch and CMDA/DTCP approval catches most poromboke traps before money changes hands.
  • Price anomaly is a warning: Deep discounts in Chennai's fringe usually signal a classification problem, not a bargain.
  • Water bodies are non-negotiable: Even regularisation schemes routinely exclude eri and channel land, so 'waiting for patta' rarely works.
  • Litigation is slow: Reclaiming occupied commons can take a decade or more, which is why prevention beats cure.
  • Civic cost is shared: Every built-over channel raises flood risk and water stress for the wider neighbourhood.

Who Should Shape the Commons?

The honest answer is that control of poromboke land in Chennai is currently shaped by three forces: documented government ownership, de facto occupation, and the slow machinery of enforcement and courts. Buyers, residents and civic groups cannot change the law, but they can refuse to participate in illegal transactions and demand transparent, digitised land records.

A commons that is mapped, published and enforced serves everyone. A commons that exists only on paper serves whoever occupies it first. Chennai's next flood, water shortage or eviction drive will make that distinction very public.
