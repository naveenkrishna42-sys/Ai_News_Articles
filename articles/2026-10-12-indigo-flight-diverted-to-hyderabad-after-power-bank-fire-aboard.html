What triggered the diversion on the Chennai-bound IndiGo flight

A Chennai-bound IndiGo flight was reportedly diverted to Hyderabad after a passenger’s power bank caught fire onboard. According to the report from The New Indian Express, the incident led to immediate crew response and operational changes to manage safety and risk.

While the aviation industry routinely handles small incidents with established checklists, a lithium-ion device fire is treated differently from many other onboard hazards. The reason is simple: damaged or high-heat lithium batteries can vent burning electrolyte, producing smoke and thermal runaway that becomes harder to contain over time.

For travellers, the key takeaway is that a power bank is not “just a gadget” in flight. It’s a high-energy electrical source that must stay within safe temperature and physical integrity limits—both of which can be compromised by poor-quality cells, physical damage, counterfeit products, or extreme charging practices.

Why power banks are high-risk in aircraft cabins

Power banks rely on lithium-ion cells, typically enclosed in a protective pack with circuitry for charging control. However, several failure modes can still occur: internal short circuits, charging-management faults, or cell degradation after repeated stress cycles.

When a power bank goes into thermal runaway, it can generate intense heat and smoke. In a cabin, the priorities become rapid isolation, air management, and landing at the earliest safe opportunity. Diversions are therefore not a “delay”—they are a risk-minimization decision.

From a buyer’s perspective, quality matters. Certified cells and robust battery management systems (BMS) reduce the odds of runaway. Equally important are safety design choices: features like temperature monitoring, overcurrent cutoffs, and protection against overcharging.

What passengers and buyers should do to reduce risk

For passengers, the practical risk controls start before boarding. Inspect the power bank for swelling, dents, frayed cables, or loose ports—any of these are red flags. Avoid using devices that feel hot while charging, even for short periods.

On the ground and in the air, use only compatible chargers and cables. Mismatched fast-charging setups can push the power bank’s electronics beyond expected operating conditions. If your airline’s guidance allows power banks in hand baggage, store them securely to prevent accidental impacts.

For commercial buyers—frequent flyers, corporate travel departments, and procurement teams—the buying standard should be higher than “it charges my phone.” Procurement checks should include brand reputation, regulatory certifications, warranty terms, and transparent battery specifications. Prefer products that clearly state capacity in Wh (watt-hours) and adhere to recognized safety testing claims.

  • Do: buy from established brands and verify certifications and warranty coverage.
  • Do: use original or certified cables; avoid “no-name” fast-charging accessories.
  • Don’t: carry power banks that are swollen, cracked, or show overheating during charging.
  • Don’t: charge when the device is in a damaged case or exposed to high heat (direct sun, closed bags).

Safety signals to watch during travel

Even high-quality power banks can fail, but the warning signs are often visible. Unusual odour, sudden increase in surface heat, clicking sounds from the pack, or rapid smoke from charging are treated as immediate hazards by cabin crew.

When such signals appear, passengers should not attempt improvisations. Crew procedures are designed around preventing escalation and managing smoke exposure. The best action for travellers is to alert staff promptly so containment and response can begin without delay.

For frequent travellers, it also helps to standardize your kit. Carry one reliable power bank rather than multiple unverified ones; the fewer “unknown” devices you use, the lower your safety variability across trips.

Operational impact: why diversions happen quickly

When reports like this emerge, they also remind travellers that aircraft systems are built with safety buffers. A power bank fire in the cabin triggers immediate threat assessment: smoke management, isolating the device, and coordinating with ground authorities for the fastest safe diversion.

For airlines, the diversion isn’t just about landing—it’s about ensuring passengers can be managed safely and that the incident device can be handled under appropriate procedures after landing. For passengers, the disruption is usually temporary, but the underlying safety logic is permanent: lithium battery incidents demand decisive action.

Whether you travel once a year or weekly, this is a procurement and usage lesson. Choosing a certified, well-designed power bank—and using it responsibly—reduces risk for both you and fellow passengers.
