India’s Defence Chief Warns of Pakistan’s Space Alliance with China, Turkey

✍️ By TIVRA News Editorial Desk 📅 2026-11-28 📂 India Original source ↗
Key points
  • Pakistan’s reliance on Chinese launches heightens strategic risk
  • Turkey’s high‑resolution satellites expand Pakistan’s surveillance reach
  • India must accelerate launch vehicle and satellite R&D to keep pace

Strategic Context of Pakistan’s Space Collaboration

General S. M. Subramani, the Chief of Defence Staff, has publicly warned that Pakistan’s expanding space programme—bolstered by Chinese launch vehicles and Turkish satellite technology—poses a tangible threat to India’s security architecture. The partnership enables Pakistan to acquire timely imagery, navigation data and early‑warning signals without developing its own launch capability.

Current Pakistani Satellite Assets

Pakistan’s Space & Upper Atmosphere Research Commission (SUPARCO) operates four operational satellites: Badr‑1, Badr‑2, Badr‑3 (in development) and the high‑resolution imaging satellite Pakistan‑Imaging Satellite (PIS). All are launched via Chinese Long March rockets, with the latest Badr‑3 slated for a Long March 3B launch in late 2026.

Implications for India’s Space and Defence Ecosystem

Pakistan’s use of Chinese launchers gives it a cost advantage: a Long March 3B launch is estimated at 30 M USD, significantly lower than India’s PSLV. This enables rapid satellite deployment, tightening the timeline between intelligence requirement and data acquisition.

Turkey’s TÜRKSAT 5A, launched on a Falcon 9, offers 4‑km resolution imagery, comparable to India’s own imaging satellites but with more frequent revisit times. The cross‑border cooperation could allow Pakistan to integrate Turkish imaging feeds with Chinese telemetry, creating a continuous surveillance loop over Indian territory.

India’s Counter‑Measures and R&D Roadmap

India’s current satellite constellation—primarily NAVIC for navigation and a handful of imaging satellites—lacks the breadth of coverage needed for real‑time battlefield awareness. To mitigate the asymmetric advantage, the Defence Ministry is accelerating the GSLV‑MkIII+ program, targeting a 4,000‑kg payload and a launch cost reduction to 70 M USD by 2028.

Parallel investment is directed at indigenous launch vehicle engines: the indigenous liquid propulsion system (ILPS) aims to replace the Russian RD‑107 engines on PSLV, potentially cutting launch costs by 15‑20 %. Additionally, a national space command is under formation to centralise satellite data fusion and space‑domain awareness.

Key Real‑World Highlights

  • Cost Efficiency: Pakistan’s reliance on Chinese Long March rockets allows launch costs of ~30 M USD, undercutting India’s PSLV by 10‑15 M USD.
  • Rapid Deployment: Chinese launch schedules can be expedited to a few months, versus India’s 6‑12‑month window for PSLV and GSLV missions.
  • High‑Resolution Imaging: Turkish TÜRKSAT 5A offers 4‑km resolution, matching India’s own imaging satellites but with higher revisit frequency.
  • Strategic Autonomy Gap: India’s limited launch fleet (three PSLVs per year) restricts timely response to emerging threats.
  • Defense Budget Allocation: The Defence Ministry’s 2026‑27 budget earmarks 25 % of the space programme for indigenous launch vehicle R&D, up from 15 % in 2024.

Conclusion: Balancing Space Capability with National Security

Pakistan’s partnership with China and Turkey is not merely a commercial arrangement; it is a strategic capability that enhances surveillance, navigation and early‑warning systems. India must match this pace by scaling its launch vehicle fleet, reducing dependency on foreign launch services, and expanding its satellite constellation to ensure parity and resilience in the space domain.
