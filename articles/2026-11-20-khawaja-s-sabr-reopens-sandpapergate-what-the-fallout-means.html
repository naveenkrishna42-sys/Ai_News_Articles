Khawaja's 'Sabr' Reopens Sandpapergate: What the Fallout Means

A Memoir That Refuses to Let 2018 Rest

Usman Khawaja's autobiography 'Sabr' has dragged the Cape Town ball-tampering scandal back into the mainstream eight years after Cameron Bancroft was filmed rubbing a foreign object on

the ball at Newlands during the third Test against South Africa in March 2018. Khawaja, then a senior member of the Australian side, has spent much of the intervening period reflecting on what he describes as a rupture in the culture of a team he loved. 'Sabr' — the Urdu and Arabic word for patience or endurance — is his attempt to make sense of that fracture, and it lands with the force of a document that was written to be argued with rather than admired.

What the Book Actually Says

Khawaja does not position himself as the moral centre of the story, and that is precisely what gives the memoir its credibility. Where earlier accounts from inside the Australian dressing room have tended toward either blanket contrition or quiet self-exoneration, 'Sabr' occupies an uncomfortable middle ground. He acknowledges that he saw warning signs — a win-at-all-costs drift in the leadership group's language, a tolerance for gamesmanship that hardened over successive tours — without claiming he could have stopped any of it from his position in the side.

The book is structured as a series of thematic chapters rather than a strict chronology, moving between the Cape Town fallout, his own battles with discrimination and identity as a Muslim Australian of Pakistani descent, and a broader meditation on what "success" was costing the people chasing it. The scandal is the spine of the narrative, but it is not the whole animal.

The Leadership Group Under the Lens

Khawaja is careful and, at times, lawyerly in how he handles the conduct of captain Steve Smith, vice-captain David Warner and the then-coach Darren Lehmann. He stops short of assigning individual blame in the manner of a tabloid, preferring to describe a system in which deference to senior figures crowded out dissent. For readers hoping for a fresh whistleblower's account of who knew what and when, the book will feel frustratingly restrained. For those interested in how institutional culture corrodes quietly, it is more rewarding.

Identity, Faith and the Outsider's View

The most original passages have little to do with cricket. Khawaja writes candidly about being the only Muslim in an Australian Test dressing room for much of his career, about the small daily calculations of belonging, and about how an outsider's vantage point let him read the team's drift more clearly than those at its centre. These sections reframe the scandal as a story about power and conformity as much as about cheating.

Analysis: Why It Still Matters Eight Years On

The enduring interest in Cape Town is not really about a piece of yellow tape and a coin. It is about whether the reckoning that followed was genuine or cosmetic. The post-scandal review promised cultural reform; the sanctions expired; the same personnel, in different combinations, drifted back toward the top of the game. Khawaja's book arrives as a quiet counter-argument to the idea that the matter was ever truly settled.

  • It challenges the "bad apples" defence by describing a culture, not a conspiracy of three men.
  • It reframes the scandal as a governance failure, implicating structures above the players.
  • It insists that the emotional and human cost — to the accused, to teammates, to Khawaja himself — has not been honestly reckoned with.
  • It asks whether a sport that celebrates ruthlessness can credibly demand humility after a scandal it helped incubate.

Critics will note the timing: memoirs of this kind benefit enormously from proximity to a still-live controversy. Khawaja is not a disinterested narrator, and 'Sabr' is, in part, a bid to define his own legacy. But partiality is not the same as dishonesty, and the book's willingness to implicate the environment he thrived in gives it a credibility that pure score-settling would lack.

Key Takeaways

  • A culture story, not a conspiracy story. Khawaja's central claim is that Cape Town was the product of an environment, not three individuals acting alone.
  • The outsider lens is the book's strength. His perspective as the team's Muslim, migrant background player gives him a clarity that insiders lacked.
  • Restraint over revelation. Readers expecting fresh, name-by-name disclosures may be disappointed; the book trades gossip for structural analysis.
  • Partiality is acknowledged, not hidden. It is a legacy-defining memoir as much as an act of conscience, and it is stronger for admitting the tension.
  • Still relevant. Eight years on, it asks whether Australian cricket's reforms were real or merely a pause before business as usual.
