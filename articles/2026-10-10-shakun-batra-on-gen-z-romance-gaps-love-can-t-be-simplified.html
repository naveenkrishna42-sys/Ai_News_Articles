Shakun Batra on Gen Z romance gaps: ‘Love can’t be simplified’

Shakun Batra pinpoints the Gen Z romance disconnect

In recent commentary reported across major Indian outlets, filmmaker Shakun Batra argues that Bollywood love stories are failing to connect with Gen Z because they don’t fully reflect how young people experience romance today. His central critique: the industry has “simplified love” in ways that flatten feelings into predictable beats—meet-cute, misunderstanding, grand confession—without capturing emotional texture.

For Gen Z viewers, romance is not just chemistry; it is communication, clarity, and choices made under real constraints. When films reduce those elements to surface-level charm, the audience feels spoken at rather than seen.

That mismatch isn’t merely taste-based. It affects casting, writing, and even how viewers interpret character growth—especially for women and for relationships that don’t follow traditional timelines.

Why “simplified love” doesn’t land with Gen Z

Batra’s “you meet this person who is you” idea touches a deeper shift: younger audiences often look for self-recognition in characters, not idealized archetypes. Gen Z tends to reward stories where attraction coexists with doubt, negotiation, and self-awareness—especially when the relationship challenges identity or values.

Classic Bollywood romances frequently position feelings as fate-driven and linear: attraction escalates quickly, obstacles appear from outside, and emotional stakes resolve neatly. Gen Z, raised on social media and nuanced relationship discourse, expects internal stakes—fear of commitment, mismatch in emotional availability, boundaries around intimacy, and the messy work of choosing one another.

In buyer terms (for creators and producers), this is a “product-market fit” issue. If the emotional offering is packaged in outdated narrative form, marketing can’t fully compensate. The story must match the way audiences process intimacy and conflict.

What changed: language of dating, consent, and emotional literacy

One reason Batra’s critique resonates is that dating culture has become more explicit about consent and more skeptical of performative romance. Gen Z conversations often include “What are we?” questions, discomfort around pressure, and expectations that communication should be legible, not magical.

Bollywood films have historically used romantic leaps—running after someone, silently suffering, or treating ambiguity as charm. Today’s viewers may read ambiguity differently: as avoidant behavior, as power imbalance, or as emotional immaturity, rather than as “romantic tension.”

That shift also shows up in how characters articulate their needs. When dialogue feels stylized or disconnected from everyday rhythms, scenes lose credibility, even if the actors perform convincingly.

  • Pro: Modern romance writing can feel more relatable when it includes negotiation, regret, and repair attempts rather than only dramatic declarations.
  • Pro: Consent-forward framing can deepen empathy and make intimacy feel earned.
  • Con: If ambiguity is used without emotional accountability, audiences may interpret it as writing laziness.
  • Con: Over-correction toward “talking about feelings” can also backfire if chemistry and cinematic stakes disappear.

Buyer’s checklist for producers and writers targeting Gen Z

If you’re a producer, writer, or marketer planning a romance for Gen Z, Batra’s comments translate into practical development questions. Start with character agency: do leads actively choose, or are they swept by momentum? Next, examine emotional causality: do misunderstandings drive growth, or do they merely delay endings?

Then test language and behavior. Ask whether the film’s romantic communication matches what audiences consider healthy: clarity around intentions, respect for boundaries, and accountability after hurt.

Finally, audit social reality. Gen Z doesn’t live in a single uniform India—class, city life, family expectations, and online-offline dynamics shape how people date. When stories ignore those layers, romance feels general, not specific.

What “connection” really means for Bollywood romances

Batra’s point isn’t that Gen Z dislikes love stories. It’s that they distrust romances that treat feelings as simplified, frictionless, and destiny-coded. When the emotional logic is shallow, viewers disengage—regardless of whether the screenplay is “clean” or the music is catchy.

For Bollywood to regain romance’s relevance, creators likely need to build stories that feel like lived experience: the slow learning of what the other person is asking for, the courage to say no, and the discipline to show up as a better partner after mistakes.

That’s why the question “why romances fail to connect” becomes, for filmmakers, a question about form and fidelity—how well cinema mirrors contemporary intimacy, without turning it into a lecture or a trend imitation.
