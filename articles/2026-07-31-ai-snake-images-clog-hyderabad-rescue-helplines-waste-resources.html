AI snake images clog Hyderabad rescue helplines, waste resources

📅 2026-07-31 📂 Hyderabad Original source ↗
Key points
  • AI-generated snake images are being submitted to Hyderabad's rescue helplines, causing false alarms.
  • Officials say these fake images divert resources and delay response to legitimate requests.
  • Authorities are urging citizens to verify images before sending and are considering stricter verification measures.

Hyderabad's wildlife rescue teams are grappling with a new, digital-age nuisance: AI-generated images of snakes that are clogging helplines and wasting precious time and resources. The trend, which has emerged in recent weeks, is forcing authorities to sift through a growing number of false alarms, delaying response to genuine emergencies.

According to officials from the city's disaster response and wildlife rescue units, the problem has become severe enough to affect their daily operations. "We receive dozens of calls each day, and a significant portion now includes images that look real but are clearly fabricated," a senior official told The Hindu, requesting anonymity.

How the fake images work

The images, often generated using freely available AI tools, depict snakes in various poses – coiled in corners, slithering across floors, or hanging from ceilings. They are so realistic that even trained personnel sometimes take a second look. However, upon closer inspection, tell-tale signs of AI generation, such as distorted scales or unnatural shadows, give them away.

Residents typically send these images along with their location, claiming a snake is present. The rescue teams, unable to verify the accuracy of the images from a distance, often dispatch units to the spot. "When we arrive, there's no snake, or the image doesn't match the surroundings," the official explained. "Often, the resident is confused, or worse, we find that the image was entirely fabricated."

This not only wastes fuel and manpower but also diverts attention from genuine calls where a venomous snake might actually be posing a threat. In some cases, teams have arrived late to real emergencies because they were busy chasing phantom reptiles.

Impact on rescue operations

The impact is measurable. The city's rescue teams, already stretched thin during the monsoon season when snake sightings increase, are now spending hours on wild goose chases. "Every fake call means a real call might have to wait," said another official, who manages the helpline. "This is a serious issue that we are trying to address."

Some residents may be sending these images as a prank, while others might be testing the system. But officials suspect that some are using AI to create images of snakes they fear might be present, even when they haven't seen one. "People are scared, and they might be trying to pre-empt a problem," the official added. "But it's causing more harm than good."

The problem is not unique to Hyderabad – similar incidents have been reported in other Indian cities – but the frequency here has caught the attention of senior officials. The city's wildlife authorities are now considering implementing stricter verification protocols, including asking callers to describe the snake's appearance or send a live video instead of a static image.

Authorities urge caution

Officials are urging citizens to be responsible when reporting snake sightings. "If you see a snake, please call us. But please don't send AI-generated images," a spokesperson said. "We rely on accurate information to do our job effectively."

They are also advising people to double-check any image before sending it, and to remember that a real emergency requires a real response. Some residents have already been warned for repeated false reports, though no formal action has been taken so far.

The city's disaster management teams are also exploring ways to filter or flag suspicious images automatically, though such technology is still in early stages. Until then, the manual verification process will continue, even if it means occasional delays.

What happens next?

In the coming weeks, authorities are likely to release a public advisory on proper snake sighting reporting procedures. They are also in talks with tech companies to develop tools that can detect AI-generated images in real-time, which could help filter out fake reports before they reach rescue teams. For now, the message is simple: don't cry wolf – or snake – with a computer.
