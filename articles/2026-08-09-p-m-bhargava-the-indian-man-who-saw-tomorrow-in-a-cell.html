P.M. Bhargava: The Indian man who saw tomorrow in a cell

📅 2026-08-09 📂 Hyderabad Original source ↗
Key points
  • P.M. Bhargava founded CCMB in Hyderabad, pioneering molecular biology research in India.
  • He was known for his futuristic vision, predicting advances in genomics and biotechnology decades ahead.
  • Bhargava's legacy includes mentorship and advocacy for scientific temper in public life.

When P.M. Bhargava looked through a microscope, he did not just see cells. He saw the blueprint of the future. The founder-director of the Centre for Cellular and Molecular Biology (CCMB) in Hyderabad, who passed away on Saturday, was a man who understood that the smallest units of life held the biggest answers for humanity.

Bhargava, who was 88, was not just a scientist. He was an institution builder, a fierce rationalist, and a public intellectual who believed science had to be part of everyday conversation. His death marks the end of an era for Indian biology, but his foresight continues to shape research directions across the country.

A vision that outran its time

In the 1970s, when molecular biology was still a nascent field in India, Bhargava pushed for a dedicated centre that would focus on understanding life at the cellular level. He argued that India could not afford to be a mere spectator in the coming biological revolution. His persistence led to the establishment of CCMB in 1977, initially housed in a small building in Hyderabad before moving to its sprawling campus in the city's outskirts.

His predictions were startlingly accurate. Decades before the Human Genome Project was completed, Bhargava spoke of the need to map genes and understand their functions. He often remarked that the 21st century would be the century of biology, much as the 20th was of physics. Colleagues recall how he would urge young researchers to think beyond conventional boundaries, to ask questions that had no immediate answers.

The man behind the lab coat

Bhargava was known for his sharp wit and even sharper criticism of pseudo-science. He was a vocal advocate of scientific temper, often taking on superstition and obscurantism in public forums. His columns and lectures were as accessible to school students as they were to seasoned researchers. He believed that a nation that does not question cannot progress.

At CCMB, he fostered a culture of open debate. Friday seminars were not just about data; they were about ideas. Young scientists were encouraged to challenge senior researchers, a practice that was uncommon in Indian institutions at the time. Many of India's leading biologists today trace their early training to Bhargava's mentorship.

A legacy that lives in every lab

Under his leadership, CCMB became a hub for research on gene expression, DNA repair, and evolution. His own work on the molecular basis of cell function earned him international recognition. But he was equally concerned about applying science to solve India's problems — from health to agriculture.

After his retirement, Bhargava remained active in public life, writing and speaking on issues ranging from GM crops to nuclear energy. He never shied away from controversy if it meant defending evidence-based thinking. His last years were marked by a quiet but persistent campaign to revive scientific temper in public discourse.

Prime Minister Narendra Modi and Union Minister Dr. Jitendra Singh expressed condolences, calling Bhargava a pioneer whose contributions would be remembered for generations. The Telangana government also paid tribute, noting his role in making Hyderabad a scientific hub.

His passing leaves a void, but also a challenge. The question now is whether India's scientific establishment will continue to nurture the kind of fearless inquiry that Bhargava embodied. His life was a reminder that science is not just about publications and patents — it is about a way of seeing the world, with curiosity and courage.
