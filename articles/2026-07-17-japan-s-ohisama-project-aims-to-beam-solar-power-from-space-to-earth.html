Japan's OHISAMA project aims to beam solar power from space to Earth

📅 2026-07-17 📂 Science Original source ↗
Key points
  • Japan's space agency JAXA is leading the OHISAMA project to beam solar electricity from orbit.
  • The system uses a satellite with solar panels that convert sunlight to microwaves for transmission.
  • A 2026 demo aims to send power from low Earth orbit to a receiver on the ground.
  • Wireless power transmission could provide clean, constant energy unaffected by weather or time of day.
  • Technical hurdles include beam accuracy, efficiency, and safety for aircraft and wildlife.

Japan's ambitious space solar plan

Japan is building a system to beam wireless electricity from space directly to Earth. The project, called OHISAMA, aims to collect solar energy in orbit and transmit it down as microwaves. It is being developed by the Japan Aerospace Exploration Agency (JAXA).

The idea is not new, but Japan is pushing to make it real. If successful, it could offer a source of clean power that works day and night, rain or shine. The country has set a target to demonstrate the technology by 2026.

How OHISAMA works

The system involves a satellite equipped with large solar panels. These panels convert sunlight into electricity. That electricity is then transformed into microwave beams, which are directed towards Earth.

On the ground, a receiving station—called a rectenna—converts the microwaves back into electricity. The beam is designed to be safe for aircraft and wildlife. JAXA has tested the concept in laboratory conditions and in low-power field experiments.

The 2026 demonstration will involve a small satellite in low Earth orbit. It will attempt to send a few kilowatts of power to a receiver on the ground. That is enough to power a few homes for a short time, but the goal is to scale up.

Why space-based solar power matters

Solar panels on Earth do not generate power at night or when clouds block the sun. In space, sunlight is constant and more intense. A space-based system could provide power 24/7, without needing batteries for overnight storage.

Japan relies heavily on energy imports and is looking for alternatives after the Fukushima nuclear disaster. Space solar power could become a major source of clean energy. Officials have not yet confirmed the total cost of the OHISAMA project, but estimates run into hundreds of crores of rupees.

Other countries, including the United States and China, are also researching similar technology. But Japan is among the first to aim for an orbital test. The project has drawn interest from private companies as well.

Technical and safety challenges

Beaming power from space requires extreme precision. The microwave beam must stay locked on the ground receiver, even as the satellite moves at high speed. Any misalignment could waste energy or cause damage.

Safety is another concern. The beam must be diffuse enough to avoid harm to living things or electronic systems. JAXA says it is designing the system to meet international safety standards. The agency has also studied the effect of microwaves on birds and airplanes.

Efficiency is a hurdle. Converting sunlight to electricity, then to microwaves, then back to electricity results in significant losses. Current prototypes achieve less than 10% end-to-end efficiency. Engineers are working to improve that.

The 2026 test will be a key milestone. If it works, it will prove that wireless power transmission from space is feasible. If it fails, the project may face delays or rethinking.

JAXA is expected to release more technical details in the coming months. The agency is also looking for international partners to share costs and expertise.
