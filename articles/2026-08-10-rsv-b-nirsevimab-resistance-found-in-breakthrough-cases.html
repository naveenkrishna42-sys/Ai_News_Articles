RSV-B Nirsevimab Resistance Found in Breakthrough Cases

📅 2026-08-10 📂 Health Original source ↗
Key points
  • Breakthrough RSV-B infections showed reduced susceptibility to nirsevimab.
  • Resistance mutations identified in the F protein gene.
  • Findings underscore need for continued surveillance and new preventive strategies.

In a development that could reshape how respiratory syncytial virus (RSV) is prevented in infants, researchers have detected resistance to the monoclonal antibody nirsevimab in breakthrough infections caused by RSV-B. The findings, reported by EMJ, indicate that some viral strains are evolving to evade the drug, which has been widely adopted as a preventive measure for newborns and young children.

How the Resistance Was Found

The detection came from a detailed analysis of breakthrough cases—infections that occurred despite the administration of nirsevimab. Scientists sequenced the viral genomes from these cases and identified specific mutations in the F protein gene, the target of the antibody. These mutations reduced the virus's susceptibility to nirsevimab in laboratory tests.

While the overall number of such cases remains small, the emergence of resistance is a warning sign. RSV is a leading cause of severe respiratory illness in infants, and nirsevimab has been a critical tool in reducing hospitalisations. Any erosion of its effectiveness could have significant clinical consequences.

Implications for Infant Health

For paediatricians and public health officials, the findings are a prompt to reassess current prevention protocols. The study suggests that while nirsevimab remains largely effective, the virus is capable of adapting under selective pressure. This is not unexpected—viruses mutate—but it underscores the fragility of relying on a single preventive agent.

The situation is particularly relevant in India, where RSV burden is high and access to newer preventive therapies is uneven. If resistance spreads, it could widen existing disparities in child health outcomes.

A Call for Vigilance

Experts are now calling for enhanced surveillance of circulating RSV strains, especially in regions where nirsevimab is used extensively. Monitoring for resistance mutations should become routine, they argue, to detect emerging threats early. The findings also highlight the need for a diversified arsenal—including vaccines and next-generation antibodies—so that prevention does not hinge on one mechanism.

The authors of the study note that while the resistant viruses were less susceptible in lab assays, it is not yet clear how this translates to clinical effectiveness. More data are needed to determine whether these mutations lead to higher rates of severe disease or hospitalisation among vaccinated infants.

What Happens Next

Research groups are expected to expand their genomic surveillance efforts, and regulatory bodies may update their guidance on nirsevimab use. In the meantime, clinicians are advised to remain alert to breakthrough infections and report them to public health databases. The coming months will be crucial in determining whether this resistance is an isolated finding or the beginning of a broader trend.
