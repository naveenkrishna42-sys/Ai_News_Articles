Smartphone tips for capturing Europe's 2026 total solar eclipse

📅 2026-08-08 📂 Science Original source ↗
Key points
  • A total solar eclipse will cross parts of Europe on August 12, 2026, drawing tourists to prime viewing spots.
  • Smartphone users need special solar filters and proper settings to photograph the eclipse safely.
  • Experts advise practicing beforehand and focusing on capturing the surroundings, not just the sun.

Europe's Celestial Show

On August 12, 2026, a total solar eclipse will sweep across parts of Europe, turning day into night for a few breathtaking minutes. From Spain's mountain villages to Yorkshire's rolling hills, skywatchers are preparing for the celestial event. But for most, the tool at hand won't be a professional camera—it'll be the smartphone in their pocket.

Capturing a total solar eclipse with a phone is possible, but it requires planning and the right precautions. Without them, you risk both a blurry photo and permanent eye damage.

Safety First: Protect Your Eyes and Phone

The golden rule of eclipse photography is the same as eclipse viewing: never look directly at the sun without certified solar filters. This applies to your phone's camera lens as well. The intense sunlight can damage the camera sensor, just as it can harm your retina.

You'll need a solar filter that fits over your phone's lens, or a pair of eclipse glasses held in front of the camera. These are inexpensive and widely available online. Remove the filter only during the brief moment of totality—when the moon completely blocks the sun—and replace it as soon as the sun reappears.

Setting Up Your Phone for the Shot

Once your lens is protected, it's time to adjust your settings. Turn off autofocus and set the focus to infinity, or tap on the sun in your viewfinder to lock focus. Manual controls, if your phone offers them, are your best friend: set a low ISO (around 100-200) and experiment with exposure compensation to avoid a blown-out disk.

Use a tripod or a stable surface. Even the steadiest hands introduce shake, which ruins the sharpness of a solar image. A smartphone tripod mount costs little and makes a world of difference. Also, consider using a remote shutter or the phone's timer to avoid touching the screen at the critical moment.

Don't Just Shoot the Sun

Here's the counterintuitive advice from seasoned eclipse chasers: don't spend the entire eclipse staring at your screen. The most memorable photos often capture the environment—the eerie twilight, the crescent-shaped shadows dappling the ground, the reactions of people around you.

During partial phases, look for shadows of tree leaves that act as natural pinhole cameras, projecting tiny crescents onto the ground. When totality hits, the corona—the sun's outer atmosphere—will be visible to the naked eye. That's the moment to switch to a wide-angle shot and capture the dramatic dark sky and horizon glow.

Prepare and Practice Before the Big Day

Astronomers recommend doing a dry run. On a sunny day before the eclipse, practice focusing and exposing on the sun (with a filter, of course) or a bright light source. This will save you precious seconds when the real thing happens.

Also, arrive at your viewing location early. Popular spots in Spain's mountain villages and other prime viewing areas are bracing for an influx of astrotourists. Roads may be congested, and parking scarce. Bring extra batteries—cold weather and prolonged screen use drain them quickly—and a backup memory card or cloud storage plan.

Finally, remember the human element. The eclipse is a shared experience. Look up, take in the moment, and let your smartphone be a tool, not a barrier. The best photo you take might be the one of your family or friends, silhouetted against the celestial spectacle.

What to Watch For

As August 12 approaches, keep an eye on weather forecasts. Cloud cover could obscure the view in some regions, so flexible plans are wise. Whether you capture a perfect corona shot or simply soak in the experience, this eclipse promises to be a memory for a lifetime.
