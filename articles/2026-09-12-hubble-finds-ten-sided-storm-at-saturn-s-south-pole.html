Hubble Finds Ten-Sided Storm at Saturn's South Pole

✍️ By TIVRA News Science & Health Desk 📅 2026-09-12 📂 Science Original source ↗
Key points
  • Hubble has confirmed a roughly 104,000-mile-wide ten-sided (decagonal) storm system at Saturn's south pole, a geometric twin to the long-studied northern hexagon.
  • The southern decagon appears to be a newer, still-forming feature, suggesting seasonal atmospheric reorganisation as Saturn's seasons shift.
  • The finding strengthens the case for long-baseline space telescope monitoring of planetary atmospheres between dedicated orbiter missions.

A Geometric Twin at Saturn's South Pole

NASA's Hubble Space Telescope has revealed a giant atmospheric wave circling Saturn's south pole — a roughly 104,000-mile-wide ten-sided storm that mirrors the planet's famous northern hexagon. Astronomers describe the feature as a decagon: a jet stream and cloud band forming a polygon with ten distinct sides, embedded in the same polar vortex structure that has fascinated planetary scientists for decades.

The northern hexagon, first spotted by the Voyager flybys in the early 1980s and later imaged in detail by Cassini, has long been treated as a unique curiosity. Hubble's new observations suggest polygon-shaped polar jets are not a one-off oddity but a repeatable behaviour of Saturn's atmosphere.

What Hubble Actually Measured

Hubble's long-wavelength imaging tracks cloud-top features and haze layers that reveal wind shear and temperature contrasts. The southern decagon sits at a comparable latitude to its northern counterpart, though it appears less sharply defined and more variable in shape, which researchers interpret as an actively forming or evolving structure rather than a stable, mature one.

Why a Decagon Forms — and Why It Matters

Polygonal jets emerge when a fast-moving ring of air develops a stable wave pattern, with the number of sides set by the balance between wind speed, latitude and the planet's rotation. On Earth, similar but far smaller meanders appear in the polar jet stream; on Saturn, the scale is planetary and the geometry is startlingly clean.

The southern decagon's rougher edges matter because they offer a natural experiment. A mature hexagon in the north and a developing decagon in the south let scientists compare how these structures stabilise, how they interact with seasonal sunlight changes, and how long they persist. That has direct relevance for atmospheric models used to interpret exoplanet weather, where polar vortices are increasingly detected.

Key Real-World Highlights

  • Hubble's ultraviolet and near-infrared sensitivity lets it track haze layers that ground-based telescopes largely miss.
  • Saturn's seasons last roughly seven Earth years each, so the southern hemisphere's changing illumination is reshaping polar dynamics.
  • The finding underscores the value of long-baseline space telescope monitoring between dedicated orbiter missions.
  • No new mission is required to keep watching — Hubble's archive provides decades of comparison data.

Pros and Cons of the Hubble-Only Approach

  • Pro: Hubble delivers consistent, calibrated polar imaging across more than three decades, enabling genuine change detection.
  • Pro: No flyby or orbiter cost is involved; the data is already public through NASA archives.
  • Con: Hubble cannot measure winds in situ, so wind speeds and vertical structure remain inferred rather than directly sampled.
  • Con: Resolution limits mean fine-scale vortex details are blurred compared with Cassini's close passes.

The Bottom Line for Skywatchers and Students

Saturn remains a prime target for amateur astronomers with 150mm or larger telescopes, where the rings and polar shading are visible on steady nights. The decagon itself is not resolvable from the ground, but its discovery shows that professional and citizen observers still add value: ABC News reported that a backyard astronomer contributed to a separate world-first Saturn finding, a reminder that planetary science is not confined to large facilities.

For now, the southern decagon stands as a fresh test case for how planetary atmospheres organise themselves into geometry — and Hubble, despite its age, remains the instrument doing the watching.
