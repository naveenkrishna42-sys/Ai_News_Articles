AI data centres strain India's water and power resources

📅 2026-08-09 📂 Business Original source ↗
Key points
  • Data centres consume vast amounts of fresh water for cooling, straining local supplies.
  • AI's exponential growth is increasing energy demand, complicating India's net-zero goals.
  • Experts urge policy interventions to promote water efficiency and renewable energy use.
  • Communities like Pahrump in the US highlight global water conflicts linked to data centres.
  • India's data centre capacity is set to grow multifold, intensifying resource pressures.

India's rapid expansion of data centres to power its digital economy and artificial intelligence ambitions is coming at a hidden cost — a staggering appetite for fresh water and electricity. As tech giants and domestic players rush to build facilities across the country, experts are flagging the environmental toll of this growth, which threatens to strain already scarce resources.

Water guzzlers in a water-stressed nation

Data centres require enormous amounts of water for cooling servers, often drawing from municipal supplies in regions that are already water-stressed. A single large facility can consume lakhs of litres daily, enough to serve thousands of households. In India, where groundwater levels are depleting and many cities face seasonal shortages, this demand is particularly concerning.

Experts point out that the problem is not just the volume but the inefficiency of current cooling systems. Most Indian data centres rely on evaporative cooling, which is water-intensive, unlike newer designs that use air cooling or closed-loop systems. Retrofitting existing facilities is costly, and many operators prioritise speed over sustainability.

AI's exponential energy appetite

Artificial intelligence workloads are far more energy-hungry than traditional cloud computing. Training a single large language model can consume electricity equivalent to hundreds of homes' annual usage. As AI adoption grows, so does the demand for high-density servers that generate more heat, requiring even more water for cooling.

This energy intensity complicates India's climate commitments. While the country has pledged to expand renewable capacity, the grid still relies heavily on coal. Data centres, which need round-the-clock power, often end up using fossil fuels, undermining progress toward net-zero targets.

Global parallels: The Pahrump problem

The issue is not unique to India. In the US, the small desert town of Pahrump has become a flashpoint in the fight over data centre water use. Residents and activists are battling tech companies that plan to build facilities in the arid region, where water is already a precious commodity. The conflict highlights a global pattern: data centres are increasingly drawing criticism for prioritising profit over local environmental and social needs.

In India, similar tensions are emerging in states like Maharashtra and Telangana, where data centre parks are being promoted as economic growth engines. Local communities, however, worry about the impact on their water supplies and the burden on power grids that already struggle to meet demand.

Experts call for policy intervention

Analysts argue that India needs a regulatory framework that mandates water efficiency and renewable energy adoption for data centres. Some suggest incentivising the use of treated wastewater or seawater for cooling, while others advocate for stricter reporting requirements on water and power usage.

There is also a push for decentralised data storage and edge computing, which could reduce the size and resource footprint of mega-facilities. However, these solutions require significant investment and a shift in industry practices, which may not happen without regulatory pressure.

Jobs versus environment: A false trade-off?

The debate often pits job creation against environmental protection. Data centres do create employment, but experts note that the jobs are relatively few compared to other industries. The environmental costs, they argue, could outweigh the economic benefits if not managed properly.

Some industry leaders are beginning to respond, with pilot projects exploring liquid cooling and solar-powered facilities. Yet these remain niche efforts, and the pace of change is slow relative to the surge in data centre construction.

The road ahead

As India's data centre capacity is projected to grow several-fold in the coming years, the environmental pressure will only intensify. Policymakers, industry, and communities must grapple with difficult choices about how to balance digital ambition with resource sustainability.

The coming months will show whether India can integrate water and energy efficiency into its data centre policy, or whether the hidden cost of the digital world becomes an unmanageable burden.
