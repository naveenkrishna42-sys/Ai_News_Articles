Amazon Birds and Monkeys Share a Natural 'Internet of the Forest'

📅 2026-07-21 📂 Science Original source ↗
Key points
  • Birds and monkeys in the Amazon use a shared alarm call system, dubbed the 'internet of the forest'.
  • The network allows different species to understand and respond to each other's predator warnings.
  • Researchers observed cross-species communication, with birds reacting to monkey calls and vice versa.

Deep in the Amazon rainforest, a new kind of network has been discovered. It is not powered by fibre optics or satellites. It runs on bird calls and monkey shrieks.

Scientists have found that birds and monkeys in the region have developed a shared communication system. They are calling it the 'internet of the forest'. The discovery was reported by The Times of India.

Cross-Species Alarm System

The research reveals that different species of birds and monkeys understand each other's alarm calls. When a monkey spots a predator, its warning cry sends birds scattering. Likewise, a bird's distress call can send monkeys scrambling up trees.

This is not just noise. The calls carry specific information. A call for a snake is different from a call for a hawk. The animals react accordingly, even if the call comes from a completely different species.

How the Network Works

The system functions like a natural internet. Each species acts as a node, broadcasting alerts across the forest canopy. The information spreads faster than any single animal could manage alone.

Researchers observed that the calls are not just instinctive. The animals seem to learn the meaning of other species' calls over time. This suggests a complex, learned behaviour that benefits the entire ecosystem.

The network is not centralised. There is no single leader or controller. Every animal that calls is both a sender and a receiver, creating a web of shared vigilance.

Implications for Conservation

This discovery has serious implications for how we understand animal intelligence and social behaviour. It shows that cooperation between species is far more advanced than previously thought.

Conservationists say the 'internet of the forest' could be fragile. If one species disappears due to deforestation or hunting, the network could break down. The loss of a single node could leave others vulnerable to predators.

The research highlights the interconnectedness of the Amazon's wildlife. Protecting the forest is not just about saving individual species. It is about preserving the invisible threads that bind them together.

What Comes Next

The scientists are now studying how far the network extends. They want to know if other animals, such as rodents or reptiles, are also part of this communication web.

Field work is ongoing. The team hopes to map the full 'internet of the forest' before parts of it are lost forever. The Amazon, it seems, has had its own network all along — one that predates any human invention.
