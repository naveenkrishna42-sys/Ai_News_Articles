Indiranagar footpaths shrink under encroachments

📅 2026-08-04 📂 Bengaluru Original source ↗
Key points
  • Footpaths in Indiranagar have narrowed due to encroachments by shops, eateries and parked vehicles.
  • Pedestrians are forced to walk on the road, raising safety concerns.
  • Residents demand stricter enforcement and better urban planning.

Indiranagar's once-spacious footpaths are disappearing, one encroachment at a time. What were designed as safe pedestrian walkways are now squeezed by shop displays, eatery seating, parked two-wheelers and haphazard construction material. The result: walkers are increasingly forced onto the carriageway, dodging traffic.

Residents and regular visitors to the area say the problem has worsened over the past few years. A stroll down the 100 Feet Road or the bylanes off it now requires constant vigilance. The pavements, where they exist, are often blocked by obstacles, making them unusable for the elderly, children and the disabled.

Shops and eateries spill onto pavements

Commercial establishments are among the biggest offenders. Many retailers extend their display racks and merchandise onto the footpath, shrinking the walkable area by half or more. Restaurants and cafes, particularly those with outdoor seating, have set up tables and chairs on the pavement without clear authorisation.

This is not a new phenomenon but one that has intensified with Indiranagar's commercial boom. The area, known for its pubs, boutiques and tech offices, draws heavy footfall. Yet the infrastructure has not kept pace, and the public realm is being ceded to private use.

Parked vehicles add to the squeeze

Parked vehicles are another major culprit. Two-wheelers are routinely parked on the footpath, leaving barely enough room for a single person to pass. In some stretches, cars are also mounted on the kerb, completely blocking the path.

Auto-rickshaw drivers and cab services compound the problem by stopping mid-road or on the pavement to pick up passengers. The lack of designated parking bays in the commercial core pushes drivers to improvise, and pedestrians pay the price.

Pedestrians take risks on the road

The consequences are tangible. With footpaths narrowed or blocked, pedestrians—office-goers, shoppers, college students—are compelled to walk on the road itself. This is particularly dangerous on stretches where two-wheelers and cars move at speed.

“It's a daily struggle,” said a local shopkeeper who has been in the area for over a decade. “People walk in the middle of the road because there's no space on the pavement. It's only a matter of time before someone gets seriously hurt.” His observation matches the experience of many regulars who have learned to navigate the area with caution.

Enforcement remains weak

Civic authorities have periodically conducted drives to clear encroachments, but the effect is short-lived. Shop owners and eateries return to their old habits within days, and the footpaths revert to their cluttered state. Residents point to a lack of sustained enforcement and a clear policy on what constitutes permissible use of the pavement.

There is also the question of design. Some footpaths in Indiranagar are simply too narrow to begin with, especially on secondary roads. Even without encroachments, two people cannot walk abreast, let alone a person with a stroller or a wheelchair user.

Calls for a walkable Indiranagar

Urban activists and resident welfare associations have been demanding a comprehensive approach. They want strict enforcement of no-encroachment rules, creation of designated parking spaces away from footpaths, and redesign of pavements to meet minimum width standards. Some have proposed the adoption of a 'complete streets' policy that prioritises pedestrians and public transport over private vehicles.

The Bruhat Bengaluru Mahanagara Palike (BBMP) has acknowledged the issue in the past but has not yet announced a concrete plan for Indiranagar. Officials have not confirmed any timeline for fresh action.

As Bengaluru grows, the battle for footpath space will only intensify. Whether Indiranagar's pavements are reclaimed for pedestrians or remain a patchwork of encroachments will depend on sustained civic pressure and political will. What is clear is that the status quo is unsustainable—for walkers, for businesses, and for the city's image.
