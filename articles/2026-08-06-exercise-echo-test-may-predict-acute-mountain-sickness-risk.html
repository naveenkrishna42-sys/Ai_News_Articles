Exercise Echo Test May Predict Acute Mountain Sickness Risk

📅 2026-08-06 📂 Health Original source ↗
Key points
  • Exercise echocardiography shows promise in predicting acute mountain sickness risk.
  • The test measures heart and lung response to exertion at altitude.
  • Findings could lead to better pre-trek screening for vulnerable individuals.

For millions who trek to high altitudes each year, acute mountain sickness (AMS) can turn a dream holiday into a nightmare. Now, a new study suggests that a simple exercise test, commonly used in cardiology, might help predict who is most at risk before they even set foot on the mountain.

The Study

Researchers have found that exercise echocardiography—an ultrasound of the heart taken during physical exertion—can identify physiological markers associated with AMS. The test measures how the heart and lungs respond to increased demand, which may mirror the stress experienced at altitude.

The findings, published in the European Journal of Preventive Cardiology, offer a potential screening tool for trekkers, mountaineers, and military personnel deployed to high-altitude regions. Currently, AMS risk is assessed largely through medical history and previous episodes, which are not always reliable.

How It Works

During exercise echocardiography, a person cycles or runs on a treadmill while ultrasound images capture the heart's function in real time. The study observed specific changes in right heart pressure and pulmonary artery dynamics that correlated with AMS susceptibility.

At high altitude, reduced oxygen levels cause pulmonary vasoconstriction, increasing pressure in the lungs. Individuals whose hearts show a pronounced response to exercise may be more prone to this effect, making them vulnerable to AMS symptoms like headache, nausea, and fatigue.

Implications for Travellers

If validated in larger trials, this test could become part of a pre-trek health check, especially for those planning rapid ascents to altitudes above 3,000 metres. It could also help clinicians advise on acclimatisation strategies or prophylactic medications.

However, experts caution that the test is not yet ready for routine use. The study's sample size was modest, and further research is needed to establish clear thresholds for risk prediction.

What This Means for High-Altitude Medicine

Acute mountain sickness affects roughly 25% of travellers sleeping above 2,500 metres, with severe cases leading to life-threatening conditions like high-altitude pulmonary oedema (HAPE). Current prevention relies on gradual ascent and drugs like acetazolamide, but these are not always practical or effective.

An objective, exercise-based screening could revolutionise how doctors advise patients. It might also help identify individuals who require more cautious ascent profiles or additional oxygen support.

Researchers are now planning multicentre studies to confirm these findings and develop standardised protocols. If successful, exercise echocardiography could become a routine part of high-altitude medical assessments, offering peace of mind to the millions who venture to the mountains each year.
