EU orders Google to open Android AI features, share search data

📅 2026-07-17 📂 Technology Original source ↗
Key points
  • EU mandates Google to allow third-party AI assistants on Android devices.
  • Google must share search data with rival search engines under new regulations.
  • The order aims to boost competition in AI and search markets; critics call it overreach.

The European Commission has ordered Google to open its Android operating system to third-party artificial intelligence assistants and share search data with rivals, in one of the most aggressive regulatory moves against the tech giant.

What the EU order demands

The directive, announced on Friday, requires Google to allow competing AI assistants to operate on Android devices on equal terms with its own Google Assistant. Users will be able to choose a default AI assistant during device setup, and Google must not favour its own services.

Separately, the Commission has ordered Google to share anonymised search data with rival search engines. This data includes query volumes and click-through rates, which competitors say they need to improve their own algorithms and challenge Google's dominance.

Why the EU is acting now

European regulators have been tightening scrutiny of Big Tech's control over emerging AI markets. The Android mobile ecosystem, which powers over 70% of smartphones in Europe, is seen as a critical gateway for AI services.

Officials argue that without intervention, Google could leverage its control over Android to lock out rivals in the fast-growing AI assistant space. The search data sharing requirement is aimed at preventing Google from using its search monopoly to stifle competition in AI-powered search tools.

Industry reaction

Google has not yet issued a formal response, but the company has previously argued that such orders could compromise user security and data privacy. The company has warned that opening Android too broadly could fragment the ecosystem and expose users to security risks.

Some industry observers note that Google has so far navigated AI regulations more adeptly than Apple, which has taken a more closed approach to its ecosystem. The Verge recently described Google as "better at playing the AI regulations game," but Friday's order suggests the EU is now demanding deeper concessions.

Criticism and support

The move has drawn sharp criticism from some quarters. The Taxpayer Watchdog group slammed the EU's order as "the latest tech overreach," arguing that it imposes unnecessary costs on a company that already complies with multiple regulatory frameworks.

Supporters of the order, including some smaller tech firms and consumer groups, say it will foster innovation and give users more choice. They argue that Google's dominance in both search and mobile operating systems has created an unfair advantage that stifles competition.

What this means for users

If implemented, Android users in Europe could soon see options to set third-party AI assistants like those from Microsoft, Amazon, or smaller startups as their default. Search data sharing could lead to more accurate alternatives to Google Search, though the impact may take years to materialise.

The order is part of a broader EU push to regulate Big Tech under the Digital Markets Act, which designates Google as a "gatekeeper" subject to stricter rules.

Google is expected to appeal the order. The tech giant has 30 days to propose compliance measures, and the European Commission will assess whether they meet the requirements. The case could take months or years to resolve in European courts.
