Crypto Basics 2026: How It Works, Real Costs, Pros and Cons

What Cryptocurrency Actually Is

Cryptocurrency is a digital asset whose ownership is recorded on a distributed ledger called a blockchain. No bank or government sits in the middle; a network of computers validates each transfer and appends it to a shared record. Bitcoin, launched in 2009, was the first working example, and Ethereum later added programmable contracts that now power most decentralised applications.

The practical takeaway for a buyer: you are not purchasing a file or a coin in a vault. You are holding a cryptographic key that proves control over an entry on a public database. Lose the key and the entry stays, but your access does not.

How a Transaction Actually Settles

When you send crypto, your wallet signs the transfer with your private key and broadcasts it to the network. Miners or validators check the signature, confirm you have the balance, and bundle the transaction into a block. Confirmation times range from a few seconds on Solana to roughly ten minutes on Bitcoin for a first confirmation.

Fees are not fixed. They rise when the network is congested, which is why a Bitcoin transfer that cost a few hundred rupees in a quiet week can cost several thousand during a demand spike. Ethereum's layer-2 rollups have cut typical fees to a fraction of mainnet levels, and most retail activity has migrated there.

Pros and Cons for a Real Buyer

  • Pro: 24x7 global settlement — transfers clear on weekends and across borders without banking hours.
  • Pro: Transparent supply schedules. Bitcoin's issuance is public and auditable, unlike central bank money supply.
  • Pro: Self-custody removes exchange counterparty risk, a lesson repeated after every major platform failure.
  • Con: Extreme volatility. Drawdowns of 50% to 80% have occurred multiple times in a single cycle.
  • Con: Irreversibility. A wrong address or a phishing signature means the money is gone, with no chargeback route.
  • Con: India's tax structure penalises active trading — the 1% TDS alone makes high-frequency strategies unviable.
  • Con: Regulatory uncertainty persists around custody, reporting and offshore platforms.

Pricing Context and Value Justification

Valuation is where most new buyers go wrong. Crypto assets generate no cash flow, so there is no earnings multiple or dividend yield to anchor a price. Value rests on network adoption, scarcity, and the willingness of the next buyer to pay more. That makes position sizing more important than entry timing.

A workable framework for an Indian retail investor is to cap crypto at 1% to 5% of investable assets, buy in tranches rather than lump sums, and treat the allocation as expendable risk capital. Factor the 30% tax and 1% TDS into every exit calculation before you enter, not after.

Practical Benchmarks Before You Buy

Compare total cost, not headline price. Add the exchange spread, withdrawal fee, blockchain fee, and tax to arrive at your true break-even. On a small trade, these can easily exceed 3% of the position. Verify that your exchange publishes proof of reserves, supports INR banking rails, and issues a TDS certificate for filing.

Bottom Line

Cryptocurrency is a genuine settlement innovation wrapped in a highly speculative asset class. The technology works; the returns are not guaranteed. Buyers who understand keys, fees, confirmation times and the Indian tax code will make better decisions than those chasing price alone.
