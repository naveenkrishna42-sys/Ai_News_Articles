Oklahoma Meteor Crater’s Age Revised: New Zircon Dating Shifts Timeline by 100 Million Years

✍️ By TIVRA News Science & Health Desk 📅 2026-10-07 📂 Science Original source ↗
Key points
  • New zircon dating places the crater at roughly 200 million years old, about 100 million years younger than prior estimates.
  • The structure is approximately 16 km in diameter and lies beneath several kilometres of sediment in the Anadarko Basin.
  • The revised age moves the crater closer to the end‑Triassic extinction rather than the Late Devonian event.
  • Implications include re‑evaluating impact‑driven extinction models and guiding future geophysical surveys in the region.

Introduction

Recent research published in a geoscience journal has altered the understood timeline of a concealed meteor impact site beneath Oklahoma. Using uranium‑lead dating on zircon grains extracted from drill cores, scientists determined that the crater formed roughly 200 million years ago, a shift of nearly 100 million years from earlier estimates. This revision has prompted fresh discussion about the crater’s possible role in major biological turnovers.

Geological Setting and Physical Characteristics

The buried structure lies within the Anadarko Basin, a deep sedimentary province known for its oil‑rich reservoirs. Seismic reflections reveal a circular anomaly about 16 kilometres across, with a central uplift and a surrounding rim that has been flattened by overlying strata. Estimates of the original crater depth, before sediment infill, range from 2 to 3 kilometres.

New Zircon Dating Methodology

Researchers isolated zircon crystals from impact‑melt breccia samples recovered from a deep exploration well. The uranium‑lead isotopic system in these crystals resets during the high‑temperature shock of an impact, providing a reliable clock. Laboratory analysis yielded a concordant age of 200 million years with a small analytical uncertainty, indicating a rapid cooling event consistent with an impact rather than prolonged magmatic activity.

Implications for Extinction Links

Earlier hypotheses tied the Oklahoma crater to the Late Devonian extinction (~372 Ma) based on the older age estimate. The new dating places the impact near the end‑Triassic extinction (~201 Ma), one of the ‘Big Five’ mass‑extinction events marked by volcanic activity and possible extraterrestrial contributions. While a causal connection remains unproven, the temporal proximity encourages re‑examination of sedimentary records for impact‑related markers such as shocked quartz or iridium anomalies.

Broader Scientific Impact

The revised age influences models that assess the frequency of large impacts over the Phanerozoic. It also guides exploration strategies in the Anadarko Basin, where companies may now consider impact‑related structural traps when evaluating hydrocarbon prospects. Furthermore, the case underscores the value of applying high‑precision geochronology to buried structures that are otherwise invisible at the surface.

Conclusion

The Oklahoma meteor crater’s age reduction by roughly 100 million years reshapes its geological narrative and its potential relationship to Earth’s most dramatic biological crises. Continued interdisciplinary work—combining drilling, seismic imaging, and isotopic analysis—will be essential to test whether this impact played a direct role in the end‑Triassic turnover or represents a separate, albeit significant, event in planetary history.
