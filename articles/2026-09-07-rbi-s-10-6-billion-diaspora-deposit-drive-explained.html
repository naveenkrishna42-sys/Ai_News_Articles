Why RBI is pursuing record foreign deposits

The Reserve Bank of India is encouraging Non-Resident Indian deposits to strengthen dollar inflows and cushion rupee volatility. The drive targets the diaspora with higher interest and flexible repatriation to attract fresh foreign currency into the banking system. For depositors, the pitch is better yields than domestic fixed deposits and access to rupee-linked returns from abroad.

The move comes as global rate differentials narrow and India seeks stable, non-debt-creating inflows. NRI deposits are classified as external commercial borrowings for banks and help meet funding needs without raising domestic policy rates. Buyers evaluating NRI schemes should compare effective post-tax returns, transfer charges and repatriation limits.

How the $10.6 billion cost is estimated

The Economic Times reported on 7 Sep 2026 that the record push could cost RBI about $10.6 billion. The estimate reflects interest payouts, hedging costs and incentives offered to banks to mobilise foreign currency deposits over a defined tenor.

The cost is not a cash outlay in one year but an accrual over the life of the deposits. It rises with the size of mobilisation, the coupon offered to NRIs and the rupee depreciation path. A longer tenor locks in higher absolute payouts and exposes RBI to forex translation risk.

Real-world impact for depositors and borrowers

For NRIs, the scheme means access to preferential rates on NRO and FCNR deposits and streamlined onboarding. Effective returns improve when rupee depreciation is factored in, but returns are sensitive to RBI rate guidance and bank spreads. Transfer fees, tax on interest and documentation requirements can erode headline rates.

For domestic borrowers, larger foreign inflows ease systemic liquidity pressure and can moderate borrowing costs. For the central bank, the trade-off is cheaper external funding versus contingent liability if outflows reverse quickly. Banks benefit from stable foreign currency liabilities for asset-liability matching.

Risk-reward assessment

The programme supports forex reserves and reduces reliance on volatile portfolio flows. It also signals policy intent to deepen the NRI deposit base as a structural funding source. The main downside is cost escalation if global rates rise and banks pass through higher coupons to NRIs.

  • Pros: Supports rupee stability, provides NRIs higher dollar-linked yields, diversifies funding for banks.
  • Cons: Potential $10.6 billion cost to RBI, exposure to forex depreciation, risk of sudden deposit outflows.
  • Key highlight: Returns depend on tenor, tax treatment and repatriation rules, not just headline rate.
