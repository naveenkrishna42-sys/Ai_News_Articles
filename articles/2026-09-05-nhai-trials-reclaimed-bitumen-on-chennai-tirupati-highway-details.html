NHAI Launches Pilot with Reclaimed Bitumen on NH 716

The National Highways Authority of India (NHAI) has begun a controlled trial of reclaimed bitumen on a defined stretch of the Chennai-Tirupati National Highway (NH 716). The pilot is designed to test whether reprocessed asphalt can deliver durability comparable to fresh mixes while easing the rising cost pressure on highway construction budgets.According to reporting by The Hindu, the trial forms part of a broader push by the Ministry of Road Transport and Highways to integrate recycled materials into core road infrastructure. Engineers involved in the project indicate that reclaimed bitumen, when blended correctly with virgin aggregates, can reduce dependence on imported asphalt binders and lower per-kilometre construction costs.

Why Reclaimed Bitumen Matters for Indian Highways

India produces millions of tonnes of milled bituminous pavement waste every year during resurfacing operations. Most of this material historically ends up as low-grade fill or landfill. Reclaimed bitumen technology flips that model by extracting and rejuvenating the aged binder for reuse in new road layers.For a high-volume corridor like the Chennai-Tirupati route, which carries heavy commercial and pilgrim traffic, the operational and financial stakes are significant. A successful pilot could pave the way for wider adoption across Tier-1 highway projects in Tamil Nadu, Andhra Pradesh, and beyond.

Technical Approach Behind the Trial

The trial reportedly uses a warm-mix asphalt formulation incorporating a measured percentage of reclaimed bitumen. The blending ratio, aggregate grading, and additive package are critical variables because older binder tends to be stiffer and more oxidation-prone than fresh material.

Buyer and Contractor ImplicationsFor infrastructure contractors operating in the South Indian market, the trial carries clear commercial signals. If NHAI codifies reclaimed bitumen specifications into tender documents, EPC bidders will need to invest in milling machines, cold recyclers, and binder rejuvenation units.

For municipalities and smart-city road projects, a successful NHAI pilot would lower the technical risk barrier for adopting recycled asphalt at scale. Procurement teams could see per-lane-kilometre costs drop materially once reclaimed material is treated as a standard input rather than an experimental one.

Potential Cost Benchmarks
  • Virgin hot-mix asphalt cost benchmark: roughly ₹4,500–₹5,500 per cubic metre of compacted mix (subject to binder price volatility).
  • Reclaimed bitumen warm-mix: projected savings of 20–30% on binder cost, translating to roughly ₹900–₹1,500 per cubic metre depending on logistics.
  • Carbon footprint reduction: estimated 15–25% lower CO₂ emissions per lane-kilometre due to lower mixing temperatures.
    • Risks, Limitations, and What to Watch NextReclaimed bitumen is not a drop-in substitute. Poorly calibrated blends can crack prematurely under heavy axle loads, particularly during the summer months when Tamil Nadu surface temperatures cross 55°C. Performance reporting from the pilot stretch will therefore be closely scrutinised.

    Real-World Highlights and Caveats
  • Pros: Lower material cost, reduced landfill pressure, smaller carbon footprint per kilometre, and better alignment with India's circular-economy targets.
  • Pros: Warm-mix placement reduces on-site fuel use and improves worker safety during paving.
  • Cons: Requires specialised milling and blending equipment that smaller contractors may not own.
  • Cons: Quality variance in feedstock (old pavement) can affect consistency unless source material is tightly controlled.
  • Watch: NHAI's post-trial performance audit, expected over the next 12–18 months, will determine whether reclaimed bitumen enters standard tender specifications for national highway packages.
