SCBA Backs Sonam Wangchuk, Flags Silence On Exam Reform Demands

📅 2026-07-16 📂 Analysis Original source ↗
Key points
  • SCBA has publicly backed Sonam Wangchuk's protest for exam reforms, criticising the government's lack of response.
  • Wangchuk's fast has drawn broad political support from leaders like Arvind Kejriwal and farmer leader Rakesh Tikait.
  • The Delhi High Court has intervened, ordering daily medical monitoring for the activist amid health concerns.

A Bar Body Breaks Its Silence

The Supreme Court Bar Association (SCBA) has stepped into a growing confrontation between civil society and the central government. In a statement issued on Thursday, the SCBA expressed solidarity with climate activist and education reform advocate Sonam Wangchuk, who is on an indefinite fast at Delhi's Jantar Mantar.

The association lamented the lack of any meaningful government response to Wangchuk's demands, which centre on changes to the student examination system in Ladakh and the wider Himalayan belt. The SCBA's intervention marks a rare institutional stance from the legal fraternity on an issue that has remained largely outside the courtroom.

Wangchuk, a mechanical engineer who rose to fame as the inspiration behind the film 3 Idiots, has been protesting for over two weeks. His core demand: that the Ladakh administration and the central government scrap the current board exam pattern and introduce a more flexible, locally relevant evaluation system for students in remote regions.

The Protest That Refuses to Fade

What began as a one-man sit-in has swelled into a multi-party platform. On Wednesday, farmer leader Rakesh Tikait joined the protest, claiming that over 500 farmers had come to support Wangchuk. Delhi's former chief minister Arvind Kejriwal also visited Jantar Mantar, calling Wangchuk 'the conscience of a nation' and suggesting he should be made the education minister.

Such endorsements have turned the protest into a political flashpoint. The ruling Bharatiya Janata Party has so far avoided a direct comment on Wangchuk's demands, while local BJP leaders in Ladakh have accused him of being a 'tool of separatists'. Wangchuk has denied any political affiliation, insisting his protest is purely about student welfare.

The numbers at the site remain modest — a few hundred supporters on most days — but the symbolic weight is considerable. Wangchuk's image as a non-political, grassroots innovator has made him a difficult target for either dismissal or co-option.

Court Steps In as Health Worsens

The legal dimension escalated sharply this week. With Wangchuk's health deteriorating — he has been on a liquid-only diet for over a week — the Delhi High Court intervened on its own motion. On Wednesday, a bench ordered that Wangchuk receive daily medical monitoring at a government hospital. The court directed that 'necessary treatment' be administered, adding that 'every life is precious'.

This is not the first time the High Court has had to step in. Earlier this month, it had directed the Delhi Police to ensure Wangchuk's protest remained peaceful and that he was not forcibly removed. The police have so far allowed the protest to continue, but have restricted the area around Jantar Mantar.

Legal experts point out that the court's intervention does not extend to the substance of Wangchuk's demands. The judiciary is only ensuring his right to life and health, not adjudicating on education policy. However, the repeated judicial nods have given the protest a legitimacy that a purely political agitation might not have commanded.

Why This Matters Beyond Jantar Mantar

At its core, Wangchuk's protest is about a structural flaw in India's examination system. Students in Ladakh, many of whom study in remote schools with limited resources, are assessed on the same national curriculum and exam pattern as students in cities like Delhi or Mumbai. Wangchuk argues that this creates a built-in disadvantage, pushing many students to drop out.

His demand for a 'flexible examination system' — where students can choose subjects based on local needs and learning speeds — is not new. Similar demands have been raised by activists in the Northeast, Jharkhand and parts of the Himalayan belt for years. What is new is that a figure of Wangchuk's stature has put his life on the line for it.

The SCBA's backing adds institutional weight. The association's statement noted that the government's silence on the matter was 'deeply concerning' and called for an immediate dialogue. It remains to be seen whether the Law Ministry or the Education Ministry will respond.

Meanwhile, the Delhi High Court has scheduled a follow-up hearing for next week to review Wangchuk's medical reports. The protest continues, and the countdown on his fast is ticking.

What to watch: Whether the government will open a formal channel of communication, or whether the courts will be forced to expand their role beyond medical monitoring into the realm of policy adjudication. The next 48 hours could determine the trajectory of a protest that has already outlasted many expectations.
