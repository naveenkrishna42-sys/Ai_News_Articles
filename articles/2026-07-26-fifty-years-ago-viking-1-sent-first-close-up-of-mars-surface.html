Fifty Years Ago: Viking 1 Sent First Close-Up of Mars Surface

📅 2026-07-26 📂 Science Original source ↗
Key points
  • Viking 1 became the first spacecraft to land on Mars on July 20, 1976.
  • A recently released image from the mission shows a rock with marks resembling the Roman letter 'B'.
  • The image was shared to mark the 50th anniversary of the historic landing.

Fifty years ago this week, humanity got its first close-up look at the surface of Mars. On July 20, 1976, NASA's Viking 1 spacecraft touched down on the red planet, sending back images that reshaped our understanding of our planetary neighbour.

To mark the golden anniversary, a newly highlighted photograph from that mission is making headlines again. It shows a rock on the Martian surface that bears markings strikingly similar to the Roman letter 'B'. The image, released by space agencies and science outlets, has sparked fresh interest in the mission that paved the way for every Mars rover that followed.

A Rock That Looks Like a Letter

The photograph, taken by Viking 1's lander camera, captures a rocky patch of the Chryse Planitia region. Among the scattered stones, one rock stands out. Its surface is etched with a pattern that, from a certain angle, reads like the letter 'B'. Scientists have long dismissed it as a natural geological formation — wind erosion or chemical weathering creating a random shape. But the resemblance is uncanny enough to draw public fascination.

This is not the first time Mars has teased us with familiar shapes. The so-called 'Face on Mars', captured by Viking 1 in a different photograph, sparked decades of speculation before higher-resolution images revealed it as a mesa. The 'Roman letter' rock is far less controversial, but it serves as a reminder of how eager we are to find patterns in the alien landscape.

The Landing That Changed Everything

Viking 1's landing was a landmark achievement in space exploration. Launched on August 20, 1975, it travelled nearly 700 million kilometres to reach Mars. Its descent was fraught with risk — the landing sequence was automated, and mission controllers on Earth could only wait. When the signal confirming a safe touchdown arrived, it was a moment of triumph for NASA.

The lander operated for over six years, far exceeding its planned 90-day mission. It beamed back the first high-resolution images of the Martian surface, analysed soil samples, and searched for signs of life. The results were inconclusive, but the data laid the groundwork for every subsequent mission, from Pathfinder to Perseverance.

On the 50th anniversary, space agencies and science publications have been sharing images from the mission. The 'Roman letter' rock photograph was featured as a 'space photo of the week' by Live Science and highlighted by The Planetary Society among its 'portraits of the red planet'.

What the Image Tells Us

The rock itself is unremarkable in size — likely a few centimetres across. Its 'B' shape is formed by a series of grooves and ridges. Scientists have not found evidence that it is anything but a natural curiosity. No official statement has been released suggesting otherwise.

But the image serves a larger purpose. It reminds us how far we have come. From the grainy black-and-white photos of Viking 1 to the 4K panoramas sent back by Perseverance, the leap in technology is staggering. Yet the sense of wonder remains the same.

The Viking 1 mission also carried a biological experiment designed to detect microbial life. It returned positive results from one test, but other tests were negative, leaving the question of life on Mars unanswered. That mystery continues to drive exploration today.

As we look back at the first close-up images of Mars, we also look forward. The next generation of missions, including sample-return efforts, may finally answer the questions Viking 1 raised half a century ago. For now, a rock that looks like a letter is a fitting symbol of our enduring curiosity about the red planet.
