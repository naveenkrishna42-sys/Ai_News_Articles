Balaghat Child Deaths: CJP Convenor Abhijeet Dipke Visits Tribal Villages, Demands Accountability

Abhijeet Dipke, convenor of the Citizens for Justice and Peace (CJP), visited tribal villages in Madhya Pradesh's Balaghat district on Saturday, demanding accountability for the deaths of more than 30 Adivasi children. His visit follows widespread reports of appalling vaccination levels and chronic hunger among the tribal population. The Madhya Pradesh High Court has already directed the state government to respond to a ground report, signalling growing legal and public pressure.

For buyers and observers of India's rural welfare schemes, this case highlights critical gaps in healthcare delivery and nutrition programmes. The deaths, concentrated in remote tribal hamlets, expose systemic failures that demand urgent policy correction.

What Happened in Balaghat?

Over 30 children, primarily from the Baiga and Gond tribal communities, have died in Balaghat over recent months. According to reports from The Hindu, Telegraph India, and Scroll.in, the fatalities are linked to severe malnutrition, low immunisation coverage, and inadequate access to primary health centres. Dipke's visit aimed to document ground realities and press authorities for action.

During his visit, Dipke was reportedly heckled by social media influencers, an incident that underscores the charged atmosphere around the tragedy. Despite the disruption, he met affected families and reiterated demands for a transparent inquiry.

Why This Matters: Buyer and Policy Implications

For stakeholders in public health and rural development, Balaghat is a case study in the cost of neglecting last-mile delivery. The district's tribal belts receive some of the lowest per-capita health spending in Madhya Pradesh. Vaccination drives often stall due to difficult terrain, shortage of mobile health units, and community mistrust.

Nutrition programmes like ICDS and Poshan Abhiyaan exist on paper, but ground reports indicate irregular supply of take-home rations and absence of anganwadi workers. Fixing these gaps requires not just more funds but better monitoring and community engagement.

Real-World Benchmarks: What Works Elsewhere

  • Odisha's tribal health model: Mobile health units and tribal health workers have improved immunisation rates in Malkangiri and Koraput.
  • Kerala's decentralised approach: Local self-governments manage primary health centres with high accountability.
  • Chhattisgarh's Mitanin programme:

    Community health volunteers bridge trust gaps in remote villages.

Pros and Cons of Current Interventions

  • Pros:
    • Judicial intervention via High Court ensures state accountability.
    • Civil society visits, like Dipke's, spotlight neglected regions.
    • Existing schemes (ICDS, NHM) provide a ready framework for improvement.
  • Cons:
    • Vaccination coverage remains abysmally low in tribal hamlets.
    • Political slugfests and heckling distract from urgent relief.
    • Ground reports often lack follow-up action, delaying justice.

What Happens Next?

The MP High Court's directive to the state to respond to the ground report could force a formal inquiry. CJP has demanded a judicial probe and compensation for affected families. Whether the state acts swiftly will determine if Balaghat becomes a turning point for tribal health in Madhya Pradesh—or another missed warning.

For now, the focus remains on Dipke's visit and the pressing question: how many more children must die before accountability is delivered?
