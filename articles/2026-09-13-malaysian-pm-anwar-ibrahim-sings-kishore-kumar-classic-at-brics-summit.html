Cultural Diplomacy on Display

During the BRICS Summit in New Delhi on September 13, 2026, Malaysian Prime Minister Anwar Ibrahim surprised attendees by rendering Kishore Kumar’s 1962 classic ‘Khwab Ho Tum’.

The Strategic Subtext of a Song

Anwar's choice of a Kishore Kumar standard from the early 1960s was not merely a nostalgic flourish. It was a carefully calibrated diplomatic signal delivered in a venue designed to showcase multipolar alignment. Malaysia has long positioned itself as a bridge between the Global South and major powers, and the BRICS platform—expanded in 2024 to include new members—offers an increasingly attractive stage for that positioning.

India, as host, had every reason to welcome the gesture. Cultural affinity between India and Malaysia runs deep, shaped by generations of Tamil and broader South Asian migration that now constitutes roughly seven percent of Malaysia's population. Indian films, music, and television have been a fixture of Malaysian popular culture for decades, particularly among the Indian diaspora but increasingly across ethnic lines. Anwar, a seasoned orator known for his command of multiple languages and cultural references, understood that a Hindi film song would resonate simultaneously in the summit hall, in the Indian press, and back home among Malaysian voters who value cultural fluency in their leadership.

The timing also mattered. The summit agenda included discussions on trade facilitation, digital currency cooperation, and food security—areas where Malaysia and India share convergent interests but also compete for foreign direct investment and manufacturing relocation. A warm cultural overture can lower the temperature on contentious economic files without requiring any formal concession.

Malaysia–India Relations: The Broader Context

Bilateral ties have oscillated over the past decade. Diplomatic friction emerged in 2019–2020 over remarks concerning India's citizenship law and the situation in Kashmir, leading to a temporary downturn in palm oil trade and investment flows. Since Anwar's ascent to the premiership in late 2022, both capitals have worked to reset the relationship, emphasizing economic complementarity and people-to-people links.

Trade data reflects the recovery. India is among Malaysia's top ten trading partners, while Malaysia ranks similarly for India within ASEAN. The two countries have deepened cooperation in semiconductors, renewable energy, and Islamic finance, and both are members of the broader Indo-Pacific economic conversation without being formal treaty allies of each other.

Key Areas of Engagement

  • Trade and investment: Palm oil, electronics, petrochemicals, and halal products dominate Malaysian exports to India; India exports refined petroleum, chemicals, and machinery.
  • Defence and security: Joint exercises, maritime domain awareness cooperation, and counterterrorism intelligence sharing have expanded.
  • Digital and fintech: Both nations are exploring cross-border payment linkages and central bank digital currency interoperability under BRICS working groups.
  • Education and diaspora: Malaysian students in India and Indian students in Malaysia form a steady exchange, supported by scholarship schemes and university partnerships.

What the Performance Achieved

Cultural diplomacy of this kind is rarely decisive on its own, but it can create political space. By singing in Hindi at a multilateral summit, Anwar accomplished several things at once:

  • Signaled cultural respect to India, reinforcing the reset in bilateral ties after years of friction.
  • Appealed to domestic constituencies—particularly Malaysian Indians, who constitute a significant voting bloc in several states.
  • Projected Malaysia as a culturally confident middle power comfortable navigating Asian, Islamic, and Global South identities simultaneously.
  • Humanized the summit, cutting through the protocol-heavy atmosphere and generating the kind of viral moment that traditional communiqués cannot produce.

Takeaways

  • Soft power remains a real instrument in a multipolar world, especially when hard economic interests are stalled or competitive.
  • Anwar's personal style—multilingual, literary, and performative—continues to shape Malaysia's foreign policy brand.
  • India benefits from such gestures as it seeks to consolidate influence across the Global South and within an expanded BRICS.
  • Expect more of this: as summits multiply and media attention fragments, leaders will increasingly use cultural moments to signal alignment and warmth.
  • Substance still matters. The song opens doors; trade agreements, defence pacts, and payment linkages are what walk through them.

In the end, a sixty-four-year-old Bollywood melody became a small but telling chapter in the ongoing story of how Asian nations court one another—through memory, music, and the quiet arithmetic of mutual interest.
