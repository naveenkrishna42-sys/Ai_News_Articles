Public Examinations Amendment Bill 2026: Anti-cheating law promises and pitfalls

📅 2026-07-28 📂 Analysis Original source ↗
Key points
  • The Bill introduces life imprisonment and a Special Task Force for exam paper leaks.
  • It mandates a centralised database for all public examination bodies.
  • Educationists argue it infringes on states' rights and duplicates existing anti-cheating laws.

Context and background

For years, India's public examination system—from the Union Public Service Commission (UPSC) to state board tests—has been plagued by paper leaks and organised cheating rings. The 2022 Rajasthan constable recruitment paper leak, the 2023 NEET-UG irregularities, and the 2024 Gujarat board leak are only a few examples. Each incident derailed lakhs of students' careers and cost the exchequer crores in re-exams.

The Public Examinations (Prevention of Unfair Means) Amendment Bill 2026, introduced in the Lok Sabha on Monday, seeks to plug these gaps. It proposes sweeping changes: life imprisonment for those found guilty of leaking papers, a dedicated Special Task Force (STF) to investigate offences, and a centralised digital database to track all public examination bodies.

The Bill arrives after a week-long parliamentary deadlock that stalled other business. With the government calling it a priority, the Lok Sabha is expected to take it up for debate today.

What the Bill promises

The draft legislation amends the original 2024 Act, which itself was enacted after a spate of high-profile leaks. The new version introduces a graded penalty structure. For conspiracy to leak papers or impersonate candidates, the maximum punishment has been raised to life imprisonment and a fine of up to Rs 1 crore. For those who abet or organise such crimes, the minimum sentence is five years.

A central provision is the creation of a national STF under the Ministry of Home Affairs. This body will have the power to investigate and prosecute cases across state borders, overriding local police jurisdictions. The Bill also compels all examination-conducting agencies—from the National Testing Agency to state public service commissions—to register on a unified IT platform. The platform will store question paper creation trails, invigilation logs, and result data in real time.

“The Bill is a response to the growing sophistication of cheating networks,” a senior Ministry of Law official told reporters. “We need a centralised mechanism to break the syndicates that operate across states.”

The government has stressed that the law targets organised crime, not individual students. Cases of minor cheating, such as copying in the exam hall, will remain under existing penal codes.

Why it matters

Indian public exams are the gateway to government jobs, medical seats, and engineering colleges. A single leak can affect millions of candidates. The 2023 NEET-UG leak, for instance, led to a Supreme Court-monitored re-test for 11 lakh students. The social cost is immense: families invest years of savings into coaching, and trust in the system erodes with every leak.

The Bill’s proponents argue that a strong deterrence is long overdue. Life imprisonment for paper leaks, they say, will force syndicates to think twice. The STF, with its cross-border powers, can dismantle networks that currently exploit jurisdictional gaps.

Yet implementation remains the core challenge. The 2024 Act already made paper leaks a cognisable, non-bailable offence. Despite that, leaks continued. ThePrint editorial board has noted: “Not new amendments, India needs a sincere implementation of existing cheating law.” The question is whether adding more layers of legislation will fix a systemic failure in enforcement.

Criticism and constitutional questions

Educationists and constitutional experts have flagged the STF clause as a potential infringement on states’ rights. Public examinations are a concurrent subject, but law and order is a state matter. “The STF clause in exam fraud bill is an infringement on states’ rights,” the Telegraph India quoted several educationists as saying. They argue that the central government cannot unilaterally create a force that bypasses state police without amending the Constitution.

Other critics point to duplication. The Indian Penal Code and the Information Technology Act already cover cheating, forgery, and cyber fraud. A separate law with overlapping provisions could lead to confusion and legal battles. “We need a sincere implementation of existing cheating law,” the ThePrint editorial said.

There is also the question of scope. The Bill targets “public examinations” but does not clearly define them. Private university entrance tests and recruitment exams by public sector undertakings may fall into a grey area. The centralised database raises privacy concerns: who controls the data, and what safeguards exist against misuse?

Some lawmakers have demanded that the Bill be sent to a parliamentary standing committee for scrutiny. The government has not yet indicated whether it will agree.

As the Lok Sabha debates the Bill today, the outcome will signal how far the government is willing to go to protect exam integrity—and at what cost to federal principles.
