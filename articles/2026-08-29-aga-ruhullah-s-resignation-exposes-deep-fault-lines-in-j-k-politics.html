Aga Ruhullah's Resignation Exposes Deep Fault Lines in J&K Politics

Key points
  • Aga Ruhullah's resignation signals fundamental disagreement with NC's approach on Article 370 restoration
  • Farooq Abdullah's dare-and-accept confrontation suggests intra-party dissent has reached breaking point
  • The resignation could reshape opposition dynamics in Kashmir ahead of future electoral battles

The Challenge That Forced a Reckoning

When veteran leader Farooq Abdullah dared Aga Ruhullah Mehdi to resign from parliament and quit the National Conference, few expected the Srinagar MP to actually accept. But that is precisely what appears to have happened. Mehdi's reported decision to step down on November 3 has thrown Kashmir's political establishment into turmoil and exposed a fundamental contradiction at the heart of the region's dominant party.

The trigger is Article 370. What was once the constitutional backbone of Jammu and Kashmir's semi-autonomous status was revoked in August 2019, fundamentally altering the region's relationship with New Delhi. The National Conference, which built decades of its political identity around the preservation and restoration of special status, now finds itself accused of quietly accepting the new reality.

What's Really Behind the Split?

Mehdi's challenge cuts to the core question haunting Kashmir politics: does opposition to New Delhi's 2019 decision mean anything substantive, or has it become mere rhetorical posturing? Sources suggest the Srinagar MP believes the NC leadership has effectively capitulated, pursuing electoral participation and administrative accommodation while abandoning the restoration of Article 370 as a live political demand.

This criticism is not entirely new. Younger party members and independent observers have long argued that the NC's continued engagement with the governance structure established after Article 370's deletion amounts to de facto acceptance. Mehdi's resignation threat elevates this internal critique into a public rupture.

Does the Criticism Hold Up?

On the surface, the NC's position deserves scrutiny. The party continues to contest elections, field candidates for assembly and parliamentary seats, and engage with New Delhi on various administrative matters. Critics argue this participation normalizes the post-2019 arrangement and undermines any genuine opposition narrative.

However, the alternative is equally problematic. Complete political withdrawal would leave Kashmir's electorate without a mainstream alternative and cede the political space entirely to New Delhi's preferred representatives. The NC's dilemma reflects a broader challenge faced by regional parties in the new constitutional environment: how to remain politically relevant while maintaining principled opposition to fundamental changes in the region's status.

Implications for Kashmir's Opposition Landscape

Mehdi's departure creates a significant vacuum. As an MP with demonstrated electoral appeal, his exit weakens the NC's parliamentary presence and gives ammunition to rivals who argue the party cannot sustain coherent opposition. Whether Mehdi launches an independent political movement or aligns with another formation, his departure signals that the consensus on Article 370 within the NC has finally fractured beyond repair.

The timing is particularly awkward for the party leadership. With no clear resolution to the Article 370 question in sight, and New Delhi showing no indication of reversing course, the NC faces mounting pressure from both ideological purists demanding confrontation and pragmatists arguing for measured engagement.

Broader Significance

This episode reveals something important about the limits of electoral politics in post-Article 370 Kashmir. The NC's struggle to define its opposition to the 2019 changes while remaining a functional political entity reflects a fundamental tension that cannot be easily resolved through party conferences or parliamentary tactics. Mehdi's resignation, whatever form it ultimately takes, underscores that for significant sections of Kashmir's political class, the accommodation with New Delhi has become impossible to accept.
