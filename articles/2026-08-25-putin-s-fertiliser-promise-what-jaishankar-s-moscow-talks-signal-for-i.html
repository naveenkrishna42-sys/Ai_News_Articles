Fertiliser Promises Mask Deeper Imbalances

Russian President Vladimir Putin's assurance to External Affairs Minister S. Jaishankar about continuing and expanding fertiliser supplies to India sounds reassuring on paper. But a closer reading of the discussions during Jaishankar's Moscow visit reveals a relationship still skewed heavily in Moscow's favour. The fertiliser narrative dominates headlines, yet India's persistent trade deficit with Russia continues to widen quietly in the background.

During the meeting, Putin reportedly expressed hope about seeing Prime Minister Narendra Modi "soon as we planned" — a reference to a visit that appears to have been delayed or rescheduled. Whether this reflects scheduling conflicts or more substantive diplomatic hesitation remains unclear. What is evident is that both sides are eager to project normalcy in a relationship that has grown increasingly complex since 2022.

Why Fertiliser Supply Matters

Fertiliser imports from Russia have become strategically critical for Indian agriculture. With global supply chains disrupted and Western sanctions complicating traditional trade routes, India turned to Russia as a reliable backup supplier. The promised increase in fertiliser shipments would help keep input costs stable for millions of farmers ahead of the kharif season.

However, this dependency cuts both ways. India's fertiliser subsidy bill runs into tens of thousands of crore annually. Any disruption in Russian supplies — whether due to logistics, pricing, or geopolitical flare-ups — would immediately impact food inflation and farmer incomes. The government has been trying to diversify sources, but Russia remains indispensable in the short term.

The Elephant in the Room: Trade Deficit

Despite the diplomatic warmth, Jaishankar again raised India's large trade deficit with Russia during the meeting. India imports far more from Russia than it exports — crude oil, fertilisers, and defence equipment dominate the import basket. Exports, meanwhile, remain modest. This asymmetry troubles New Delhi, which wants a more balanced commercial relationship.

Indian pharmaceutical companies, automobile manufacturers, and IT firms see Russia as an untapped market. But payment difficulties arising from sanctions-related banking restrictions have deterred many businesses. Until this infrastructure issue is resolved, the trade gap will likely persist.

Geopolitical Tightrope

India's position remains delicate. It has maintained ties with Moscow while simultaneously deepening engagement with Western partners. The United States and Europe have watched India's Russia trade with concern but stopped short of imposing secondary sanctions. Jaishankar's visit, coming shortly after diplomatic engagements with Western capitals, signals India's continued refusal to abandon its traditional partner.

The fertiliser agreement is also a political statement. It shows India can secure essential commodities from non-Western sources, reducing vulnerability to any future disruptions. Whether this pragmatism translates into long-term strategic benefits depends on how effectively New Delhi manages the relationship's economic imbalances.

What Comes Next

A Modi visit to Moscow would likely finalise several pending agreements, including expanded fertiliser supply contracts and mechanisms to address the payment bottleneck. It would also provide an opportunity to reset the trade narrative. However, without concrete steps to boost Indian exports, the relationship risks becoming purely transactional — India buys Russian goods, Russia provides diplomatic pleasantries.

The question is whether both sides can move beyond the fertiliser headline and address the structural issues plaguing the relationship. Jaishankar's frank flagging of the trade deficit suggests India expects more than goodwill from its oldest strategic partner.
