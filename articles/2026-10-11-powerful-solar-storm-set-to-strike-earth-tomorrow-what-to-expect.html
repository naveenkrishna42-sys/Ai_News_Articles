Powerful Solar Storm Set to Strike Earth Tomorrow – What to Expect

Overview of the Forecasted Solar Storm

Space weather agencies have issued a watch for a moderate to strong geomagnetic storm expected to impact Earth on 11 October 2026. The disturbance originates from two coronal mass ejections (CMEs) that erupted from the Sun’s western limb on 7 and 8 October. Current modelling by NOAA’s Space Weather Prediction Center (SWPC) predicts arrival late on 10 October, with the storm’s intensity peaking during the early hours of 11 October.

Origin: Two Coronal Mass Ejections from the Sun

The first CME, observed on 7 October, had an estimated plane‑of‑sky speed of ~750 km/s and a moderate angular width. The second, more energetic eruption on 8 October, measured roughly 900 km/s and displayed a broader halo signature. When these structures interact in interplanetary space, they can combine to produce a denser, faster-moving shock front that enhances the southward component of the interplanetary magnetic field (Bz), a key driver of geomagnetic activity.

Expected Geomagnetic Response and Aurora Visibility

SWPC’s forecast indexes the impending disturbance at Kp 6‑7, corresponding to a G2‑G3 (moderate to strong) storm on the NOAA G‑scale. Such levels typically push the auroral oval equatorward to magnetic latitudes around 50°, meaning the northern lights could be seen from southern England, parts of northern Germany, and as far south as northern India’s higher elevations (e.g., Ladakh, Himachal Pradesh). Visibility will depend heavily on local cloud cover and ambient light pollution.

Potential Impacts on Satellite, Power and Communication Systems

Moderate‑strong storms can induce geomagnetically induced currents (GICs) in long conductors such as power‑grid transformers and pipeline networks. Operators in high‑latitude regions may experience voltage fluctuations requiring reactive power adjustments. Low‑Earth‑orbit satellites face increased atmospheric drag, which can shorten mission lifespans if not corrected via orbital maneuvers. High‑frequency (HF) radio propagation may suffer degradation, affecting aviation and maritime communications over polar routes.

Mitigation Steps and Public Guidance

Utilities are advised to monitor real‑time GIC measurements and, where feasible, schedule load‑shifting or transformer tapping to reduce stress. Satellite operators should consider postponing non‑essential maneuvers and ensure onboard fault‑protection systems are active. For the general public, the storm poses no direct health risk; the main observable effect will be enhanced auroral displays. Those wishing to view the lights should seek dark, clear skies away from city glare and check local aurora forecasts for timing updates.
