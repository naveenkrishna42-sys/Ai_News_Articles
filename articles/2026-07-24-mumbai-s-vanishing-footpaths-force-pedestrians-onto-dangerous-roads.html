Mumbai's vanishing footpaths force pedestrians onto dangerous roads

📅 2026-07-24 📂 Mumbai Original source ↗
Key points
  • Footpaths in Mumbai are blocked by hawkers, parked vehicles, and debris, leaving no space for pedestrians.
  • Residents in areas like Andheri and Dadar report dangerous walks, with some forced onto busy roads.
  • BMC has launched drives but lacks sustained enforcement, while pedestrians call for long-term solutions.

Footpaths become no-go zones

Mumbai's footpaths are vanishing. What was once a basic pedestrian right is now a luxury. Encroachments — from hawker stalls to parked two-wheelers and construction debris — have turned pavements into obstacle courses. In many parts of the city, walking is an extreme sport.

Residents in Andheri West say the footpath near the station is completely blocked. Commuters spill onto the road, dodging autorickshaws and buses. A local shopkeeper said the situation worsens during peak hours. Pedestrians have no choice but to take risks.

Hawkers and vehicles win the space war

Hawkers are often blamed, but the problem is bigger. Footpaths double as parking lots for cars and bikes. In Dadar, a stretch near the market has no clear walking space. Garbage piles and broken tiles add to the mess. The Brihanmumbai Municipal Corporation (BMC) has conducted clearance drives, but they rarely last.

An official from the BMC's roads department admitted that enforcement is weak. Hawkers get licenses, but many operate beyond permitted areas. Residents say the civic body turns a blind eye until complaints pile up. Then a drive happens, only for the status quo to return within weeks.

Pedestrians demand long-term fixes

Walkers in areas like Bandra and Marine Drive also struggle. Even in posh localities, footpaths are uneven or occupied by parked vehicles. Senior citizens and children find it hardest. Many avoid walking altogether. A resident of Khar said she now takes a rickshaw for a 10-minute walk because the footpath is unsafe.

The BMC has a footpath policy, but implementation is patchy. Activists say the city needs dedicated pedestrian lanes and stricter fines for encroachments. Some wards have installed bollards to prevent parking, but not everywhere. The problem is acute in market areas and near railway stations.

What needs to change

Mumbai has over 12 crore daily commuters, many of whom walk. Yet, footpaths are treated as afterthoughts. Urban planners argue that pedestrian infrastructure should be prioritised. The BMC's own surveys show that many footpaths are less than a metre wide in busy areas. That is not enough for two people to pass.

The city's development plan includes wider footpaths and dedicated hawking zones. But progress is slow. Residents want the BMC to conduct regular audits and impose penalties on violators. They also want hawkers to be given legal spaces so pavements remain free.

For now, Mumbaikars will keep walking — if they can find the path. The next few months will show whether the BMC's latest drive yields lasting change, or if it's just another temporary fix.
