What commuters in Delhi are facing: travel disruption meets payment downtime

Rail passengers in Delhi are reporting a double hit during the protest-related curbs linked to CJP—no trains on certain routes and patchy internet access that affects UPI payments. When either component fails, travellers can improvise; when both fail together, the impact becomes immediate: missed boarding windows, inability to pay for food or transport, and long queues at stations.

UPI is widely used across India for quick, low-friction payments. But the user experience assumes stable connectivity—mobile data or a working network path for app-to-bank authorization. If internet services degrade due to local restrictions, congestion, or network throttling, UPI transactions can fail even when users have sufficient balance.

For rail travellers, that fragility matters because stations often demand payments on short notice: platform taxis, last-mile autorickshaws, station kiosks, bottled water, and change requests when vendors run out of cash. A payment delay turns into missed time and rising stress—especially for women, senior citizens, and families travelling with children.

Why UPI depends on internet, and what it means at station level

UPI transactions typically require the payment app to connect to payment rails and then reach the user’s bank account for authorization. Even if the bank app is accessible, the UPI flow still needs data connectivity. In field conditions—crowded platforms, weak signal pockets, or curbs that affect mobile network activity—users may see repeated “transaction pending” or “unable to process” errors.

From a buyer’s perspective (commuter’s decision-making is also a “buying” decision: choosing what to carry and what payment method to rely on), this creates a reliability gap. The commuter has no control over network disruptions, and ATMs may be unavailable due to downtime, restrictions, or simply limited cash replenishment during disruptions.

There’s also a secondary effect: vendors who accept UPI may switch to cash when they see transaction failures. If passengers arrive with only UPI-based habits, they get forced into last-minute cash withdrawals—often at higher friction or longer queues.

Practical risk benchmarks for commuters during disruptions

Based on common on-ground travel patterns, the key question is not whether UPI is “good,” but whether a passenger can complete basic transactions within minutes under constrained connectivity. As a benchmark, commuters should assume UPI reliability drops when mobile data is unstable or network coverage is uneven across stations.

A workable contingency plan is measurable: travellers should have at least one “offline-leaning” or “low-connectivity” payment route, plus enough cash for short-duration purchases. The goal is to avoid cascading failures—train cancellations forcing longer waits, which then triggers additional spontaneous spending.

For value justification, compare the cost of redundancy (small cash reserve, an additional payment option, or an offline-capable wallet) against the losses of a few hours stranded: missed hotel check-ins, food queues, and transport reroutes. The savings come from preventing transaction dead-ends.

Key real-world impacts: who gets hurt first, and how

When “no trains, no internet” converge, the most affected are passengers with limited buffers—those who did not plan for delays, travellers with small children, and commuters who have to reach workplaces or return to homes by fixed schedules. Elderly passengers are also more likely to face navigation and verification friction in app-based payment flows.

Families feel the cost differently: if UPI stops, every purchase becomes a negotiation for cash, change, or a waiting game. For students and daily wage earners, the inability to pay quickly can translate into lost earnings or delayed return trips.

Additionally, digital payment failures can amplify anger around cancellations. Even if train services return, disrupted payment flows can leave passengers with temporary uncertainty about debits or pending confirmations, increasing frustration at already crowded stations.

What to do before you travel (a buyer-style checklist for commuters)

  • Carry a small cash reserve for essentials (water/tea/short transport) when network access is uncertain.
  • Keep at least one non-UPI payment option active (prepaid instruments or card access), and test whether it works in the station area before relying on it.
  • Download critical tickets and offline boarding/route info, because disruptions often create last-minute rerouting.
  • Update phone network basics: ensure your app is logged in, permissions are granted, and you have sufficient balance across relevant accounts.
  • Use UPI only when connectivity is stable; if transactions show “pending,” avoid repeated attempts that can create confusion.

Policy and operational takeaways for authorities and rail operators

For rail operators and local administrators, this episode highlights a systems problem: transport continuity and payment continuity are linked in passenger experience. When curbs reduce connectivity, the public assumes digital payments will still function, leading to a sharper sense of helplessness when they don’t.

Operationally, stations can reduce harm by ensuring cash-ready counters or clearly communicated alternatives during disruptions. Better signage about network limitations, quick-help desk support, and guidance for passengers on payment failures can prevent escalation.

For commuters, resilience comes from redundancy. UPI remains a strong default for routine travel, but disruptions demand a “plan B” mindset—especially in dense urban transit hubs where network conditions can change quickly.
