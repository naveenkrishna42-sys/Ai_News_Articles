SCO Forum: Pakistan's 'Two Rulers, One Fort' History Play

✍️ By TIVRA News Editorial Desk 📅 2026-11-22 📂 Top Stories Original source ↗
Key points
  • The first SCO Civilisational Dialogue Forum was inaugurated by PM Modi in New Delhi, with Pakistan's minister invoking Humayun and Sher Shah Suri as joint claimants to Delhi's Purana Qila.
  • Itinerary clearances were calibrated: the Taj Mahal visit was approved while a requested stop at the Nizamuddin Dargah was not.
  • The cultural track creates recurring, low-stakes contact between estranged SCO members but does not move trade, airspace or border disputes.

A History Lesson Delivered as Diplomacy

Prime Minister Narendra Modi inaugurated the first SCO Civilisational Dialogue Forum in New Delhi on Saturday, formally opening a cultural track that the Shanghai Cooperation Organisation has discussed for years without giving it a standing venue. Pakistan's representative at the forum used his address to reach back five centuries, invoking Humayun and Sher Shah Suri under a single phrase: 'two rulers, one fort.'

The reference is to Purana Qila, the hexagonal citadel in the heart of the Indian capital. Humayun founded the site as Dinpanah in the 1530s. Sher Shah Suri, who drove him out in 1540, demolished parts of the enclosure and rebuilt it as Shergarh, leaving behind the Qila-i-Kuhna mosque that still stands. Two emperors who spent years fighting each other ended up sharing one archaeological footprint.

The minister also called for dialogue and mutual respect among diverse identities — language that reads as boilerplate until you notice who is saying it, and where.

Why Purana Qila Is a Safe, Useful Metaphor

Civilisational rhetoric lets a visiting minister claim a shared inheritance without conceding a political point. Humayun and Sher Shah are not contested figures in the way Partition-era leaders are; both sit in Indian and Pakistani syllabi, and both left monuments that are physically in Delhi. The metaphor costs nothing and signals willingness to talk.

It also quietly reframes the SCO's cultural agenda. The organisation's ten member states — India, Pakistan, China, Russia, Kazakhstan, Kyrgyzstan, Tajikistan, Uzbekistan, Iran and Belarus — share Silk Road geography but not historical narratives. A pre-colonial, pre-national frame sidesteps the arguments that usually stall joint statements.

The Itinerary Signals Are the Real Story

Reported clearances around the visit were as instructive as the speech itself. The delegation's trip to Agra's Taj Mahal was approved; a requested stop at the Nizamuddin Dargah was not. That pattern is standard for incoming dignitaries: ticketed heritage sites with controlled access are straightforward, while living religious shrines bring crowd management, protocol and political-sensitivity variables that security agencies prefer to avoid.

Read together, the approved and declined stops describe a deliberately calibrated visit — enough cultural engagement to justify attendance at the forum, not enough unscripted public contact to create a second news cycle.
