RBI proposes NBFC revolving credit ban; sector seeks selective curbs

📅 2026-08-11 📂 Banking Original source ↗
Key points
  • RBI has formally proposed a ban on revolving credit facilities for NBFCs, following a two-year advisory.
  • The proposal has sparked pushback from the sector, which is asking for selective restrictions instead of a blanket ban.
  • The move aims to curb riskier lending practices, but industry warns of unintended consequences for credit flow.

RBI's two-year warning turns into formal proposal

The Reserve Bank of India (RBI) has formally proposed banning revolving credit facilities for non-banking financial companies (NBFCs), two years after first flagging concerns about the practice. The proposal, which has been shared with industry stakeholders, seeks to restrict the use of credit lines that allow borrowers to draw, repay, and redraw funds repeatedly.

Officials familiar with the development said the central bank views these facilities as a potential source of liquidity mismatches and unsecured lending risks. The ban would apply to fresh sanctions, though the RBI has not yet specified whether existing facilities would be grandfathered.

Industry pushback: 'One size doesn't fit all'

The sector, however, is pushing back. Industry bodies and major NBFCs have argued that a blanket prohibition would hurt legitimate business models, especially in retail lending and microfinance. They are requesting that the RBI adopt a more calibrated approach, targeting only high-risk segments or imposing limits rather than an outright ban.

"A uniform ban could stifle credit delivery to underserved segments," a senior industry executive said, speaking on condition of anonymity. The sector has proposed alternatives such as stricter underwriting norms, exposure caps, and enhanced reporting requirements.

What is revolving credit and why does it matter?

Revolving credit facilities allow borrowers to access funds up to a specified limit, repay, and borrow again without reapplying. For NBFCs, these lines are often extended to smaller businesses and individuals, providing flexible working capital.

The RBI's concern stems from the potential for these facilities to mask deteriorating asset quality. If a borrower repeatedly draws and repays, stress may not surface in traditional delinquency metrics until it is too late.

Potential impact on lending landscape

If implemented in its current form, the ban could reshape how NBFCs manage liquidity and serve clients. Many NBFCs rely on such lines to manage short-term cash flow mismatches, and a sudden withdrawal could force them to recalibrate their lending strategies.

Analysts note that smaller NBFCs, which may lack diversified funding sources, could be disproportionately affected. Larger players with access to bond markets and bank credit might adapt more easily, but the transition would not be seamless.

What comes next

The RBI is expected to review industry feedback before finalising the framework. It remains unclear whether the central bank will soften its stance or proceed with the ban, possibly with transition periods.

Stakeholders are watching closely, as the outcome will set a precedent for how the regulator balances financial stability with credit growth. The next few months are critical, as the RBI weighs systemic risks against the sector's plea for nuance.
