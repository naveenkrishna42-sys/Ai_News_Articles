Pakistan Rejects Airspace for Russian Nuclear Fuel Ship to Bangladesh’s Rooppur – A Delayed Deliver

Background: Bangladesh’s Rooppur Nuclear Power Project

The Rooppur Nuclear Power Plant, a collaboration between Bangladesh and Russia, is intended to supply 1000 MW of low‑carbon electricity to the nation’s grid. The first unit, Rooppur‑1, couples an 800 MW VVER-1200 reactor, while the second unit, Rooppur‑2, is due to come online by the end of 2027. Both units rely on Russian-made nuclear fuel assemblies, each delivering roughly 22 tU of enriched uranium. The transport of this fuel is tightly scheduled to dovetail with construction milestones and reactor start‑up tests.

Incident Overview: Airspace Denial and Flight Disruption

On 28 September 2026, a charter flight operated by Russian airline Aeroflot, carrying a full cargo hold of nuclear fuel rods destined for Rooppur‑2, was denied passage through Pakistani airspace. The Russian ministry of transport cited a “technical issue” involving aircraft systems that it could not resolve in a timely fashion. As a result, the flight had to divert to a carrier‑free bay in Turkmenistan, then reconnect by road to Tashkent before proceeding to Dhaka. The itinerary change has shifted the delivery window from mid‑September to early October, approximately a week‑long delay.

Technical and Regulatory Implications

Cross‑border nuclear shipments fall under the International Atomic Energy Agency (IAEA) safeguards and UN Security Council regulations. The sudden reroute raises questions on compliance with the IAEA’s International Transport of Nuclear Materials (ITNM) Code, particularly concerning sealed source handling and emergency response protocols. Pakistan’s refusal, framed as an “airspace denial”, suggests an intersection of technical, political, and security considerations that may not have been communicated transparently to the parties involved.

From a logistical standpoint, the detour consumes additional aviation fuel and increases wear on aircraft systems. The added time also forces the delivery team to arrange a new cargo manifest, requiring re‑certification of the transport sealed packages and an update to the shipping logbooks overseen by the IAEA.

Impact on Rooppur‑2 Construction Schedule

Construction crews at Rooppur were poised to begin in‑situ irradiation tests within two weeks of arrival. A one‑week lag jeopardises the 18‑month reactor commissioning cycle calculated by Bangladesh’s Nuclear Regulatory Board (BNRB), potentially nudging the plant’s operational date from December 2027 to early 2028. While the delay is not catastrophic, it propagates cost overruns and may influence the procurement of auxiliary components, such as steam turbines and emergency core cooling systems, whose delivery is tightly coupled to fuel arrival.

Moreover, Bangladesh’s Energy Policy for 2026–2028 earmarks Rooppur‑2 to address an anticipated shortfall of 700 MW in national grid reliability. Deferment amplifies the reliance on coal and natural gas imports until the reactor is fully commissioned, affecting national carbon targets.
