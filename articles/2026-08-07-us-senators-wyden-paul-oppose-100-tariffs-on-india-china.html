US Senators Wyden, Paul oppose 100% tariffs on India, China

📅 2026-08-07 📂 Breaking News Original source ↗
Key points
  • Senators Wyden and Paul criticize 100% tariff proposal on India and China
  • They argue tariffs would harm US economy and consumers
  • Senate consideration of related sanctions bill underway
  • India may continue buying Russian crude despite tariff threats

Washington: Two influential US senators have openly broken ranks with the Trump administration's aggressive trade posture, calling proposed 100% tariffs on India and China a self-inflicted wound. Senators Ron Wyden and Rand Paul, from opposite ends of the political spectrum, have voiced strong opposition to the sweeping tariff plan currently under discussion in Congress.

The rare bipartisan pushback comes as the US Senate begins consideration of a broader bill that includes stringent sanctions on Russia and Iran. The tariff proposal, aimed at punishing countries that continue energy trade with Moscow, has now become a flashpoint in the domestic debate over America's economic strategy.

Bipartisan criticism of tariff plan

Senator Wyden, a Democrat from Oregon, did not mince words when describing the proposed tariffs. He said the United States was "shooting itself in the foot" by threatening to impose 100% duties on goods from India and China. His Republican colleague Rand Paul echoed similar concerns, arguing that such punitive measures would ultimately hurt American businesses and consumers.

The senators' opposition highlights growing unease within the US political establishment about the collateral damage of trade wars. Both lawmakers have traditionally championed free trade principles, though from different ideological lenses.

India's crude oil dilemma

At the heart of the tariff threat lies India's continued purchase of Russian crude oil. Despite Western pressure, New Delhi has maintained its energy ties with Moscow, citing national interest and energy security. The Times of India has reported that India may simply ignore the tariff threat and continue its crude purchases from Russia.

Indian refiners have benefited significantly from discounted Russian oil, which has helped moderate domestic fuel prices. The calculus for New Delhi appears straightforward: the savings from cheaper crude outweigh the risks of potential US tariffs on Indian exports.

Senate bill and trade negotiations

The tariff proposal is part of a larger legislative package that includes what Senator Lindsey Graham has described as "hellish" sanctions against Russia and Iran. The bill's progress through the Senate will be watched closely by trade partners across Asia, particularly India and China, who stand to be directly affected.

Meanwhile, a top White House adviser has stated that the impact of Russia sanctions on US-India trade talks remains "up to negotiators." This suggests that despite the harsh rhetoric, there is still room for diplomatic manoeuvring between Washington and New Delhi.

Economic fallout concerns

Critics of the tariff plan argue that imposing 100% duties would be unprecedented in modern trade relations. Such a move would likely trigger retaliatory measures from both India and China, potentially disrupting global supply chains that American companies depend on.

Analysts point out that the US has much to lose in a trade confrontation with India, a growing market for American exports including defence equipment, aircraft and agricultural products. The two countries have significantly deepened their economic and strategic ties over the past decade.

The White House has not yet publicly responded to the senators' objections. Officials have not confirmed whether the administration would soften its stance or push ahead with the full tariff threat.

As the Senate debate unfolds, all eyes will be on the final shape of the legislation. The outcome could determine not just the future of US-India trade relations, but also the broader trajectory of America's economic engagement with Asia.
