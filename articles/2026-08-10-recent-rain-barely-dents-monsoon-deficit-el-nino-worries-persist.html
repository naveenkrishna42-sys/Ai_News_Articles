Recent rain barely dents monsoon deficit, El Nino worries persist

📅 2026-08-10 📂 Top Stories Original source ↗
Key points
  • Rainfall in the past week has not significantly improved the overall monsoon shortfall.
  • The monsoon deficit remains a concern for agriculture and water resources.
  • El Nino conditions could further weaken the monsoon in the coming months.
  • Some regions have received excess rain, while others remain dry.

The recent spell of rain across parts of India has brought little cheer to those tracking the monsoon. Despite a brief revival in some areas, the national deficit remains largely unchanged. According to data from the India Meteorological Department (IMD), the seasonal rainfall shortfall continues to hover around the same level as it did before the wet spell.

This means that while some regions have received a temporary respite, the larger picture is still worrying. The monsoon, which is crucial for India's agriculture and economy, has been uneven this year. Many areas in the central and western parts of the country are still waiting for substantial rain.

Deficit persists despite revival

The recent rains, though welcome in some pockets, have not been enough to bridge the gap. The IMD's data shows that the overall deficit is still in double digits. This is a concern because the monsoon season is already past its peak, and the window for recovery is narrowing.

Agriculture experts point out that the timing of rain is as important as the quantity. Late rains, even if they are heavy, may not help crops that have already been sown. Farmers in many regions are anxious, and the government is keeping a close watch on the situation.

El Nino threat returns

Adding to the worry is the possibility of El Nino conditions developing later this year. El Nino is a climate pattern that typically brings drier conditions to the Indian subcontinent. Several global weather models are now indicating a higher chance of El Nino forming in the coming months.

If that happens, the monsoon could weaken further, especially in the second half of the season. This could have a significant impact on kharif crops, which are sown during the monsoon and harvested in the winter. The last time India experienced a severe El Nino, it led to droughts in many parts of the country.

Uneven distribution of rain

One of the key issues this year has been the uneven distribution of rainfall. Some states, like those in the northeast, have received excess rain, leading to floods. Others, particularly in the northwest and parts of the Deccan plateau, are facing dry conditions.

This imbalance is not new, but it is becoming more pronounced. Climate scientists say that climate change is making the monsoon more erratic, with longer dry spells and shorter, more intense bursts of rain. This makes water management and agricultural planning more challenging.

Government and farmer response

The government has been trying to mitigate the impact by providing relief to affected farmers and ensuring the availability of seeds and fertilizers. However, the fundamental issue of a deficient monsoon remains.

Farmers are being advised to adopt water-saving techniques and consider crop diversification. In some regions, there are calls for declaring drought-like conditions to provide additional support. The situation is being monitored at the highest levels, and any further shortfall could trigger more intervention.

As the monsoon season progresses, all eyes will be on the weather forecasts. The next few weeks are critical for determining whether the deficit can be reduced or if the country will have to brace for a difficult agricultural year.

Looking ahead, the focus will be on the IMD's updated forecasts and the development of El Nino conditions in the Pacific Ocean. A clearer picture should emerge by early September, which will help guide policy and relief measures.
