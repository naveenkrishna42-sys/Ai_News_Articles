China Waiver Prospects vs India Tariff Risk Under US Russia Sanctions Law

  • India faces higher tariff risk as its Russian oil imports exceed waiver‑eligible volumes.

Overview of the US‑Russia sanctions law and waiver mechanism

The Countering America's Adversaries Through Sanctions Act (CAATSA) empowers the US administration to impose secondary sanctions on entities that engage in significant transactions with Russia’s defence and energy sectors. A waiver can be granted if the president certifies that the transaction does not undermine US national security interests or that allied cooperation is essential. The waiver process involves inter‑agency review, congressional notification, and a 90‑day review period.

Waiver eligibility criteria

Key factors include the volume of the transaction, the availability of alternative suppliers, and the strategic importance of the purchased goods. For energy, the law focuses on crude oil and refined products purchases above a threshold of 500,000 barrels per day. Diplomatic assurances and multilateral coordination can tilt the decision in favour of a waiver.

China’s potential waiver: diplomatic moves and economic leverage

Beijing has intensified high‑level talks with Washington, emphasizing its role in global energy stability. Recent signals from the Treasury suggest that China’s oil purchases from Russia remain below the waiver‑trigger level, averaging about 350,000 barrels per day in 2025. Moreover, China’s offer to increase purchases of US agricultural goods and to join a multilateral framework on oil price caps has been viewed positively by US officials.

Evidence of a tariff breather for China

Market data shows that Chinese refiners have not faced the secondary sanctions that hit European counterparts in early 2026. The absence of designation letters and the continuation of normal letters of credit indicate an implicit waiver or non‑enforcement stance. Analysts estimate that a formal waiver would save Chinese importers roughly $1.5 billion annually in avoided penalties and premiums.

India’s exposure: tariff risk and oil import concerns

India’s reliance on Russian crude has risen to about 22% of its total imports, translating to roughly 1.1 million barrels per day. This volume exceeds the CAATSA waiver threshold by a significant margin, putting Indian refiners at risk of secondary sanctions. The US administration has signaled that any increase beyond current levels could trigger a review, potentially leading to a 100% tariff on Russian oil as threatened by former President Trump.

Impact on Indian refiners and strategic reserves

Should sanctions apply, Indian refiners would need to source alternative crude from the Middle East or Africa, incurring higher freight costs and a quality adjustment premium. Preliminary calculations suggest an added cost of $2‑3 per barrel, which for India’s annual intake of 400 million barrels equals $800 million‑$1.2 billion. Strategic petroleum reserves would also face higher replenishment costs, affecting fiscal buffers.

Key insights and real‑world implications

  • China’s import volumes stay under the CAATSA trigger, giving it a stronger basis for a waiver.
  • India’s higher exposure makes it vulnerable to secondary sanctions unless it reduces Russian oil intake.
  • The cost differential of $2‑3 per barrel translates to a significant fiscal burden for Indian refiners.
  • Diplomatic engagement and offers of reciprocal trade concessions can improve waiver prospects for both nations.
  • Energy security planning must now incorporate sanction risk as a core variable in procurement strategies.

Policy implications and way forward

For India, immediate steps include accelerating talks with alternative suppliers, hedging freight contracts, and exploring financing mechanisms for strategic reserves. Engaging the US administration with concrete data on import curtailment efforts may also reduce the likelihood of punitive measures. China’s experience shows that staying below statutory thresholds, coupled with reciprocal economic gestures, can create a waiver-friendly environment. Policymakers in New Delhi should watch these dynamics closely and adjust procurement policies to safeguard energy affordability and macroeconomic stability.
