KITU vs Labour Dept: Women's Night-Shift Safety Rules Explained

✍️ By TIVRA News Editorial Team 📅 2026-09-12 📂 Bengaluru Original source ↗
Key points
  • KITU alleges that specific night-shift protections for women were dropped from Karnataka's draft rules, while the Labour Department insists the OSH Code's general provisions legally cover them.
  • Employers in Karnataka's IT, BPO, garment, and healthcare sectors must reassess consent records, transport, and safety audits for women working between 7 pm and 6 am.
  • Non-compliance exposure includes OSH Code penalties, civil liability, and reputational damage, making documented safeguards a commercial necessity, not just a legal formality.

The Dispute in Brief

Karnataka's labour ecosystem is witnessing a sharp disagreement over women's night-shift protections. The Karnataka Industrial and Trade Unions (KITU) centre has publicly flagged the deletion of specific safeguards for women working night shifts, arguing that the removal weakens an established layer of protection. The state Labour Department has countered that the Occupational Safety, Health and Working Conditions (OSH) Code, 2020, and its Karnataka rules already cover these workers through general safety, health, and welfare obligations.

For employers, the practical question is not who wins the argument, but what compliance looks like on the factory floor and in the IT park.

What the OSH Code Actually Says

The OSH Code consolidated 13 labour laws, including the Factories Act, 1948, and the Shops and Establishments provisions. On night work, the Code permits women to work beyond 7 pm and before 6 am only where the appropriate government notifies conditions relating to consent, safety, holidays, working hours, and transport. Karnataka has issued rules under this framework, but KITU contends the state's draft deleted explicit clauses that previously mandated door-step drop, verified transport, and specific consent documentation.

The Labour Department's position is that these protections are embedded in the Code's welfare provisions, including Section 26 on night-shift safeguards and the general duties of employers under Sections 6 and 7. In its reading, deletion of a standalone clause does not erase the underlying obligation.

Real-World Implications for Employers

For a mid-sized Bengaluru BPO with 400 women on night shifts, the compliance burden is not abstract. Employers must retain signed consent forms, maintain a register of night-shift workers, provide verified pick-up and drop, and document safety committee reviews. A single missing transport log can become evidence in an inspection or a civil claim.

Costs are material. Dedicated transport for a 200-seat night operation can run ₹3-6 lakh per month depending on routes and vendor rates. Security staffing, CCTV coverage, and rest-room facilities add further overhead. Firms treating this as an HR formality face higher exposure than those embedding it into vendor contracts and audit calendars.

Key Insights: What Buyers and Employers Should Track

  • Documentation is the defence: Consent letters, transport logs, and safety audit reports are the first documents inspectors request.
  • Sector risk varies: IT/ITeS and healthcare face the highest scrutiny due to large night-shift female workforces.
  • Union pressure is rising: KITU's public stand signals that non-compliance may attract organised labour action, not just fines.
  • Vendor accountability: Transport and security vendors must be contractually bound to OSH Code standards.
  • Insurance angle: Insurers increasingly ask for night-shift safety documentation during liability underwriting.

Pros and Cons of the Current Framework

  • Pros: A single consolidated Code reduces compliance confusion; general welfare clauses offer flexibility; Karnataka's rules retain consent and transport requirements.
  • Cons: Deletion of explicit clauses creates ambiguity; enforcement capacity remains thin; smaller firms may under-document; unions argue protection now depends on interpretation rather than clear text.

What Happens Next

KITU is expected to press for reinstatement of explicit safeguards through representations to the Labour Department and possibly the state legislature. Employers should not wait for clarity. A practical response is to audit night-shift compliance this quarter, map every woman working between 7 pm and 6 am, verify transport arrangements, refresh consent records, and brief supervisors on reporting obligations.

The legal text may be contested, but the operational standard is clear: consent, safe transport, rest, and documented oversight. Firms that meet that standard will be insulated from both regulatory action and reputational risk.
