Cryptocurrency regulation: US laws and global developments in 2026

📅 2026-07-20 📂 Crypto Original source ↗
Key points
  • US regulators continue to classify most cryptocurrencies as securities, subject to SEC oversight.
  • The EU's Markets in Crypto-Assets (MiCA) framework provides a comprehensive regulatory model.
  • Global coordination remains fragmented, with divergent approaches from China's ban to El Salvador's adoption.

Cryptocurrency regulation remains one of the most dynamic areas of financial law in 2026. Governments worldwide are grappling with how to oversee digital assets without stifling innovation. The United States, the European Union, and several Asian economies have taken distinct paths, creating a patchwork of rules that exchanges and investors must navigate.

The US approach: SEC vs. CFTC

In the United States, the Securities and Exchange Commission (SEC) has taken an aggressive stance. Under Chair Gary Gensler, the agency has argued that most cryptocurrencies qualify as securities under the Howey Test. This classification means tokens must register with the SEC unless they qualify for an exemption.

The Commodity Futures Trading Commission (CFTC) oversees Bitcoin and Ethereum as commodities. This split jurisdiction has created confusion. Exchanges offering multiple tokens must comply with two different regulatory regimes. Several major exchanges have faced SEC enforcement actions for listing unregistered securities.

Congress has not passed comprehensive crypto legislation in 2026. Bills like the Lummis-Gillibrand Responsible Financial Innovation Act have stalled. Lawmakers remain divided on whether to give the CFTC more authority over digital assets.

Europe leads with MiCA

The European Union implemented the Markets in Crypto-Assets (MiCA) regulation in 2024. This framework provides a single rulebook for all 27 member states. It covers issuers of stablecoins, crypto-asset service providers, and market abuse prevention.

MiCA requires exchanges to obtain a license and comply with anti-money laundering rules. Stablecoin issuers must hold sufficient reserves and publish regular disclosures. The regulation has made Europe a more predictable environment for crypto businesses.

Several global firms have moved operations to EU countries like France and Germany to take advantage of regulatory clarity. The European Securities and Markets Authority continues to issue guidance on implementation.

Asia: A tale of two extremes

China maintains a complete ban on cryptocurrency trading and mining. The government views digital assets as a threat to financial stability and capital controls. Its central bank digital currency, the digital yuan, is the only state-sanctioned digital currency.

Japan has taken a more welcoming approach. It recognizes Bitcoin and other cryptocurrencies as legal property under the Payment Services Act. Exchanges must register with the Financial Services Agency and follow strict custody rules. Japan was one of the first countries to regulate crypto after the Mt. Gox collapse in 2014.

Singapore has positioned itself as a hub for digital asset innovation. The Monetary Authority of Singapore requires licensing under the Payment Services Act. It has approved several major exchanges while cracking down on unlicensed operators.

India has not passed comprehensive crypto legislation. The Reserve Bank of India has expressed concerns about financial stability. The Supreme Court struck down a banking ban in 2020, but the government has discussed a potential ban on private cryptocurrencies.

Global coordination remains elusive

The Financial Action Task Force (FATF) has issued global standards for crypto regulation. These include the travel rule, which requires exchanges to share customer information for transactions above a threshold. Implementation has been uneven across jurisdictions.

The International Monetary Fund has warned that a fragmented regulatory landscape could create arbitrage opportunities. Some countries have become crypto-friendly jurisdictions to attract business, while others impose strict controls. This divergence makes international enforcement challenging.

El Salvador and the Central African Republic have adopted Bitcoin as legal tender. This move has drawn criticism from the IMF and World Bank over financial stability risks. Both countries have seen limited adoption of Bitcoin for everyday transactions.

As 2026 progresses, regulators will continue refining their approaches. The US Congress may still pass legislation providing clearer rules. Global coordination through the FATF and G20 will likely remain incremental rather than revolutionary. Market participants should prepare for continued regulatory evolution across major economies.
