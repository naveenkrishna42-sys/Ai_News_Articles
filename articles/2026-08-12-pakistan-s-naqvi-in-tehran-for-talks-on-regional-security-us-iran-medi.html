Pakistan's Naqvi in Tehran for talks on regional security, US-Iran mediation

📅 2026-08-12 📂 World Original source ↗
Key points
  • Pakistan's Interior Minister Mohsin Naqvi is in Tehran for discussions on regional security.
  • Islamabad says the mediation file between Washington and Tehran remains open, with the 60-day window extendable.
  • US and Iran trade reparation demands even as Pakistan pushes for progress on a deal.

Pakistan's Interior Minister Mohsin Naqvi has arrived in Tehran for talks focused on regional security, as Islamabad continues to navigate a delicate role as mediator between Washington and Tehran. The visit comes at a moment when the two adversaries are publicly exchanging reparation demands, even as Pakistan signals flexibility on the timeline of its diplomatic push.

The Pakistani Foreign Ministry confirmed that the mediation file between the United States and Iran has not been closed, and that the 60-day period outlined in the memorandum of understanding could be extended. The statement, carried by multiple international outlets, underscores Islamabad's intent to keep the process alive despite entrenched positions on both sides.

Naqvi's agenda in Tehran

Naqvi's itinerary in Tehran has not been fully disclosed, but officials familiar with the visit indicate that regional security will dominate discussions. The interior minister is expected to meet senior Iranian leadership to exchange assessments on border stability, cross-border movements, and the broader implications of the ongoing West Asia crisis.

This is not the first high-level contact between the two neighbours in recent weeks. Pakistan has positioned itself as a bridge between Tehran and Washington, leveraging its unique relationships with both capitals. However, the trip also carries domestic significance, as Pakistan faces its own security challenges along the Iran border.

Mediation on a tightrope

The 60-day window mentioned in the memorandum was initially seen as a firm deadline, but Pakistan's Foreign Ministry has now signalled that it is not bound by a rigid calendar. “The period can be extended,” a ministry statement noted, without specifying conditions or a new timeframe.

That flexibility, however, is being tested by the ongoing exchange of reparation demands between Washington and Tehran. Both sides have publicly insisted on conditions that the other finds difficult to accept, complicating Pakistan's efforts to broker common ground. The reparation issue, in particular, has emerged as a sticking point, with each side framing its demands as a matter of principle.

What the talks could yield

Analysts say Naqvi's visit is unlikely to produce a breakthrough on the core disputes, but it could help keep the channel open. The fact that Pakistan is still willing to send a senior minister to Tehran at this juncture is itself a signal of its commitment to the process.

There is also a practical dimension. Pakistan needs Iranian cooperation on border management and counter-smuggling operations, which are often linked to the larger regional security picture. A stable relationship with Tehran serves Islamabad's interests regardless of the US-Iran track.

Reparation demands and the road ahead

The reparation demands, which have been reported but not detailed in official statements, remain a major obstacle. Washington and Tehran have each framed their positions in public, but neither has shown readiness to soften its stance. Pakistan's role, as it has repeatedly stated, is to facilitate—not to impose solutions.

Observers note that the next few weeks will be crucial. If the 60-day window is extended, it would give Pakistan more room to manoeuvre, but it would also raise questions about the credibility of the memorandum's timelines. The absence of concrete progress could erode trust in the mediation effort.

For now, Naqvi's presence in Tehran is a reminder that the diplomatic track is still alive, however fragile. The visit also signals to Washington that Pakistan is not abandoning the file, even as the two sides trade demands publicly.

What happens next will depend on whether either Washington or Tehran shows a willingness to shift its position. Pakistan has indicated it can extend the timeline, but it cannot manufacture consensus. The coming weeks will test whether the mediation can move from process to substance, or whether the reparation standoff will stall the effort altogether.
