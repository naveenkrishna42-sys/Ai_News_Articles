Gujarat Chandipura outbreak: 22 children dead, 7 under treatment

📅 2026-08-04 📂 Health Original source ↗
Key points
  • At least 22 children have died from Chandipura virus in Gujarat this monsoon season.
  • Seven children are currently undergoing treatment for the viral infection.
  • The state health machinery has been placed on high alert following the outbreak.

Monsoon Outbreak Claims Lives Across Districts

At least 22 children have died from Chandipura virus in Gujarat since the monsoon began, with seven more currently undergoing treatment, state health officials confirmed on Monday. The viral encephalitis outbreak has put the state's medical infrastructure on edge, triggering an urgent review of preparedness in affected districts.

Health Minister Rushikesh Patel told reporters that the suspected deaths have been reported from various parts of the state, though he did not provide a district-wise breakdown. Officials have not yet confirmed the exact number of suspected cases, but the toll has already surpassed last year's figures for the same period.

What is Chandipura Virus?

Chandipura virus is a mosquito-borne pathogen that belongs to the Rhabdoviridae family, transmitted primarily by sandflies and ticks. It causes acute encephalitis, an inflammation of the brain, with symptoms that escalate rapidly—sudden high fever, convulsions, altered consciousness, and in severe cases, coma. The virus has a high fatality rate, especially among children under 15, and can kill within 48 to 72 hours of symptom onset if untreated.

Doctors point out that children are the most vulnerable demographic because their immune systems are still developing. "The virus attacks the central nervous system aggressively in young patients," a paediatrician at a government hospital in Ahmedabad said, requesting anonymity. He added that early hospitalisation is critical to survival, which is why the health department has been pushing for immediate referral of any child with fever and neurological signs.

Health Machinery on High Alert

The state health department has placed all district hospitals and primary health centres on high alert. Rapid response teams have been deployed in the affected regions to conduct door-to-door surveillance and vector-control measures, including fogging and spraying of insecticides to kill sandfly larvae. The government has also issued advisories to private practitioners to report any suspected case without delay.

Testing facilities have been scaled up, with samples being sent to the National Institute of Virology in Pune for confirmation. Officials say that while most deaths have occurred in rural areas with limited access to tertiary care, urban centres have not been spared. The health minister assured the public that adequate stocks of essential medicines and IV fluids are available at all treatment centres.

Why Children Bear the Brunt

Medical experts explain that Chandipura virus targets the endothelial cells lining the blood-brain barrier, causing inflammation and fluid accumulation in the brain. In children, the barrier is more permeable, making them more susceptible to severe neurological damage. Additionally, young children are more likely to be outdoors during dusk and dawn, when sandflies are most active, increasing their exposure to infected vectors.

Poor sanitation and stagnant water after monsoon rains create ideal breeding grounds for the insects. Public health specialists have urged families to use mosquito nets, wear full-sleeve clothing, and keep their surroundings clean to reduce the risk of infection. Schools in high-risk districts have been advised to postpone evening assemblies and outdoor activities.

Looking Ahead

With the monsoon expected to continue for several more weeks, the threat of new cases remains significant. The state government is expected to release a detailed epidemiological report soon, which will help refine containment strategies. Officials are also monitoring neighbouring states for any spillover, as the virus can spread through travel.

The coming weeks will be crucial in determining whether the outbreak has peaked or is still escalating. For now, the focus remains on saving the seven children under treatment and preventing further fatalities through early detection and aggressive vector control.
