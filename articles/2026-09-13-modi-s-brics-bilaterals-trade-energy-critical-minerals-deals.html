Why the BRICS Sidelines Matter More Than the Summit Plenary

Prime Minister Narendra Modi used the BRICS Summit 2026 in New Delhi to hold one-on-one meetings with the Presidents of South Africa and Nigeria and the leadership of Kazakhstan. The joint communique is the headline event, but for buyers, importers and manufacturers, the bilateral rooms are where actual supply terms get shaped. Trade, energy and critical minerals dominated all three conversations.

India imports over 85 percent of its crude requirement and is almost entirely dependent on overseas sources for lithium, cobalt and rare earth elements. Every bilateral that touches these three categories has a direct bearing on landed costs for Indian industry.

What Was Actually Discussed, Country by Country

South Africa: Platinum Group Metals and Coal

The meeting with President Cyril Ramaphosa centred on critical minerals, particularly platinum group metals used in catalytic converters and hydrogen fuel cells, along with thermal coal supplies for India's coastal power plants. South Africa is already among India's top coal suppliers, and any long-term offtake arrangement would give Indian utilities a hedge against Indonesian and Australian price swings.

Nigeria: Crude Term Contracts and Rupee Settlement

Nigeria remains one of India's largest crude oil sources in West Africa. Talks focused on term contract volumes, payment mechanisms and the possibility of expanding rupee-based trade settlement, which reduces dollar conversion costs for refiners. A working group mechanism was the stated outcome rather than a signed purchase agreement.

Kazakhstan: Uranium, Titanium and the INSTC Route

Kazakhstan supplies uranium for India's nuclear programme and titanium sponge for aerospace and defence manufacturing. The discussion covered the International North-South Transport Corridor, which cuts transit time to Central Asia compared to the Suez route, and expanded uranium supply commitments.

Buyer and Industry Implications

Critical minerals access is the single most consequential thread. India's EV, battery and defence sectors depend on imported lithium, cobalt and rare earths, with China controlling a large share of global refining. Diversifying sourcing toward South Africa and Kazakhstan reduces single-supplier risk, though new mining and refining capacity takes years, not quarters, to come online.

For refiners, Nigeria talks signal continuity of West African crude supply and possible cost savings through rupee settlement. For power producers, South African coal arrangements offer a buffer against seaborne price volatility. For aerospace and nuclear buyers, Kazakh titanium and uranium remain strategic inputs with limited substitutes.

  • Pro: Diversified critical mineral sourcing reduces exposure to a single dominant supplier.
  • Pro: Rupee settlement talks could lower forex transaction costs for crude importers.
  • Pro: INSTC route offers faster Central Asia transit versus the Suez Canal.
  • Con: No binding supply contracts signed; outcomes remain at the working-group stage.
  • Con: New mining and refining capacity timelines stretch to several years.
  • Con: Near-term import prices and availability remain unchanged for buyers.

Verdict for Indian Buyers

These bilaterals are strategic positioning, not immediate procurement. Importers should track the working group mandates over the next two to three quarters for concrete offtake terms. Until binding contracts emerge, existing supply chains and price structures stay intact.
