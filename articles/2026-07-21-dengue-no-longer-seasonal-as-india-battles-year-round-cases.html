Dengue no longer seasonal as India battles year-round cases

📅 2026-07-21 📂 Health Original source ↗
Key points
  • Dengue has become a year-round disease in India, no longer confined to monsoon months.
  • India's drug regulator approved Takeda's Qdenga for ages 4 to 60 on July 21.
  • The vaccine is the first dengue shot cleared for use in the country.

For decades, Indians braced for dengue only after the first monsoon rains. That pattern has collapsed. The Aedes aegypti mosquito now breeds across seasons, and hospitals report cases even in the dry winter months. What was once called 'breakbone fever' for its excruciating joint pain has become a permanent threat.

Public health experts say climate change, rapid urbanisation and poor water storage practices have extended the mosquito's breeding window. Cities like Delhi, Bengaluru and Pune now see dengue outbreaks in January and February. The shift has overwhelmed healthcare systems that once staffed dengue wards only between July and November.

India approves first dengue vaccine

On July 21, the Central Drugs Standard Control Organisation (CDSCO) granted approval to Qdenga, a vaccine developed by Japanese pharmaceutical company Takeda. The shot is cleared for individuals aged 4 to 60 years. It is the first dengue vaccine ever approved in India.

Qdenga is a live-attenuated vaccine. It targets all four dengue virus serotypes. Clinical trials have shown it reduces hospitalisations and severe disease. The approval follows a recommendation from the Subject Expert Committee earlier this year. The Drugs Controller General of India (DCGI) gave the final nod.

Who gets the shot first?

The government has not yet announced a national rollout plan. The vaccine is expected to be available in private markets initially. Public health officials are studying whether to include it in the Universal Immunisation Programme. Cost and logistics remain key questions.

Takeda has said it will supply doses to India at a tiered pricing model. The company has manufacturing capacity in Europe and is exploring local partnerships. Serum Institute of India had earlier partnered with Takeda to manufacture and distribute Qdenga in India, but that agreement's current status is unclear.

Dengue burden rising fast

India reported over 2.3 lakh dengue cases in 2024, according to the National Vector Borne Disease Control Programme. That number is expected to rise in 2025. The actual burden is likely much higher because many cases go unreported.

Dengue now accounts for a significant share of fever-related hospital visits across the country. The disease hits children and young adults hardest. The new vaccine covers a wide age range, which could reduce pressure on paediatric and general wards.

What about previous vaccine concerns?

An earlier dengue vaccine, Sanofi's Dengvaxia, was linked to severe disease in people who had never been infected. That vaccine was never approved in India. Qdenga uses a different technology and has shown a better safety profile in trials, but long-term data is still being collected.

Health authorities have advised that vaccination should complement, not replace, mosquito control. Fogging, larvicide and source reduction remain essential. The vaccine is not a silver bullet, but it gives India a new tool against a disease that refuses to follow the calendar.

Officials have not yet confirmed when the first doses will reach clinics. Private hospitals are expected to start offering Qdenga within weeks. The big question is whether the government will subsidise the shot for the poorest, who bear the highest dengue burden.
