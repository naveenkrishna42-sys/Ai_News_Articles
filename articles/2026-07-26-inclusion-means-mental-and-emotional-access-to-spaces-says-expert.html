Inclusion means mental and emotional access to spaces, says expert

📅 2026-07-26 📂 Chennai Original source ↗
Key points
  • Inclusion is redefined as mental and emotional access, not just physical access
  • Expert speaks at a Chennai event on accessibility and design
  • Call to shift focus from compliance to lived experiences

Inclusion is often reduced to ramps and reserved parking spots. But a Chennai-based accessibility expert argues that true inclusion goes far beyond the physical.

Speaking at a recent event in the city, the expert said, 'Inclusion is about mental access and emotional access to spaces.' The remark challenges conventional thinking that equates accessibility largely with infrastructure.

Beyond ramps and rails

For years, accessibility in India has focused on physical modifications — wider doorways, tactile tiles, and accessible washrooms. While necessary, these measures often miss the deeper barriers that keep people out.

The expert pointed out that a person may be able to enter a building but still feel unwelcome or unable to participate fully. 'Mental access means a person does not feel anxious or judged when they walk into a room. Emotional access means they feel they belong,' they explained.

This perspective shifts the burden from the individual to the environment and the attitudes of those who design and manage spaces.

Designing for dignity

The talk also highlighted the importance of involving diverse voices in the design process. 'You cannot design for inclusion if you have never listened to someone who lives exclusion every day,' the expert said.

Examples included workplaces where neurodivergent employees struggle with open-plan layouts and social expectations, or public events where loudspeakers and bright lights can overwhelm those with sensory sensitivities.

The expert called for a more empathetic approach, where architects, event planners, and policymakers consider how a space makes people feel, not just whether it meets legal codes.

A shift in mindset

The event drew architects, urban planners, and disability rights advocates. Many agreed that the conversation needs to move from compliance to culture.

'We have guidelines, but we don't have enough sensitivity,' said one attendee. Another added, 'If we only focus on the physical, we are only solving half the problem.'

The expert urged organisations to train staff in inclusive communication and to create feedback loops where marginalised groups can shape policies and spaces.

They also stressed that small changes — like offering quiet hours at a museum or using plain language in signage — can make a significant difference.

What this means going forward

The definition of inclusion is expanding. As Indian cities grow and public spaces multiply, the need to think beyond physical access becomes urgent.

Policymakers, designers, and citizens are being asked to consider a simple question: does this space make everyone feel they can be themselves?

The expert's call for mental and emotional access is likely to spark more debate and, perhaps, a redesign of how we think about belonging.
