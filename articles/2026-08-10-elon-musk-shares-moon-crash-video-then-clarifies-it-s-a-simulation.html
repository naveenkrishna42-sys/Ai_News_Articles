Elon Musk Shares Moon Crash Video, Then Clarifies: It's a Simulation

📅 2026-08-10 📂 Science Original source ↗
Key points
  • Elon Musk shared a video appearing to show a SpaceX Falcon 9 rocket stage crashing into the Moon.
  • He later clarified that the video was a simulation, not actual footage.
  • The real crash happened in March 2022, creating a crater spotted by Korea's Danuri orbiter.
  • The incident has renewed concerns about space debris and unregulated lunar impacts.

Elon Musk set the internet abuzz on Monday by sharing a video that appeared to show a SpaceX Falcon 9 rocket stage slamming into the Moon. Within hours, however, the tech billionaire issued a clarification: the footage was a simulation, not a recording of an actual event.

The clarification did little to dampen interest in the underlying story — a real SpaceX rocket part did hit the Moon, and it happened more than four years ago. The crash, which took place in March 2022, has now been confirmed by NASA and independent observers, and its aftermath was recently photographed by a South Korean lunar orbiter.

The video and the clarification

In his initial post on X (formerly Twitter), Musk shared a dramatic clip showing a rocket stage hurtling toward the lunar surface, followed by an explosion and the formation of a fresh crater. The video was widely shared, with many users assuming it was actual footage of the 2022 impact.

But Musk soon corrected the record, stating that the video was a computer-generated simulation created to visualise the event. He did not specify who made the simulation or why it was posted without a disclaimer at first. The episode highlights how quickly unverified visual content can spread, even when it originates from prominent figures.

What actually happened in 2022

The real incident involved the upper stage of a SpaceX Falcon 9 rocket, which had been used to launch a weather satellite in 2015. After completing its mission, the stage was left in a chaotic orbit around Earth. Over the years, gravitational interactions gradually pushed it onto a collision course with the Moon.

On March 4, 2022, the 4-tonne piece of hardware crashed into the lunar far side, carving out a crater estimated to be between 10 and 20 metres wide. Because the impact occurred on the side of the Moon that faces away from Earth, it could not be observed directly from our planet. Scientists had to rely on predictions and later orbital observations to confirm the event.

NASA and the Korean orbiter's findings

NASA had announced ahead of the impact that it would attempt to observe the crash site using its Lunar Reconnaissance Orbiter (LRO). The agency later confirmed that the LRO had identified a new crater at the predicted location, though the exact dimensions and characteristics remain under study.

More recently, South Korea's Danuri (Korea Pathfinder Lunar Orbiter) captured images of the impact site. Those photos, shared by space agencies, show a distinct double crater — a feature that surprised some scientists. The twin depression suggests the rocket body may have had two large masses at either end, which struck the surface nearly simultaneously.

Space.com reported that the images from Danuri provide the clearest view yet of the damage caused by the 2022 crash. The crater's shape offers clues about the structure of the rocket stage and the angle at which it hit.

Space debris concerns resurface

The incident has once again brought the issue of space debris to the fore. The Falcon 9 upper stage was not tracked with precision after its 2015 launch, and its uncontrolled re-entry into lunar space was a reminder that human junk is not confined to Earth's orbit.

Scientists and policy experts have long warned about the growing volume of defunct satellites, spent rocket bodies, and other debris circling both Earth and the Moon. Unlike Earth, where atmosphere burns up most falling objects, the Moon has no such protection, so any debris that reaches it simply crashes into the surface, leaving permanent scars.

The 2022 impact was not the first time a human-made object has struck the Moon, but it was among the largest in recent years. The event has prompted calls for better tracking of deep-space hardware and for international guidelines on disposing of spacecraft at the end of their missions.

While Musk's simulation video may have blurred the line between reality and visualisation, the underlying problem of space litter remains very real. As more countries and private companies plan lunar missions, the risk of further accidental impacts will only grow.

Going forward, space agencies and regulators will be watching closely to see whether new debris-mitigation rules are adopted before the next wave of Moon-bound launches begins.
