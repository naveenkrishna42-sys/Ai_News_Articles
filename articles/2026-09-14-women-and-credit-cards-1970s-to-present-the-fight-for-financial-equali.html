Why the History of Women and Credit Cards Still Matters

In the 1970s, a woman in the United States could be denied a credit card simply because of her gender. Banks demanded a male co-signer, often a husband or father, before issuing a card—even if she had a stable job. This wasn't anecdotal; it was standard practice until the Equal Credit Opportunity Act (ECOA) of 1974 made such discrimination illegal. That law, and its 1976 amendment to include race, religion, and national origin, transformed credit access for women. Today, women are a driving force in the global credit card market. In India, issuers actively court female customers with tailored rewards and faster approvals. Yet the journey from exclusion to empowerment is not complete. Understanding this history helps buyers assess whether today's cards truly serve women's financial needs.

The 1970s: Legal Barriers and the Fight for Financial Independence

Before the ECOA, a single woman could be required to provide a male co-signer to get a credit card, and married women often needed their husband's permission. Even after the law passed, enforcement was slow. Banks continued to ask intrusive questions about marital status and childbearing plans. In India, the scenario was different but equally restrictive: credit was largely a male domain, with women rarely holding cards in their own name. The turning point in the US came when women like Loretta Lynn and other public figures spoke out, and legal challenges forced banks to change. By the 1980s, women began obtaining cards independently, but credit limits remained lower than men's for similar incomes—a gap that persists in subtle forms even today.

India's Credit Card Revolution: Where Women Stand Now

India's credit card market has exploded over the past two decades. As of 2026, there are over 100 million credit cards in circulation, and women account for a growing share—roughly 30% of new issuances, according to industry estimates. Banks like HDFC, ICICI, SBI Card, and Axis have launched cards specifically designed for women, offering cashback on online shopping, dining, and grocery spends. The approval process has also become more inclusive, with many issuers using alternative data like utility payments and mobile recharges to assess creditworthiness. However, women still receive lower average credit limits than men, and many remain unaware of the benefits they can leverage. The rise of fintech and UPI-linked credit has further democratised access, but financial literacy remains a hurdle.

What This Means for Today's Buyer

If you're a woman applying for a credit card in India, the market is more welcoming than ever—but not all cards are equal. Look for cards that offer cashback on categories you actually spend on: online shopping, groceries, dining, and bill payments. Some cards, like the SBI Card SimplyCLICK or the Amazon Pay ICICI Card, provide 5% cashback on Amazon and 2% on other online spends. Annual fees range from free to ₹999, but many waive the fee if you meet a spending threshold. Your credit limit depends on income, credit score, and the issuer's internal policies; women with a stable income and a good CIBIL score (750+) can negotiate for higher limits. Always check the fine print for fuel surcharge waivers, reward point expiry, and late payment fees.

  • Con: Financial literacy gaps can lead to missed rewards or unnecessary fees.

The Road Ahead: Financial Equality and Smart Choices

The history of women and credit cards is a story of progress, but it's not finished. In India, women are increasingly using credit cards for large purchases, travel, and online shopping, and issuers are responding with better products. Yet the gender gap in credit limits and financial knowledge persists. Buyers should compare cards based on annual fees, reward rates, and acceptance network—not just marketing. As more women build credit histories, they gain leverage to demand better terms. The next decade could see parity in credit access, but only if women actively use and understand the financial tools available. For now, the smart move is to pick a card that matches your spending, pay on time, and monitor your credit score. That's how you turn history into advantage.
