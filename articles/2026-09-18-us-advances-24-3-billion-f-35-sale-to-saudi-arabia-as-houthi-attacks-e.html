US Advances $24.3 Billion F-35 Sale to Saudi Arabia as Houthi Attacks Escalate

✍️ By TIVRA News Editorial Team 📅 2026-09-18 📂 World Original source ↗
Key points
  • The State Department has advanced a $24.3 billion package for up to 50 F-35A jets and munitions to Saudi Arabia, the first such sale to an Arab state.
  • Israel is seeking compensatory advanced weapons systems to preserve its qualitative military edge (QME) under US law.
  • Congress has 30 days to block the sale, but a presidential veto makes rejection unlikely.
  • The deal deepens US-Gulf defence ties as Houthi attacks on Saudi infrastructure and shipping intensify.
  • Buyers in the region will watch delivery timelines and industrial offsets, which could reshape Gulf air power.

Deal Overview: What Washington Approved

The Trump administration has formally advanced a $24.3 billion arms package to Saudi Arabia, centred on up to 50 F-35A Lightning II stealth fighters and associated munitions. The State Department notified Congress this week, setting in motion a 30-day review window. This marks the first proposed transfer of the F-35 to an Arab Gulf state and the largest single US arms sale to Riyadh since the 2017 $110 billion package.

According to CNBC, the deal includes AIM-120 AMRAAM air-to-air missiles, precision-guided bombs, and sustainment contracts. The move comes as Houthi attacks on Saudi oil infrastructure and Red Sea shipping escalate, pressuring Washington to reinforce Gulf air defences.

Technical Specifications and Pricing

The F-35A, manufactured by Lockheed Martin, is a fifth-generation multirole stealth fighter. The package also covers training, spare parts, and a decade of maintenance. Unit flyaway cost for the F-35A has dropped to roughly $80 million in recent lots, but the Saudi package includes a full support ecosystem, driving the $24.3 billion total.

Why the Sale Is Controversial

The deal faces bipartisan resistance over Saudi Arabia's human rights record and the risk of undermining Israel's Qualitative Military Edge (QME). Under US law, any arms sale to a Middle Eastern state must not degrade Israel's ability to counter regional threats. The Jerusalem Post reports that Israel will seek advanced weapons systems as compensation, potentially including additional F-35Is or new electronic warfare platforms.

Intelligence concerns also persist. The New York Times notes that US agencies worry about technology leakage to China, given Saudi Arabia's growing defence ties with Beijing. The F-35's stealth and sensor fusion represent the crown jewels of US air power, and any compromise would be costly.

Regional and Buyer Implications

For Saudi Arabia, the F-35 would transform its air force from a legacy F-15 and Typhoon fleet to a true stealth capability. This would allow Riyadh to penetrate advanced air defences and strike Houthi targets in Yemen with reduced risk. However, delivery timelines stretch to the late 2020s, and pilot training will take years.

Other Gulf buyers, including the UAE and Qatar, will watch closely. If the sale proceeds, it could trigger a regional stealth arms race, with consequences for India's security calculus. India, which operates Russian S-400 systems, may need to reassess its air power balance.

  • Pros: Strengthens US-Gulf alliance; boosts interoperability; creates American jobs; counters Iranian and Houthi threats.
  • Cons: Risks Israel's QME; raises human rights concerns; potential tech leakage to China; may fuel regional arms race.
  • Real-world benchmark: F-35A has a 70%+ mission-capable rate in US service, but export variants may see lower readiness due to supply chain complexity.

Congressional Hurdles and Timeline

Congress has 30 days to pass a joint resolution of disapproval. While such resolutions have succeeded before, a presidential veto would likely require a two-thirds majority to override. Given Republican control of the Senate, the sale is expected to proceed. Delivery could begin in 2028 if contracts are signed by late 2026.

The deal's fate will signal Washington's willingness to prioritise Gulf security over Israeli objections and human rights scrutiny. For defence buyers, it underscores the F-35's status as the West's premier export fighter, despite its cost and political baggage.
