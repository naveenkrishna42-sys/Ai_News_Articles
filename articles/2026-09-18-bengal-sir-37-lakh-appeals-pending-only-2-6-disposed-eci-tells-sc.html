Bengal SIR: 37 Lakh Appeals Pending, Only 2.6% Disposed, ECI Tells SC

The Numbers Behind West Bengal's SIR Appeals Backlog

The Election Commission of India has told the Supreme Court that more than 37 lakh appeals arising from the West Bengal Special Intensive Revision (SIR) exercise remain pending before appellate tribunals. Of these, only around 1.02 lakh have been heard, translating to a disposal rate of roughly 2.6% — under 3% of the total load. The disclosure, reported by Live Law and corroborated by The Hindu, Telegraph India and Scroll.in, places the administrative machinery of the SIR under direct judicial scrutiny.

For citizens who filed appeals after being left off or flagged during the roll revision, the practical question is simple: how long will it take to get a hearing, and what happens to their vote in the interim.

What the Appellate Process Actually Involves

Under the SIR framework, a person excluded from the draft electoral roll can file an appeal before an appellate tribunal designated for the purpose. Each tribunal is expected to hear claims, examine documentary proof of citizenship and residency, and pass an order either restoring or rejecting the claim. The Commission's own submission to the Supreme Court confirms that this second-tier redress mechanism is moving far slower than the volume of claims demands.

Why the Municipal Poll Calendar Raises the Stakes

The backlog is not an abstract statistic. Municipal polls in West Bengal are approaching, and the electoral roll in force at the time of notification determines who can vote. If lakhs of appeals remain undecided when the rolls are finalised, a substantial set of claimants may find themselves without a vote — not because their claim failed, but because no tribunal ever reached it.

That distinction matters legally. An undecided appeal is not a rejected appeal. It is an administrative debt owed by the system to the claimant, and the longer it stays unpaid, the harder it becomes to argue that the process was meaningful.

Operational Bottlenecks, Not Just Volume

Three structural issues explain the gap between filings and hearings. First, tribunal capacity appears fixed while the appeal count scaled into lakhs, creating a queue that grows faster than it clears. Second, documentary verification in SIR matters is inherently slow — each case can require residency proof, identity records and cross-checking against multiple databases. Third, the compressed timeline between the revision exercise and the municipal poll calendar leaves little room for a second pass.

  • Scale mismatch: 37 lakh-plus appeals against a tribunal apparatus designed for far smaller caseloads.
  • Throughput problem: A 2.6% disposal rate means the queue is effectively standing still relative to new filings.
  • Timing risk: Municipal polls create a hard deadline that the appellate process may not meet.
  • Legal exposure: Undecided appeals invite further Supreme Court intervention and possible timeline extensions.
  • Voter impact: Affected individuals face disenfranchisement through delay rather than adjudication.

What This Means for the Road Ahead

The Election Commission's submission effectively concedes that the appellate tier is the weak link in the SIR chain. Remedies available to the Supreme Court include directing additional tribunal benches, setting outer time limits for disposal, or mandating that undecided claimants be permitted to vote subject to later verification. Each option carries trade-offs between speed and verification integrity.

For observers of Indian electoral administration, the West Bengal SIR case is becoming a test of whether large-scale roll revision can be paired with a redress mechanism that actually functions at scale. The 2.6% figure is the clearest available measure of how far that pairing currently falls short.
