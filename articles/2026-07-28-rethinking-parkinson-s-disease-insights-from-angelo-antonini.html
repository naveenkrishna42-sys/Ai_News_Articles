Rethinking Parkinson’s Disease: Insights from Angelo Antonini

📅 2026-07-28 📂 Health Original source ↗
Key points
  • Angelo Antonini advocates for a shift in understanding Parkinson's as a multi-system disorder.
  • Early diagnosis and non-motor symptoms like sleep disorders and depression are gaining focus.
  • Treatment strategies are moving beyond dopamine replacement to include holistic care.

A New Lens on Parkinson's

Parkinson's disease has long been viewed primarily as a motor disorder. But neurologist Angelo Antonini, in a recent interview with EMJ, argues that this perspective is too narrow. He calls for a fundamental shift in how clinicians and researchers approach the condition.

Antonini, a leading figure in movement disorders, emphasises that Parkinson's affects multiple systems in the body. The classic tremors and rigidity are just one part of a much larger picture. Recognising this, he says, is key to improving patient outcomes.

The Importance of Non-Motor Symptoms

One of the central themes of the interview is the critical role of non-motor symptoms. Antonini points out that issues like sleep disturbances, depression, and constipation often appear years before any motor signs. These early indicators, he believes, offer a window for earlier intervention.

“We need to train doctors to look beyond the shaking,” Antonini said. “A patient might come in with a sleep disorder or loss of smell, and that could be the first clue.” He stresses that addressing these symptoms early can significantly improve quality of life.

Diagnosis: Moving Earlier

Antonini is a strong advocate for diagnosing Parkinson's at its earliest stages. Current diagnostic methods rely heavily on motor symptoms, which means the disease has often progressed significantly by the time it is caught. He suggests that biomarkers and advanced imaging could change this.

“We are at a point where we can identify changes in the brain years before the typical symptoms appear,” he explained. “The challenge is making these tools accessible and standardising their use.” Earlier diagnosis could open the door to treatments that slow or halt the disease's progression.

Treatment Beyond Dopamine

For decades, dopamine replacement therapy has been the mainstay of Parkinson's treatment. While effective for motor symptoms, Antonini notes that it does not address the full spectrum of the disease. He calls for a more comprehensive treatment plan that includes physical therapy, cognitive support, and management of non-motor symptoms.

“We cannot rely on a single drug to solve everything,” he said. “A multidisciplinary approach is essential. That means neurologists working with psychiatrists, physiotherapists, and occupational therapists as a team.” Antonini also highlights the potential of new therapies, including gene therapy and deep brain stimulation, though he cautions that more research is needed.

What This Means for Patients

For patients and their families, Antonini's message is one of cautious optimism. He acknowledges that Parkinson's remains a challenging disease with no cure, but he believes that the way we think about it is evolving. The focus is shifting from managing symptoms to understanding the underlying biology and intervening earlier.

“Every patient is different,” he said. “We need to tailor treatments to the individual, not just the diagnosis.” This personalised approach, he argues, could lead to better outcomes and a higher quality of life for millions.

Looking ahead, Antonini expects further advances in imaging and biomarker research to reshape the field. The next few years, he suggests, will be critical in translating these insights into everyday clinical practice.
