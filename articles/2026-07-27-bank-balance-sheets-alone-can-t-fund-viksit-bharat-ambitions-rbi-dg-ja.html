Bank balance sheets alone can't fund Viksit Bharat ambitions: RBI DG Jain

📅 2026-07-27 📂 Banking Original source ↗
Key points
  • RBI DG Jain warns that bank balance sheets are insufficient to finance the Viksit Bharat vision.
  • He calls for a shift towards capital markets, insurance, and pension funds for long-term infrastructure funding.
  • The statement underscores the need for a diversified financial ecosystem to support India's growth targets.

RBI Deputy Governor flags funding gap for Viksit Bharat

Reserve Bank of India Deputy Governor M. Rajeshwar Rao has cautioned that the country's ambitious Viksit Bharat vision cannot be achieved on the back of bank balance sheets alone. Speaking at a banking conference in Mumbai, Rao emphasised that India's infrastructure and development needs far outstrip the lending capacity of the banking system.

He pointed out that banks, constrained by asset-liability mismatches and regulatory limits, cannot be the sole engines of credit for large, long-gestation projects. The government's target of becoming a developed nation by 2047, he argued, will require a fundamental shift in how capital is raised and deployed.

Need to tap capital markets and long-term savings

Rao called for greater reliance on capital markets, insurance companies, and pension funds to bridge the funding gap. These institutions, he noted, are better suited to provide the patient capital required for infrastructure, renewable energy, and industrial modernisation.

“We must move from a bank-dominated system to a market-based one,” Rao said, adding that the National Pension System and insurance sector have significant untapped potential. He urged regulators and policymakers to create incentives for long-term savings to flow into productive assets.

Challenges in the current banking model

The deputy governor highlighted that Indian banks already face high credit-to-deposit ratios in several regions, limiting their ability to expand lending further. Non-performing assets, though reduced, still constrain balance sheets, especially for public sector lenders.

He also warned against over-reliance on short-term deposits to fund long-term projects, calling it a recipe for liquidity stress. The solution, he said, lies in deepening the corporate bond market and encouraging alternative investment funds.

Implications for borrowers and investors

For businesses, Rao’s remarks signal that large infrastructure players may need to look beyond bank loans. They will have to tap bond markets, external commercial borrowings, or equity financing to meet their capital needs.

For retail investors, the shift could mean more opportunities to invest in infrastructure bonds and debt instruments. The RBI, Rao indicated, is working on frameworks to make these products more accessible and transparent.

The statement comes at a time when the government is pushing for a rapid expansion of highways, ports, digital infrastructure, and green energy projects. The total investment required for Viksit Bharat is estimated in the hundreds of lakh crore rupees, a sum that no single sector can provide.

Rao did not provide specific numbers or a timeline for the transition, but his message was clear: the status quo will not suffice. Bankers at the conference acknowledged the challenge but noted that a shift will require coordinated action from the RBI, the government, and market participants.

What to watch next: The RBI is expected to release a discussion paper on deepening the corporate bond market within the next quarter. Market participants will closely watch for any regulatory changes that could make it easier for insurers and pension funds to invest in infrastructure projects.
