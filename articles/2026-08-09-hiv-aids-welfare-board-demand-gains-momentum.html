HIV/AIDS welfare board demand gains momentum

📅 2026-08-09 📂 Health Original source ↗
Key points
  • Association calls for dedicated welfare board for HIV/AIDS affected individuals
  • Move aims to streamline benefits and support services
  • Government response awaited; activists stress urgency

Demand for dedicated board

An association representing people living with HIV/AIDS has urged the government to establish a separate welfare board to address their specific needs. The demand comes amid growing concerns that existing welfare measures are not adequately reaching those affected by the virus.

The association argues that a dedicated board would help streamline access to healthcare, financial assistance, and social security schemes. Activists say such a body could also ensure better monitoring of government programmes and faster redressal of grievances.

Why a separate board is needed

Currently, people living with HIV/AIDS often fall through the cracks, with benefits spread across multiple departments. A single board, the association contends, would consolidate efforts and make support more accessible.

Officials have not yet responded publicly to the proposal. However, the association has submitted a detailed memorandum outlining the board's proposed functions, including pension schemes, medical subsidies, and skill development programmes.

Challenges in implementation

Setting up a new board would require legislative approval and budgetary allocation. Some experts caution that bureaucratic hurdles could delay the process, even if the government agrees in principle.

Activists, however, stress that the need is urgent, pointing to the disproportionate impact of the disease on marginalised communities. They argue that a dedicated board would send a strong signal of the state's commitment to their welfare.

Community response

Support groups have welcomed the demand, noting that a welfare board would give them a stronger voice in policy decisions. Many see it as a step towards reducing stigma and ensuring dignity for those living with the condition.

Some have also called for the inclusion of people living with HIV/AIDS in the board's decision-making bodies, to ensure that policies are shaped by those who understand the ground realities.

What happens next

The onus is now on the government to consider the proposal and initiate consultations with stakeholders. If accepted, the board could become a model for other states to follow, potentially transforming the welfare landscape for thousands of affected families.
