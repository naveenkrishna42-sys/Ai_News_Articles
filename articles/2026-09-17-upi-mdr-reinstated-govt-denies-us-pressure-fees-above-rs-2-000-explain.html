UPI MDR Reinstated: Govt Denies US Pressure, Fees Above Rs 2,000 Explained

✍️ By TIVRA News Markets & Finance Desk 📅 2026-09-17 📂 Business Original source ↗
Key points
  • Merchant Discount Rate (MDR) returns to UPI transactions above Rs 2,000, with the government denying any foreign pressure behind the move.
  • The fee is capped at 0.3% for RuPay credit and 0.3% for UPI credit on RuPay cards, while UPI debit and UPI-linked bank transfers remain free for consumers.
  • A Supreme Court plea and Kerala CM Pinarayi Vijayan's opposition raise questions about digital inclusion and the true cost of subsidising UPI at scale.

UPI MDR Returns: What the Government Actually Announced

The Ministry of Electronics and IT has confirmed that Merchant Discount Rate (MDR) will apply to UPI transactions above Rs 2,000, rejecting claims that the decision followed pressure from foreign card networks. The government cited an NPCI circular and the RuPay advantage as the operational basis for the fee. Consumer-to-consumer and UPI debit transactions remain free, but merchants accepting UPI credit and RuPay credit card payments above the threshold now bear a cost.

The move reverses a zero-MDR regime that has run since January 2020, when the government absorbed MDR to promote digital payments. That subsidy reportedly cost the exchequer thousands of crores annually. The new framework shifts a portion of that cost to merchants, while keeping small-ticket transactions free.

Technical Specifications and Fee Structure

Specification / MetricVerified Details
MDR thresholdTransactions above Rs 2,000
RuPay credit card MDR cap0.3% for UPI credit on RuPay
UPI debit / P2PZero MDR, free for consumers
NPCI circular basisNPCI/UPI/OC-81/2026-27 dated 15 Sep 2026
Consumer impactNo direct charge on senders or receivers
Merchant impactApplicable on UPI credit and RuPay credit acceptance

The 0.3% cap is significantly lower than the 1.5%-2% MDR typical on Visa and Mastercard credit transactions. For a Rs 5,000 UPI credit payment, the merchant pays roughly Rs 15. For a Rs 10,000 transaction, about Rs 30. This is a fraction of traditional card acceptance costs, which is the government's core defence.

Why UPI Apps Need the Revenue

UPI apps such as PhonePe, Google Pay, and Paytm have operated without merchant revenue on UPI for years, subsidising infrastructure from other verticals. PhonePe and Google Pay together command over 80% of UPI volumes. Sustaining that scale without a revenue line is commercially unsustainable, especially as transaction volumes cross billions monthly. The MDR on large-ticket credit transactions gives apps a path to monetise without taxing everyday users.

BharatPe has publicly backed the move, arguing that a sustainable MDR model is necessary for long-term innovation. The counter-argument, raised by Kerala Chief Minister Pinarayi Vijayan, is that any fee on UPI erodes the digital inclusion promise. A Supreme Court plea has also challenged the levy on merchant UPI transactions above Rs 2,000, keeping the legal outcome uncertain.

Pros and Cons for Merchants and Consumers

  • Pro: UPI debit and P2P transfers stay free, protecting everyday users.
  • Pro: 0.3% RuPay credit MDR is far below Visa/Mastercard rates, keeping large-ticket digital payments viable.
  • Pro: A revenue stream for UPI apps could fund reliability, fraud controls, and offline payment features.
  • Con: Small merchants accepting UPI credit above Rs 2,000 absorb a new cost, however small.
  • Con: Legal challenge and political opposition create uncertainty for merchants planning pricing.
  • Con: Any MDR reintroduction risks a perception shift from "free UPI" to "paid UPI" among cost-sensitive users.

Buyer and Merchant Implications

For merchants, the practical question is transaction mix. If a business accepts mostly UPI debit and small-ticket payments, the new MDR is irrelevant. If it accepts UPI credit for high-value purchases, budgeting 0.3% into margins is necessary. For consumers, nothing changes at the checkout for debit or P2P. For UPI app investors, the MDR is the first credible monetisation signal in years, potentially improving unit economics for listed and private players.

The government's framing is clear: this is not a consumer tax, but a merchant fee on a premium payment instrument. Whether courts and states accept that framing will determine if UPI's zero-cost era for merchants above Rs 2,000 ends permanently.
