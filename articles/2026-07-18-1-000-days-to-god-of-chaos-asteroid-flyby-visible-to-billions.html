1,000 Days to ‘God of Chaos’ Asteroid Flyby Visible to Billions

📅 2026-07-18 📂 Science Original source ↗
Key points
  • Apophis, a 'potentially hazardous' asteroid, will fly past Earth on April 13, 2029.
  • New maps suggest up to 90% of the world's population may see it with the naked eye.
  • The event is a 'shared cosmic experience' unlike any recorded in history.

Just 1,000 days remain before the asteroid Apophis — nicknamed the 'God of Chaos' — makes an ultraclose pass by Earth. New maps released by researchers reveal that up to 90% of the global population could witness the event with the naked eye.

Apophis, a 340-metre-wide space rock, will zoom past Earth on April 13, 2029. At its closest, it will come within 31,000 kilometres of the planet's surface — closer than many geostationary satellites. That proximity will make it visible as a bright, fast-moving point of light across much of the Eastern Hemisphere.

A Shared Cosmic Spectacle

Scientists at NASA and other institutions have called the flyby a once-in-a-lifetime opportunity. For the first time in recorded history, an asteroid of this size will pass so close that it becomes visible to billions of people without telescopes or binoculars.

Maps published by Forbes and Live Science show the viewing corridor stretches across Africa, Europe, Asia, and parts of Australia. In India, the asteroid will appear low in the eastern sky just before dawn, moving steadily from south to north.

The event is being described as a 'shared cosmic experience.' No known asteroid has ever offered such widespread visibility during a close approach.

What to Expect in 2029

Apophis will appear as a bright star-like object, moving noticeably against the background stars over the course of a few hours. It will be brightest just before closest approach, around 5:45 p.m. Indian Standard Time on April 13, 2029.

Observers in clear, dark skies will see it best. City dwellers may still spot it if they look away from bright lights. The asteroid's speed — about 7.4 kilometres per second relative to Earth — will make its motion obvious to anyone watching for a few minutes.

No impact is expected. Earlier fears that Apophis might hit Earth in 2029 or 2036 have been ruled out by radar and optical tracking. However, the asteroid remains classified as 'potentially hazardous' because of its size and future orbital paths.

Scientific Opportunity and Public Engagement

Astronomers plan to use the flyby for detailed observations. Telescopes on the ground and in space will study Apophis's shape, spin, and composition. The data could help refine planetary defence strategies.

Space agencies are also considering sending probes to rendezvous with the asteroid during its pass. Such missions could provide close-up images and surface measurements. No official mission has been confirmed yet for the 2029 window.

For the public, the flyby offers a rare chance to connect with the solar system. Amateur astronomers are already planning viewing events. Social media groups have formed to coordinate observations across different time zones.

What Comes Next

As the countdown continues, more maps and viewing guides are expected from observatories worldwide. The next major milestone will be in early 2029, when Apophis becomes bright enough for small telescopes to track.

For now, skywatchers can mark their calendars. April 13, 2029, will be a night when the 'God of Chaos' puts on a show for the entire planet.
