UN Adopts Map Resolution: Africa Gets Its True Size on World Maps

Why the World Map Is About to Look Very Different

The United Nations General Assembly has passed a landmark resolution urging the worldwide adoption of maps that accurately depict the size of the African continent. The vote saw 164 member states in favour, with the United States and a handful of allied countries voting against. India, alongside most of the Global South, backed the measure. The resolution is not merely symbolic; it is a formal recognition that the prevailing Mercator projection has materially distorted how the world understands Africa for more than five centuries.

Cartographers and geoscientists have long argued that the Mercator projection, designed in 1569 for maritime navigation, inflates landmasses near the poles while shrinking equatorial regions. Africa, straddling the equator, is the worst-hit continent. On most classroom and digital maps, it appears comparable in size to Greenland, when in reality the African continent is roughly 14 times larger.

What the UN Resolution Actually Mandates

The resolution, titled "Correct the Map," calls on international organisations, academic institutions, media outlets, and digital mapping platforms to transition toward equal-area projections, most notably the Equal Earth projection developed in 2018 by Bojan Šavrič and colleagues. Unlike Mercator, Equal Earth preserves the relative area of every landmass, making it suitable for statistical, educational, and policy applications.

While the resolution is non-binding on individual map publishers, its diplomatic weight is significant. Major agencies such as the African Union have already endorsed the shift, and pressure is mounting on tech platforms, including Google Maps, to offer accurate projections as default views in educational contexts.

Real-World Implications for Buyers, Educators, and Policymakers

For commercial buyers in geospatial analytics, logistics, and aviation, the shift has operational consequences. Equal-area projections distort shapes but preserve size, making them unsuitable for navigation but ideal for market sizing, demographic studies, and supply-chain planning. Procurement heads should evaluate whether their GIS vendors, such as Esri, Mapbox, and HERE Technologies, support Equal Earth natively or require custom tiling.

For the education sector, publishers including Oxford University Press and Pearson have begun migrating to Equal Earth in new editions. School boards in India, Nigeria, and Brazil are reviewing textbook maps, a market segment worth an estimated USD 1.2 billion annually.

Why the US Voted Against the Resolution

The United States Mission to the UN issued an Explanation of Vote objecting to what it termed the politicisation of cartography. Washington argued that map projection is a technical, not diplomatic, matter and should be left to professional bodies such as the International Cartographic Association. Critics counter that the US position effectively endorses a 16th-century European naval tool as the global standard, a stance increasingly seen as anachronistic.

India's vote in favour reflects a broader diplomatic posture of supporting multipolar narratives and African-led institutions, including the African Union's inclusion in the G20 under India's 2023 presidency.

Key Real-World Highlights

  • Africa is 14 times larger than Greenland, not the same size as Mercator maps suggest.
  • 164 UN members backed the resolution, signalling overwhelming diplomatic consensus.
  • The Equal Earth projection is the leading technical replacement, already in use at NASA.
  • Tech platforms face growing pressure to default to accurate maps in educational tools.
  • Commercial GIS and mapping software must be audited for projection accuracy to remain compliant with institutional procurement standards.

What Happens Next in the Cartographic Shift

Expect a cascade effect across digital platforms. Google Maps has signalled it will study the resolution but has not committed to changing defaults. Meanwhile, the African Union has pledged funding to assist member states in adopting Equal Earth for official documentation, census data, and educational materials within 24 months. For analysts, educators, and procurement professionals, the takeaway is clear: the world map is no longer just a navigational artefact, it is a data artefact, and Africa is finally getting its true dimensions on the page.
