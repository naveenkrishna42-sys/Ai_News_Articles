Iran warns it will target Gulf and Israeli energy sites if US strikes

📅 2026-08-02 📂 World Original source ↗
Key points
  • Iran has identified energy infrastructure in Saudi Arabia, UAE, Qatar and Israel as potential targets.
  • The warning comes amid US threats of strikes on Iranian energy sites.
  • Iran has also discussed the situation with Pakistan and Turkey, warning against further attacks.

Iran's warning amid US strike threats

Iran has explicitly named energy sites in Saudi Arabia, the UAE, Qatar and Israel as potential targets if the United States under President Donald Trump weighs new strikes against the Islamic Republic. The warning, reported by multiple outlets including Moneycontrol and Al Jazeera, signals a dangerous escalation in rhetoric between Tehran and Washington.

An Iranian official, speaking to state-linked media, described a comprehensive plan prepared to respond to what they called "any US madness." The official did not provide operational details, but the message was unambiguous: any American aggression on Iranian soil would trigger retaliatory strikes far beyond the region's usual flashpoints.

Gulf states caught in the crossfire

The naming of Saudi Arabia, the UAE and Qatar is significant. These Gulf nations host key US military facilities, including Al Udeid Air Base in Qatar, which serves as the forward headquarters of US Central Command. Their energy infrastructure—oilfields, refineries and export terminals—forms the backbone of global supply chains.

Iran's threat appears designed to raise the cost of any US action by widening the theatre of conflict. If Washington strikes Iranian nuclear or oil facilities, Tehran could retaliate by hitting its neighbours' energy assets, potentially disrupting global markets and drawing regional powers into a broader war.

Previous attacks, such as the 2019 strikes on Saudi Aramco's Abqaiq facility, demonstrated the vulnerability of Gulf energy infrastructure. A repeat, this time orchestrated by Iran, could send oil prices soaring and trigger a global economic shock.

Diplomatic outreach amid tensions

In parallel, Iran has engaged in talks with Pakistan and Turkey, warning against further US attacks. These diplomatic efforts suggest Tehran is seeking to shore up regional support while signalling its red lines. Pakistan and Turkey, both Muslim-majority nations with complex ties to Washington and Tehran, could play mediating roles—or choose to stay on the sidelines.

Iran's warning also extends to Israel, a long-standing adversary. Israeli energy sites, including offshore gas platforms in the eastern Mediterranean, are within range of Iranian missiles and proxy forces. The threat adds a new dimension to the already volatile Israel-Iran shadow war, which has seen tit-for-tat strikes in Syria and beyond.

What the 'flames of war' rhetoric means

Iran's use of the phrase "flames of war" is a deliberate escalation in tone. It suggests that Tehran views the current standoff as a potential inferno that could consume the entire region. The warning is as much about deterrence as it is about preparing for the worst-case scenario.

US officials have not yet formally responded to Iran's latest threat. However, the Trump administration has repeatedly stated its willingness to use military force to prevent Iran from acquiring nuclear weapons. The two sides remain far apart, with no apparent channel for direct communication.

Global markets on edge

The mere mention of Gulf energy targets has already sent ripples through oil markets, with traders bracing for potential supply disruptions. Analysts note that any actual strike on Saudi or Emirati infrastructure could remove millions of barrels per day from global supply, pushing prices to levels not seen in years.

For now, the situation remains a standoff of words and threats. Neither side appears willing to back down, but neither has yet taken the fateful step of launching a direct military strike. The next move could come from Washington, which may decide to act on its threats—or seek a diplomatic off-ramp.

What happens next depends on whether President Trump chooses to authorise new strikes, and how Iran's leadership interprets the signals from Washington. The region holds its breath, with the prospect of a multi-front conflict looming larger than at any point in recent memory.
