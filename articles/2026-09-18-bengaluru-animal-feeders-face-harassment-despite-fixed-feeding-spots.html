Bengaluru Animal Feeders Face Harassment Despite Fixed Feeding Spots

The Ground Reality Behind Bengaluru's Feeding Spot Rules

Bengaluru's street animal feeders operate in a peculiar legal grey zone. The BBMP's Animal Birth Control (Dogs) Rules, 2019, backed by Animal Welfare Board of India (AWBI) circulars, mandate that stray dogs be fed at designated spots identified jointly by feeders and Resident Welfare Associations (RWAs). On paper, this protects feeders from harassment. On the ground, the protection is fragile.

Feeders across wards report that even at officially marked spots, they face fines, equipment confiscation, and intimidation from residents and, in some cases, local officials. The core dispute is rarely about feeding itself; it is about who controls public space and how much discretion RWAs wield in identifying those spots.

What the Rules Actually Say

The 2019 rules and subsequent AWBI directions are unambiguous on three points: feeding cannot be criminalised, designated spots must be accessible, and feeders cannot be arbitrarily evicted from those spots. The Supreme Court has also weighed in, holding that feeding stray dogs is a legitimate expression of compassion and cannot be prohibited outright.

Yet compliance depends almost entirely on local interpretation. There is no central registry of designated spots, no standard signage, and no uniform escalation mechanism when disputes arise. This leaves feeders exposed to ward-level whims.

Why Designated Spots Still Fail

The designation process assumes a cooperative relationship between feeders and RWAs. In practice, many RWAs refuse to participate or withdraw cooperation after spots are marked. Feeders then operate without formal cover, making them easy targets.

Enforcement is equally inconsistent. Some wards have trained marshals who understand the rules; others rely on security guards or residents who do not. The result is a postcode lottery: the same act of feeding is tolerated in one neighbourhood and penalised in the next.

Real-World Impact on Feeders

  • Financial burden: Repeated fines and replacement of seized equipment drain feeders who often fund feeding from their own pockets.
  • Legal fatigue: Feeders must repeatedly produce rule copies and AWBI circulars to assert their rights, a time-consuming process.
  • Safety concerns: Women feeders, in particular, report intimidation and avoid feeding after dark.
  • Animal welfare fallout: Disrupted feeding schedules affect sterilisation and vaccination drives, since feeders are often the primary point of contact for ABC programmes.
  • Positive outliers: Some wards with proactive marshals and cooperative RWAs have seen disputes drop sharply, showing the model can work.

Buyer Guide: What Feeders and RWAs Should Know

If you feed strays in Bengaluru, document everything. Carry a copy of the 2019 rules and relevant AWBI circulars. Photograph the designated spot with a timestamp. Register complaints with the BBMP Animal Husbandry wing and the local police station if harassment escalates. For RWAs, the practical route is to formalise spots in writing and display signage, which reduces ad hoc disputes.

For those funding feeders or animal welfare groups, the value proposition is straightforward: a modest monthly contribution of Rs 1,500 to Rs 3,000 covers food and basic equipment for one feeder, directly supporting sterilisation compliance in that area. That is far cheaper than the long-term cost of unchecked stray populations.

The Way Forward

A public, ward-wise registry of designated feeding spots would remove much of the ambiguity. Standard signage, training for marshals and security staff, and a single grievance portal would cut down on repeat disputes. Until then, feeders will continue to rely on legal awareness and community support to hold their ground.
