Iran's military strategy shifts focus to proxies and missile deterrence since June 2025 war

📅 2026-07-18 📂 Wars & Conflicts Original source ↗
Key points
  • Iran's military strategy now prioritizes proxy forces and missile deterrence after the June 2025 war
  • The shift aims to avoid direct confrontation while maintaining regional influence
  • Changes reflect lessons learned from the conflict and evolving threats

A new doctrine emerges

Iran's military strategy has undergone a significant transformation since the June 2025 war. The conflict, which lasted several weeks and involved direct exchanges with Israel, forced Tehran to rethink its approach to regional power projection. The new doctrine places greater emphasis on proxy forces and missile deterrence while reducing the risk of direct state-on-state confrontation.

Analysts tracking Iranian military developments note that the shift is not abrupt but represents an acceleration of trends visible before the war. The Islamic Revolutionary Guard Corps (IRGC) has reportedly restructured its command and control systems to decentralize decision-making. This allows proxy groups in Lebanon, Syria, Iraq and Yemen to operate with greater autonomy while still aligning with Tehran's strategic goals.

Lessons from the battlefield

The June 2025 war exposed vulnerabilities in Iran's conventional military capabilities. Israeli air strikes targeted key Iranian assets, including air defense systems and missile production facilities. In response, Iran has focused on hardening its missile infrastructure and dispersing launch sites. Underground bunkers and mobile launchers have become central to the new strategy.

Another key lesson was the effectiveness of drone warfare. Iran has invested heavily in drone technology, both offensive and defensive. The war demonstrated that swarms of low-cost drones can overwhelm advanced air defense systems. Tehran now supplies these systems to its proxies, enabling them to strike deeper into enemy territory without direct Iranian involvement.

Proxy forces take center stage

The role of proxy forces has expanded significantly since the war. Groups like Hezbollah in Lebanon, the Houthis in Yemen and various militias in Iraq and Syria now receive more advanced weaponry and intelligence support. Iran's strategy is to create a multi-front deterrent that can pressure Israel and its allies without triggering a full-scale war.

This approach has practical advantages. Proxies allow Iran to project power across the region while maintaining plausible deniability. If a proxy group attacks an Israeli or American target, Tehran can claim it was not directly involved. This reduces the risk of retaliatory strikes on Iranian soil, a key lesson from the June conflict.

Nuclear program as a bargaining chip

Iran's nuclear program remains a central pillar of its military strategy, but its role has shifted. Before the war, the program was seen primarily as a path to a nuclear weapon. Now, it serves as a bargaining chip in negotiations with world powers. Iran has increased its uranium enrichment levels since the war, signaling that it can quickly weaponize if threatened.

This dual-track strategy — building proxy capabilities while advancing the nuclear program — gives Iran leverage in diplomatic talks. The regime calculates that the threat of a nuclear breakout, combined with the ability to strike via proxies, creates a powerful deterrent against any future attacks. However, this approach also risks further isolation and potential military action from the US or Israel.

Economic constraints shape strategy

Economic pressures continue to limit Iran's military options. International sanctions have severely reduced Iran's oil revenue, making it difficult to fund large-scale military modernization. The IRGC has responded by focusing on asymmetric warfare, which is cheaper and more effective against technologically superior adversaries.

The shift to proxy warfare also has an economic logic. Supporting militias in Yemen or Lebanon costs a fraction of maintaining a conventional army. Iran also uses its proxies to disrupt rival economies, such as Saudi Arabia's oil infrastructure, creating strategic parity without matching their spending.

Looking ahead, Iran's military strategy will likely remain focused on deterrence through proxies and missiles. The June 2025 war confirmed that direct confrontation is costly, but Tehran has shown it can adapt. The next test will be whether this strategy can prevent another conflict or merely delay it.
