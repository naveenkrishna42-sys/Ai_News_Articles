India Names 27 Arunachal Sites in Counter to China's Map Moves

📅 2026-08-08 📂 Analysis Original source ↗
Key points
  • India has officially assigned standard names to 27 locations in Arunachal Pradesh, formalising their presence on national maps.
  • The move is a direct response to China's repeated renaming of Indian territory in the region.
  • Officials say the naming exercise aims to strengthen administrative and strategic clarity along the border.

New Delhi has formally assigned official names to 27 locations in Arunachal Pradesh, a decisive administrative counter to China's persistent attempts to rename Indian territory along the Line of Actual Control (LAC). The Union government's Survey of India has standardised these names for use on all national maps, effectively drawing a cartographic line that Beijing's map-makers cannot cross.

The naming exercise covers strategic locations, including border outposts, river confluences, and mountain passes, many of which have appeared under Chinese-assigned names on foreign maps and in Beijing's official communications. By fixing these names in the Indian cartographic record, the government is signalling that nomenclature is not a trivial matter when it comes to sovereignty.

A Pattern of Provocation

China has, over the past several years, repeatedly assigned Chinese names to villages, peaks, and rivers in Arunachal Pradesh, which it claims as part of its territory. These renaming attempts have often been accompanied by the publication of maps showing the region as part of China's Tibet Autonomous Region. Beijing's nomenclature offensive has targeted at least 30 locations in the state, according to official assessments.

India's response has been deliberate and calibrated. The naming of these 27 locations is not a one-off gesture but part of a sustained effort to consolidate administrative control over the region. The Survey of India, the country's top mapping agency, has been tasked with ensuring that all official documents and public platforms use the newly standardised names.

The locations span several districts, including Tawang, Upper Subansiri, and Anjaw, areas that have witnessed standoffs between Indian and Chinese troops in recent years. The names themselves are drawn from local tribal languages and historical usage, an attempt to ground the cartographic exercise in the region's cultural reality.

Why It Matters

Map wars may seem symbolic, but they carry strategic weight. In international diplomacy, maps are tools of persuasion and evidence. A name on a map is a claim, and a standardised name is a claim repeated across every official document, every ministry file, and every multilateral forum. India's action ensures that no Indian map, whether issued by the central government or a state agency, shows anything other than the officially recognised names.

The timing is significant. The move comes amid heightened military deployment on both sides of the LAC, with multiple rounds of troop disengagement talks having failed to fully resolve the standoff. By acting on maps, India is making a quiet but firm point: administrative normalcy will continue even as military negotiations proceed.

Officials in New Delhi have not disclosed the full list of the 27 locations, citing operational sensitivities. However, they have confirmed that the exercise was completed after consultations with the Arunachal Pradesh government, local communities, and security agencies. The state government has welcomed the move, viewing it as recognition of the region's indigenous heritage.

The Broader Context

India's action is not isolated. In 2021, the government renamed a road in Ladakh after a key battle, and it has consistently objected to China's use of the term 'South Tibet' for Arunachal Pradesh. China, for its part, has rejected India's naming exercise, with its foreign ministry calling it a violation of Chinese sovereignty. Beijing's official maps continue to show Arunachal Pradesh as part of China, a position that New Delhi has repeatedly rejected.

The naming exercise also has domestic political dimensions. Arunachal Pradesh is a border state with significant tribal diversity, and local leaders have often complained that New Delhi pays little attention to their concerns. By consulting local communities and using indigenous names, the government is attempting to build a sense of ownership over the cartographic process.

Analysts point out that the move is unlikely to change Beijing's behaviour on the ground, but it does create a clearer legal and administrative framework for India's claims. International courts and tribunals, when adjudicating border disputes, often rely on maps and official documents as evidence. A well-documented naming exercise strengthens India's case, should the dispute ever be taken to a third-party forum.

What to Watch

The coming weeks will reveal whether China responds with further renaming attempts or shifts to other forms of pressure. India's next step may be to publish the full list of names along with coordinates, a move that would make the exercise more difficult for Beijing to ignore. The government is also expected to push for the use of these names in international forums, including the United Nations, where maps are routinely circulated. For now, the map war has a clear new front line, and India has drawn it.
