From Ayurveda to yoga: India's global wellness footprint grows

📅 2026-07-17 📂 Lifestyle Original source ↗
Key points
  • Ayurveda and yoga are gaining mainstream acceptance worldwide, with growing scientific research backing their benefits.
  • India's Ministry of AYUSH is actively promoting these traditions through international collaborations and events.
  • The global wellness industry, valued at over $4.5 trillion, is seeing a significant Indian influence.

The ancient roots of modern wellness

For centuries, India's wellness traditions—Ayurveda and yoga—remained largely within its borders. That is changing rapidly. Today, these ancient practices are reshaping how the world approaches health, from Manhattan yoga studios to European spas offering Panchakarma treatments.

The global wellness economy, now valued at over $4.5 trillion, has embraced India's holistic approach. Yoga, once seen as a niche practice, has become a mainstream fitness and mental health tool. The International Yoga Day, proposed by Prime Minister Narendra Modi and adopted by the UN in 2014, is now celebrated in over 190 countries.

Ayurveda goes global

Ayurveda, the 5,000-year-old system of natural healing, is no longer confined to Kerala's traditional clinics. German tourists fly to India for authentic treatments. American wellness brands sell turmeric lattes and ashwagandha supplements. The global Ayurveda market is projected to reach $14.9 billion by 2028, according to industry reports.

The Indian government's Ministry of AYUSH has signed over 60 memoranda of understanding with countries to promote Ayurveda and yoga. These agreements cover research, education, and practice standards. Countries like Switzerland, Hungary, and Japan now have official Ayurveda centres recognised by Indian authorities.

Scientific validation drives acceptance

What was once dismissed as alternative medicine is now backed by rigorous science. Studies published in journals like the Journal of Alternative and Complementary Medicine have shown yoga's efficacy in managing chronic pain, anxiety, and hypertension. The US National Institutes of Health has funded research on Ayurveda's potential in treating diabetes and arthritis.

This validation is key. Western doctors, once sceptical, are now referring patients to yoga therapists. Insurance companies in some countries cover Ayurvedic treatments. The shift is not just about trends—it is about data.

India's soft power play

India has consciously used these traditions as diplomatic tools. The Ministry of External Affairs funds yoga centres in Indian missions abroad. The government has established the World Yoga Congress and the Global Ayurveda Festival to showcase expertise. In 2023, India hosted the first WHO Global Traditional Medicine Centre in Jamnagar.

Critics point out that commodification dilutes authenticity. Some worry that western interpretations strip practices of their spiritual core. Yet, the economic and cultural benefits are undeniable. India earns over $1 crore annually from medical tourism, a large chunk of which comes from wellness travellers.

Challenges remain

Despite the growth, challenges persist. Standardisation of training and practice is a major issue. There is no global regulatory framework for Ayurveda. Unqualified practitioners abroad have led to cases of heavy metal poisoning from improperly prepared herbal medicines.

India's AYUSH ministry has responded by developing certification programmes for international teachers and therapists. The government is also pushing for the inclusion of Ayurveda in the WHO's International Classification of Diseases.

What happens next depends on how India manages this global shift. The demand is real, but so is the responsibility to ensure quality and safety. The world is watching—and rolling out its yoga mats.
