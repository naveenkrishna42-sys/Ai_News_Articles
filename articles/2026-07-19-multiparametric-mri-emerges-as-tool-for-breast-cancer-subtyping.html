Multiparametric MRI Emerges as Tool for Breast Cancer Subtyping

📅 2026-07-19 📂 Health Original source ↗
Key points
  • Multiparametric MRI can distinguish between different breast cancer subtypes with high accuracy.
  • The approach offers a non-invasive alternative to repeated biopsies for treatment monitoring.
  • Larger trials are needed before the technique can enter routine clinical use.

Non-Invasive Subtype Classification

A new study suggests that multiparametric magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) could help doctors identify breast cancer subtypes without the need for repeated tissue biopsies. Researchers found that the imaging technique, which combines multiple MRI sequences, can distinguish between different molecular subtypes of the disease.

The findings, reported by EMJ, offer a potential step towards more personalised treatment decisions. Currently, subtype classification relies on tissue samples obtained through invasive procedures.

How the Technique Works

Multiparametric MRI captures several different tissue characteristics in a single scan. It measures features such as blood flow, cell density, and water diffusion. By analysing these combined signals, the algorithm can predict the underlying biology of a tumour.

In the study, the approach showed particular promise in differentiating between hormone receptor-positive, HER2-positive, and triple-negative breast cancers. These distinctions are critical because each subtype responds to different therapies.

Why It Matters for Patients

If validated in larger trials, multiparametric MRI could spare patients from frequent biopsies. Biopsies are not only uncomfortable but also carry risks such as bleeding or infection. Moreover, a single biopsy may not capture the full heterogeneity of a tumour.

Non-invasive imaging could allow clinicians to monitor how a tumour changes over time, especially during treatment. This could lead to earlier detection of resistance or the need for a therapy switch.

Limitations and Next Steps

The study is promising, but experts caution that the technique is not yet ready for widespread clinical use. The current findings are based on a relatively small sample size. Larger, multi-centre trials are needed to confirm the accuracy and reproducibility of the method.

Another challenge is standardising the MRI protocols across different hospitals. Without standardisation, results may vary from one centre to another. Researchers are working on developing uniform guidelines for image acquisition and analysis.

Regulatory approval will also be required before the technique can be adopted as a standard diagnostic tool. The path from research to clinical practice typically takes several years.

What happens next: Researchers are planning larger validation studies to confirm the findings and refine the imaging protocol. If successful, multiparametric MRI could become a routine tool for guiding breast cancer treatment decisions within the next five to ten years.
