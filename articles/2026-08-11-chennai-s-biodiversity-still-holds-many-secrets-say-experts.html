Chennai's biodiversity still holds many secrets, say experts

📅 2026-08-11 📂 Chennai Original source ↗
Key points
  • Experts highlight gaps in knowledge about Chennai's biodiversity
  • Urbanisation poses threats to local ecosystems and species
  • Calls for more research and citizen science initiatives

Chennai's natural world is far richer than most people realise, but much of it remains a mystery. Experts speaking at a recent forum on urban ecology said that the city's biological diversity is still largely undocumented, with many species and habitats awaiting formal study.

An Understudied Urban Ecosystem

Chennai's landscape — from the Adyar estuary and Pallikaranai marsh to the scrub forests of the outskirts — supports a surprising variety of life. Yet researchers argue that the city's biodiversity has not received the attention it deserves.

"There is much more to unravel about Chennai's biological diversity," said one ecologist at the event, pointing to the need for comprehensive surveys of flora and fauna in both protected and unprotected areas.

Many of the city's wetlands, for instance, are seasonal and highly dynamic. Their species composition can shift dramatically between monsoon and dry months, making them difficult to study without year-round monitoring.

Threats from Rapid Urbanisation

Urban expansion continues to encroach on natural habitats. Wetlands are being filled, tree cover is being lost, and pollution is affecting water bodies. These pressures are not just environmental — they also have direct consequences for human well-being, from flood regulation to air quality.

Experts noted that some species are adapting to city life, but others are not. The loss of native vegetation, for example, has reduced the availability of food and shelter for birds, butterflies, and small mammals.

"We are losing biodiversity before we even know what exists," warned another participant, urging local authorities to integrate ecological data into city planning.

The Role of Citizen Science

Researchers believe that public participation can play a key role in filling the knowledge gap. Birdwatchers, nature enthusiasts, and school students have already contributed valuable observations through platforms like eBird and iNaturalist.

Such data, experts say, can help identify critical habitats that need protection and guide conservation efforts. They also stress the importance of documenting traditional knowledge held by local communities, which often includes insights about native plants and animals that formal science has yet to record.

"Every observation counts," said a naturalist who has been documenting Chennai's biodiversity for over two decades. "But we need more people looking, and we need more systematic studies."

A Call for Long-Term Research

While short-term surveys are useful, experts emphasise the need for long-term ecological research. Monitoring plots, seasonal sampling, and genetic studies are essential to understand how species are responding to climate change and urban pressures.

Universities and research institutions in Chennai are being encouraged to adopt local sites as living laboratories. Such initiatives could provide the data needed to make informed decisions about development and conservation.

There is also a need for better public awareness. Many residents are unaware of the biodiversity in their own neighbourhoods — from the birds in their parks to the insects in their gardens.

"If people know what lives around them, they are more likely to care about protecting it," an organiser of the forum said.

As Chennai continues to grow, the challenge will be to balance development with the preservation of its natural heritage. The next few years could be critical, as new infrastructure projects and housing plans take shape.

Officials have not yet announced any specific policy changes following the forum. However, experts hope that the discussions will lead to concrete steps, including more funding for biodiversity research and stronger enforcement of wetland protection rules.

What remains clear is that Chennai's biological wealth is far from fully mapped. Whether it will be studied in time to save what is still there is an open question — and one that will shape the city's ecological future.
