EU Allows Greek LNG Exemptions in New Russia Sanctions Deal

📅 2026-07-23 📂 World Original source ↗
Key points
  • The EU's 21st sanctions package includes a concession to Greece on LNG imports.
  • Greek concerns about energy security and economic impact led to the exemption.
  • The deal maintains the oil price cap while targeting Russian energy and financial services.

Athens Wins Concession in Brussels

The European Union has agreed to exempt certain Greek liquefied natural gas (LNG) shipments from its latest round of sanctions on Russia. The decision came after Athens pushed back against measures that could have disrupted its energy trade and broader economy.

The exemption forms part of the EU's 21st sanctions package, which targets Russian energy exports, financial services, and cryptocurrency transactions. The package also locks in an existing oil price cap designed to limit Moscow's revenue from crude sales.

Why Greece Pushed for the Exception

Greek officials argued that blanket restrictions on Russian LNG would harm domestic energy security. The country relies on gas imports for power generation and industrial use, and a sudden cutoff could have led to supply shortfalls and price spikes.

Beyond energy, Athens flagged the economic fallout for its shipping and trading sectors. Greece is home to one of the world's largest merchant fleets, and many Greek-owned vessels are involved in LNG transportation. A strict ban would have hit ship owners and related businesses hard.

European diplomats said the compromise allows specific LNG cargoes to move freely, provided they meet agreed criteria. The exact volume and duration of the exemption have not been disclosed.

What the New Sanctions Cover

The broader package, agreed after weeks of negotiation among member states, introduces fresh restrictions on Russian banks and financial firms. It also tightens controls on crypto assets, aiming to close loopholes that Moscow has used to bypass earlier sanctions.

On energy, the EU has reaffirmed the oil price cap mechanism, which forces buyers to pay no more than a set amount per barrel. The cap has been a key tool in reducing Russia's oil revenues while keeping global markets supplied.

Russian gas cargoes remain largely exempt from direct sanctions, a point of contention among some Eastern European members who wanted tougher action. The Financial Times has reported that the exemption will persist under the new deal.

Mixed Reactions Across the Bloc

The Greek concession drew criticism from some EU states that favour a harder line against Moscow. They argue that exceptions weaken the bloc's collective stance and create loopholes Russia can exploit.

Others, however, see the move as pragmatic. The EU has repeatedly balanced deterrence against economic self-harm since the invasion of Ukraine began. The Greek LNG exemption follows a pattern of targeted flexibility, similar to past carve-outs for Hungarian oil imports and Bulgarian nuclear fuel.

The European Commission has not commented on whether further exemptions are under consideration.

What Comes Next

The 21st sanctions package now moves to formal adoption by the Council of the EU, expected within days. Member states will then begin implementing the measures at the national level.

Attention will shift to enforcement. The EU has struggled to prevent sanctions evasion, particularly through third countries. The new rules on crypto and financial services aim to close some of those gaps, but officials acknowledge that monitoring remains a challenge.
