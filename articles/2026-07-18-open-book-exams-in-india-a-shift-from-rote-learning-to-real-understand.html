Open Book Exams in India: A Shift from Rote Learning to Real Understanding

📅 2026-07-18 📂 Education Original source ↗
Key points
  • Open book exams aim to test comprehension and application, not memorisation.
  • Pilot programmes in some Indian universities show mixed results on stress and cheating.
  • Implementation challenges include question design, teacher training, and digital divide.

The Push for a New Exam Format

India's education system is taking a hard look at its reliance on rote learning. The Central Board of Secondary Education (CBSE) and several state boards are testing open book exams as a way to shift focus from memorisation to deeper understanding. The idea is simple: let students use textbooks and notes during the test, then ask questions that require analysis, synthesis, or problem-solving.

Proponents argue that the current system rewards short-term recall, not genuine learning. Open book exams, they say, better prepare students for real-world scenarios where information is readily available but critical thinking is not. In 2023, the University of Delhi piloted open book exams for some undergraduate papers, and the National Education Policy 2020 explicitly encourages such reforms.

Early Results: Less Cheating, But New Problems

Pilot programmes have produced mixed outcomes. In Delhi University's trial, students reported lower stress during the exam because they did not have to memorise vast amounts of content. However, faculty noted that poorly designed questions still rewarded copying from the textbook. When questions were well-crafted—requiring students to compare two historical events or apply a mathematical concept to a novel problem—performance varied widely.

Surprisingly, some studies found that open book exams reduced the incidence of cheating. When students are allowed to consult materials openly, the incentive to smuggle notes or copy from peers drops. But the digital divide remains a hurdle. In rural schools, where internet access is patchy, online open book exams can disadvantage students who cannot afford devices or stable connectivity.

What Makes a Good Open Book Question?

Educators say the key is question design. A good open book question cannot be answered by simply flipping to a page. For example, instead of asking 'What is the formula for photosynthesis?', a better question would be 'Design an experiment to test how light intensity affects photosynthesis in a local plant species, using only the materials listed in your textbook.' This forces students to understand and apply concepts, not just recall facts.

Training teachers to write such questions is a significant challenge. Many current exam setters are used to straightforward recall-based questions, and shifting their approach requires workshops, sample papers, and time. Without this investment, open book exams risk becoming a glorified scavenger hunt.

The Road Ahead: Implementation and Equity

For open book exams to succeed, policymakers must address infrastructure gaps. The Department of School Education and Literacy is considering a phased rollout, starting with subjects like social studies and science in select schools. The plan includes providing digital textbooks and offline resources for students without internet access.

Another concern is evaluation. Grading open book answers requires more nuanced rubrics, as there is rarely a single correct response. This could increase teacher workload and require additional training in assessment design. The National Assessment Centre is developing model question banks to guide educators.

What to watch: In the next academic year, CBSE is expected to release a detailed framework for open book exams in Classes 9 and 10. The rollout will likely be gradual, with lessons from pilot schools shaping the final policy.
