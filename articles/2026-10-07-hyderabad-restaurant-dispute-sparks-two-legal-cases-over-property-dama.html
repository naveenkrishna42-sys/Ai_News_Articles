Hyderabad Restaurant Dispute Sparks Two Legal Cases Over Property Damage and Police Obstruction

Overview of the Hyderabad Restaurant Dispute

On 5 October 2026, a verbal altercation at a popular Banjara Hills restaurant escalated into a formal police complaint, resulting in two distinct First Information Reports (FIRs). The establishment, known for its South Indian cuisine, allegedly undertook interior renovations that extended beyond its sanctioned plot, encroaching onto a municipal footpath. Local residents reported the obstruction, prompting a patrol to intervene.

Incident Details and Parties Involved

The restaurant’s management claimed the work was approved by the building’s owner and that they had obtained a no‑objection certificate from the local corporation. However, municipal officials present at the scene stated that no such permission had been granted for the footpath extension. As officers attempted to document the violation, the restaurant staff reportedly blocked their access to the premises, leading to allegations of obstruction of duty.

Legal Action: Property Damage and Police Obstruction Cases

Following the incident, the Banjara Hills police station registered FIR No. 112/2026 under Section 427 of the Indian Penal Code (IPC) for mischief causing damage to public property. A second FIR, No. 113/2026, was lodged under Section 186 IPC for obstructing a public servant in the discharge of his functions. Both reports cite eyewitness accounts from nearby shop owners and the patrol team’s body‑camera footage as corroborative evidence.

Implications for Restaurant Operators and Public Safety

Legal practitioners note that a conviction under Section 427 could attract imprisonment up to two years, a fine, or both, while Section 186 carries a maximum penalty of three months’ imprisonment or a fine. Beyond criminal liability, the restaurant may face civil claims for restitution of the altered footpath and potential revocation of its trade licence by the Greater Hyderabad Municipal Corporation.

Industry analysts advise hospitality businesses to secure written clearances for any structural modifications, maintain copies of sanctioned plans, and engage in pre‑emptive dialogue with civic authorities to avoid similar confrontations. The case also highlights the need for municipal bodies to streamline the approval process and provide accessible channels for dispute resolution.

Key Takeaways

  • Two FIRs were filed: one for property damage (IPC 427) and one for obstructing public servants (IPC 186).
  • The dispute originated from alleged unauthorized extension onto a municipal footpath.
  • Restaurant owners risk criminal penalties, financial compensation, and licence scrutiny.
  • Clear documentation and proactive communication with local bodies can mitigate such risks.
  • The incident underscores broader challenges in balancing urban development with commercial activity in Hyderabad.
