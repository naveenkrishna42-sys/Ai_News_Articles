Taslima Nasrin returns to Kolkata after 19-year exile

Key points
  • Taslima Nasrin arrives in Kolkata after 19 years of forced exile
  • She describes the visit as politically symbolic and emotionally significant
  • Nasrin says she feels 'very good' to return to the city she once called home

A City Revisited

Kolkata, July 31: Taslima Nasrin stepped onto Kolkata soil on Friday, ending a 19-year absence that began with death threats and a bounty on her head. The exiled Bangladeshi author, who has spent nearly two decades shuttling between Delhi, Europe and the United States, arrived to a city that once sheltered her and then, reluctantly, let her go.

Speaking briefly at the airport, Nasrin told reporters, "I am feeling very good to be back." The words were simple, but the weight of them was not. For a writer who has lived under fatwa, faced book bans and survived an exodus from her homeland, returning to Kolkata is not just a journey—it is a statement.

The Long Road Home

Nasrin's forced departure from Kolkata in 2007 came after Islamist groups, angered by her writings on religion and women's rights, threatened her life. She had been living in the city since 2004, when she left Bangladesh following a fatwa issued by a fundamentalist cleric. Kolkata, with its literary culture and political radicalism, seemed a natural refuge. But the threats followed her, and she was eventually compelled to leave India's eastern metropolis as well.

For 19 years, she has remained a controversial figure—celebrated by secularists and feminists, reviled by religious conservatives. Her novels, including the seminal Lajja, have been translated into dozens of languages, and her voice has never softened, even from afar.

A Politically Charged Return

The homecoming is being viewed as politically symbolic. Nasrin has remained a sharp critic of religious extremism and political authoritarianism, both in Bangladesh and in India. Her return to Kolkata—a city with deep intellectual and political traditions—is seen as an assertion that she will not be silenced by the threats that once drove her away.

Organisers of her visit have planned a public event, though details remain sparse. Security has been tightened, and police are expected to maintain a visible presence. Officials have not yet confirmed the full itinerary, but sources indicate that Nasrin will address a gathering of writers and activists in the coming days.

Mixed Reactions

Reactions in Kolkata have been mixed. Literary circles have welcomed her return, with several prominent authors expressing solidarity. "Her presence is a reminder that the pen outlasts the sword," one local writer said, requesting anonymity. Others, however, have voiced concerns about potential unrest. Religious groups have remained largely silent, but some have hinted at protests.

Nasrin, for her part, seems undeterred. In earlier interviews, she has said that she does not fear death, only silence. Her return to Kolkata—a city that once gave her refuge and then took it away—may be her most defiant act yet.

What To Watch

Over the next few days, all eyes will be on her scheduled public appearances. Whether she will use the platform to comment on the current political climate in India and Bangladesh remains to be seen. What is clear is that Taslima Nasrin is not merely visiting a city—she is reclaiming a chapter of her life that was forcibly closed, and she is doing so on her own terms.
