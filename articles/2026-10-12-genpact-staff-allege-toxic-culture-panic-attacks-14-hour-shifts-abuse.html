What Genpact employees are alleging—and why it changes the risk picture

Employees at Genpact have raised serious workplace allegations, including panic attacks, prolonged shifts reportedly lasting up to 14 hours, and harassment by a manager. Multiple reports cite complaints about bias and abuse, alongside claims that medical or personal emergencies were not taken seriously at work.

In a technology delivery model where large teams handle client systems and deadlines, “pressure” can become a compliance and duty-of-care issue if it crosses into sustained overwork, intimidation, or retaliation. Even when claims are disputed, the existence of such allegations forces a re-check of HR controls, incident response timelines, and workplace escalation channels.

How excessive hours and “ignored emergencies” become workplace compliance failures

Allegations of 14-hour shifts are not just a comfort issue; they can indicate systemic labour risk. In India, employers are expected to follow statutory working time rules and maintain safe working conditions. When employees describe persistent overwork, compounded by stress responses like panic attacks, the situation moves from performance management into health and safety territory.

“Ignored emergencies” claims also matter because they test whether a company treats employee well-being as a core operational requirement. For buyers and risk managers, this is a signal to look beyond delivery metrics and ask how escalation works in real time—especially for stress, mental health, and acute medical concerns.

Company response: reviews and probes—what employees should expect next

Reporting indicates that Genpact ordered a review and that a probe process was initiated following the allegations. For employees, the credibility of such actions depends on practical steps: timely acknowledgement, an independent investigation, and protection against retaliation while complaints are being examined.

For employers, the benchmark is transparency without compromising confidentiality—clear communication on timelines, grievance mechanisms, and whether interim safeguards were implemented (like changing reporting lines, adjusting schedules, or limiting manager access during the inquiry).

  • Pros: Company action to review allegations can reduce prolonged harm if interim safeguards are applied quickly.
  • Pros: Probing improves documentation and can lead to process fixes, not just individual case outcomes.
  • Cons: If investigations are slow or opaque, employees may disengage or self-censor, worsening retention and mental health risk.
  • Cons: “Review” without independent oversight can be perceived as procedural cover, especially when multiple complaint themes recur.

Buyer implications: what outsourcing customers should ask about labour-risk controls

Moneycontrol’s coverage points to an issue inside an IT services organisation. For clients—banks, e-commerce firms, and enterprises buying tech and operations—this becomes a vendor management and contract governance question.

At procurement time, buyers can protect themselves by requiring evidence of HR due diligence: anti-harassment policy effectiveness, average and maximum working hours reporting, grievance redressal SLAs, and audit rights. The key is to tie these to measurable controls rather than statements of intent.

Practical checks include asking whether the vendor tracks overtime compliance, whether incident investigations are handled by independent HR/legal teams, and whether employees can report concerns without going through the same reporting manager. Buyers should also ensure their contracts include remediation commitments and the right to demand corrective action plans.

Benchmarks buyers can use in vendor assessments

Because “toxic culture” is hard to quantify, procurement teams should look for proxies: turnover patterns, employee engagement survey scores, time-to-investigate complaint resolution, and documented training completion rates for managers. In high-pressure delivery roles, monitoring working-hour distribution (not only averages) helps identify chronic overwork pockets.

What “credible remediation” should look like after allegations

When claims involve panic attacks, alleged harassment, and extended shifts, remediation needs to address both immediate harm and long-term drivers. Immediate steps could include workload rebalancing, schedule caps, and removing accused personnel from decision-making roles while investigations proceed.

Long-term steps should include managerial accountability tied to HR outcomes, not only delivery targets. Companies that get serious typically publish internal learnings—training updates, policy clarifications, and changes in how teams plan staffing for peak cycles—without revealing personal identities.

For employees, the most important factor is whether the process leads to a fair outcome and tangible changes in day-to-day management. For buyers, the most important factor is whether vendor governance prevents similar risks from repeating across projects, locations, or client accounts.
