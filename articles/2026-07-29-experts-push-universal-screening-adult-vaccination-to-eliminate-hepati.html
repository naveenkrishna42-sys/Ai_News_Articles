Experts push universal screening, adult vaccination to eliminate hepatitis by 2030

📅 2026-07-29 📂 Health Original source ↗
Key points
  • Over 21 crore people screened, 6 lakh treated under National Viral Hepatitis Control Programme so far.
  • Experts urge universal screening and adult vaccination to meet the 2030 elimination goal.
  • AIIMS doctors stress early detection can prevent most hepatitis deaths.

Targeting elimination in four years

Health experts and policymakers are pushing for universal screening and adult immunisation to eliminate viral hepatitis in India by 2030. The call comes as the country marks World Hepatitis Day, with officials pointing to significant progress under the National Viral Hepatitis Control Programme (NVHCP).

More than 21 crore people have been screened and 6 lakh patients treated under the programme so far, the Health Secretary said. But experts argue that without widespread adult vaccination and routine testing, the 2030 deadline will slip.

Silent epidemic, preventable deaths

Viral hepatitis — particularly hepatitis B and C — often shows no symptoms until the liver is severely damaged. By then, treatment is costlier and outcomes poorer. AIIMS doctors have stressed that early screening can prevent most hepatitis-related deaths, which number in the lakhs every year in India.

“Hepatitis deaths are largely preventable if we catch the infection early,” a senior AIIMS gastroenterologist said. Universal screening, especially among adults aged 30-65, and vaccination for hepatitis B among adults who missed childhood doses, are the two urgent interventions needed.

Adult vaccination gap

India’s childhood immunisation programme covers hepatitis B at birth and in infancy. But millions of adults remain unprotected. Experts say adult hepatitis B vaccination should be integrated into routine primary care, especially for high-risk groups such as healthcare workers, pregnant women, and people with multiple sexual partners.

The government’s NVHCP currently provides free diagnosis and treatment for hepatitis B and C at district hospitals and medical colleges. But awareness remains low, and many patients report only after developing cirrhosis or liver cancer.

Illuminating the message

In a symbolic push for awareness, the Ripon Building in Chennai was illuminated in green on World Hepatitis Day. Landmarks across several states joined the campaign to draw public attention to the disease.

“Green is the colour of hope for a hepatitis-free India,” a health ministry official said. The gesture was part of a broader awareness drive that included free screening camps and public lectures in cities and rural areas.

What the numbers show

Data from the Union health ministry indicates that while screening coverage has expanded rapidly — from just a few lakh in 2018 to over 21 crore today — treatment linkage remains a challenge. Of those diagnosed, only about 6 lakh have been enrolled in care. Many patients, particularly in rural areas, do not return for follow-up.

Experts warn that without boosting treatment adherence and adult vaccination, the progress in screening will not translate into elimination. “Screening without treatment is like a half-built bridge,” one public health researcher noted.

The World Health Organization has set a global target to eliminate viral hepatitis as a public health threat by 2030 — defined as a 90 percent reduction in new infections and a 65 percent reduction in deaths. India accounts for a significant share of the world's hepatitis burden.

What comes next

The health ministry is expected to release updated operational guidelines for NVHCP later this year, which may include a stronger focus on adult vaccination and community-based screening. Whether these measures can close the gap between diagnosis and treatment will determine if India meets the 2030 deadline.
