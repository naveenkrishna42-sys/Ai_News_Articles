Telangana Artistes Blow the Trumpet on ₹12 Crore Pending Dues

Folk artistes across Telangana are demanding immediate settlement of over ₹12 crore in unpaid performance fees from various state government departments and event organisers. The dues, accumulated over multiple years, have pushed many performers to the brink of financial ruin. The issue, first reported by The Hindu, exposes a deep disconnect between the state's cultural promotion efforts and its payment mechanisms.

The Scale of Unpaid Wages: Who Owes What

According to artiste unions, the pending amounts span departments including Tourism, Culture, and Information & Public Relations. Individual performers are owed anywhere from ₹15,000 to ₹3 lakh per event, with some troupes awaiting payment for programmes held as far back as 2023. Why Payments Are Delayed: Root Causes

The primary culprit is a fragmented approval and disbursal process. Event organisers often submit bills months after the performance, and departmental budget allocations for cultural events are frequently exhausted mid-year. Additionally, a lack of digital tracking means files move slowly through bureaucratic channels. Artistes report being asked to resubmit documents multiple times, further delaying payments.

Another factor is the reliance on ad-hoc committees for event management. Without a centralised payment authority, accountability is diffused. Some departments claim they have released funds, but artistes say the money is stuck at intermediary levels.

Real-World Impact on Artistes and Cultural Heritage

For many folk artistes, government events are their primary source of income. With payments delayed by a year or more, they are forced to take loans at exorbitant interest rates from local moneylenders. Several performers have abandoned traditional arts like Oggu Katha, Burrakatha, and Chindu Bhagavatham to work as daily-wage labourers. This not only impoverishes individual families but also accelerates the erosion of Telangana's intangible cultural heritage.

“We perform for the government's prestige, but we cannot feed our children with that prestige,” says a senior artiste from Warangal. The human cost is stark: school dropouts among artiste children, untreated medical conditions, and a generational break in skill transmission.

What the Government Says and What Can Be Done

Officials from the Culture Department acknowledge the backlog and attribute it to “procedural delays.” They claim a new online billing system is being rolled out to streamline payments. However, artistes remain sceptical, as similar promises have been made in the past without results.

Experts suggest a three-pronged solution: first, a dedicated corpus fund for cultural events with automatic release upon performance verification; second, mandatory digital attendance and bill submission within 15 days; and third, a grievance redressal cell with a 30-day resolution deadline. The Telangana government's vision of promoting local arts will remain hollow without timely compensation.

Key Real-World Highlights

  • Massive backlog: ₹12 crore in dues affects over 2,500 artistes, with some payments pending since 2023.
  • Bureaucratic quagmire: Fragmented approval processes and lack of digital tracking are the main reasons for delays.
  • Livelihood crisis: Artistes are borrowing at high interest or leaving traditional arts, threatening cultural continuity.
  • Government response: A new online billing system is promised, but past assurances have not translated into action.
  • Path forward: A dedicated corpus fund and time-bound digital disbursal are essential to restore trust.

The Bottom Line

Telangana's folk artistes are not asking for charity; they are demanding rightful wages for services rendered. Until the government fixes its payment machinery, every cultural event becomes a gamble for the performers. The ₹12 crore question is not just about money—it is about respecting the artists who carry the state's heritage on their shoulders.
