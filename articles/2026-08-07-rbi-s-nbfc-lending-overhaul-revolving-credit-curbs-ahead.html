RBI's NBFC Lending Overhaul: Revolving Credit Curbs Ahead

📅 2026-08-07 📂 Banking Original source ↗
Key points
  • RBI has proposed new rules that would restrict revolving credit facilities for most NBFCs.
  • The move aims to curb potential liquidity risks and over-leveraging in the non-bank lending sector.
  • Industry experts say smaller NBFCs may face higher borrowing costs and tighter funding access.
  • The central bank has invited stakeholder feedback before finalizing the norms.

The Reserve Bank of India (RBI) has proposed a sweeping change in how non-banking financial companies (NBFCs) access short-term credit, a move that could fundamentally reshape the lending landscape for thousands of non-bank lenders across the country. At the heart of the proposal is a clampdown on revolving credit lines — the flexible borrowing arrangements that many NBFCs rely on for day-to-day liquidity.

Under the current framework, NBFCs draw down and repay these credit lines at will, giving them a cushion to manage cash flow mismatches. The RBI’s new draft norms, however, would treat such facilities with far more caution, potentially forcing most NBFCs to replace them with term loans or other, more rigid funding instruments.

Why the RBI is Pushing for Change

The central bank’s concern is not new. Revolving credit, while convenient, can mask underlying liquidity stress. In times of market turbulence, lenders can suddenly withdraw these lines, leaving NBFCs scrambling for funds. The RBI has witnessed this play out in past crises, and it appears determined to build a more resilient system.

By restricting revolving credit, the regulator aims to compel NBFCs to match their assets and liabilities more prudently. The proposal is part of a broader effort to tighten oversight of a sector that has grown rapidly but remains vulnerable to funding shocks.

Impact on NBFCs: Winners and Losers

The fallout will not be uniform. Large, well-capitalised NBFCs—those with strong credit ratings—are likely to adapt quickly, shifting to other funding avenues like commercial paper or longer-term borrowings. But for smaller players, the change could be painful. These firms often depend heavily on revolving lines from banks to manage their daily operations.

“Smaller NBFCs will bear the brunt,” said a banking analyst familiar with the draft, speaking on condition of anonymity. “They will face tighter liquidity buffers, higher costs, and possibly reduced lending capacity.” The proposal could also accelerate consolidation in the sector, as weaker firms struggle to maintain their funding lines.

What the New Rules Entail

The RBI’s draft suggests that revolving credit facilities should be treated as a form of unsecured borrowing, which would attract higher capital requirements. Additionally, NBFCs may be required to maintain a minimum level of liquid assets against such facilities, reducing the flexibility they currently enjoy.

Banks that extend these lines would also face stricter exposure limits. The central bank has floated the idea of capping the amount any single bank can lend to an NBFC via revolving credit, a move that would diversify risk but also fragment funding sources.

Industry bodies have already begun lobbying for a softer implementation, arguing that a blanket ban could hurt credit flow to the real economy. NBFCs are crucial lenders to small businesses and individuals in semi-urban and rural areas, and a sudden squeeze could have ripple effects.

Stakeholder Feedback and the Road Ahead

The RBI has thrown the proposal open for public and industry comment, with a deadline expected within the next few months. The final rules are likely to be calibrated after considering the feedback, but the direction of travel is clear: the era of easy, revolving credit for NBFCs is drawing to a close.

As the consultation progresses, market watchers will be keenly watching how the RBI balances its prudential concerns with the need to keep credit flowing. The next few months are critical—both for NBFCs and for the millions of borrowers who depend on them.
