Danuri captures before-after images of Falcon 9 debris impact on Moon

📅 2026-08-06 📂 Science Original source ↗
Key points
  • Danuri captured before-and-after images of the Moon's surface around the Falcon 9 debris impact site.
  • The impact was caused by a SpaceX rocket stage that crashed into the lunar far side.
  • South Korea's space agency shared the images, highlighting the rare observation opportunity.
  • NASA and other agencies were also involved in observing the event.

Moon orbiter documents rare collision

South Korea's lunar orbiter Danuri has captured before-and-after images of the Moon's surface following the impact of a SpaceX Falcon 9 rocket stage, offering scientists a rare look at how such debris alters the lunar landscape. The images, released by the Korea Aerospace Research Institute (KARI), show a new crater where the rocket body slammed into the far side of the Moon.

The impact occurred as predicted earlier this year, when astronomers calculated that the spent rocket stage would crash into the lunar surface. The Falcon 9 upper stage, roughly the size of a school bus, had been drifting in space for years after completing its mission, and its trajectory finally intersected with the Moon.

Danuri, which has been orbiting the Moon since late 2022, was in a position to observe the impact site before and after the collision. The orbiter's high-resolution cameras captured the region in detail, allowing scientists to compare the two images and identify the fresh crater.

A rare observation opportunity

The event marks one of the few times that scientists have been able to document a human-made object crashing into the Moon. Most previous impacts, such as those from Apollo mission stages, were not observed with modern imaging technology. The Danuri images provide a unique dataset for studying the mechanics of such collisions.

KARI shared the images with international partners, including NASA, which had also planned to observe the impact using its Lunar Reconnaissance Orbiter. The coordination between agencies highlights the growing interest in monitoring lunar activity, especially as more missions head to the Moon.

The impact site is on the lunar far side, which is permanently facing away from Earth. This made direct observation from Earth impossible, but orbiters like Danuri and NASA's LRO were well-positioned to capture the event.

What the images show

The before-and-after images reveal a distinct new crater, with ejecta—the material thrown up by the impact—scattered around the rim. Scientists are now analyzing the images to estimate the size of the crater and the energy of the impact, which could provide insights into the behavior of similar debris in cislunar space.

The Falcon 9 upper stage was part of a 2015 mission that launched the Deep Space Climate Observatory (DSCOVR) for NOAA. After completing its primary task, the stage was left in a high Earth orbit, and its orbit gradually decayed until it intersected with the Moon's path.

While the impact was not a surprise, it underscores the growing issue of space debris. As more rockets are launched, the risk of uncontrolled reentries and impacts increases, and scientists are calling for better tracking and disposal practices.

Implications for future lunar missions

The event also has practical implications for upcoming lunar missions. With multiple countries and private companies planning to land on the Moon, understanding the effects of impacts—both natural and human-made—is crucial for site selection and safety.

KARI's Danuri mission, which was launched in 2022, continues to operate beyond its primary year-long mission, providing valuable data on the lunar environment. The orbiter's success demonstrates South Korea's growing capabilities in space exploration.

Looking ahead, scientists will continue to analyze the Danuri images to refine their models of impact cratering. The data could also help in planning for future missions that might encounter similar debris.

As lunar traffic increases, the need for coordinated observation and regulation will become more pressing. The Danuri images serve as a reminder that the Moon is not just a destination but also a witness to humanity's expanding footprint in space.
