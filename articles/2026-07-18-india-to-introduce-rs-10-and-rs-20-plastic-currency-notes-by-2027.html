India to introduce Rs 10 and Rs 20 plastic currency notes by 2027

📅 2026-07-18 📂 Business Original source ↗
Key points
  • RBI subsidiary Bharatiya Reserve Bank Note Mudran has floated a global tender for polymer banknote substrate.
  • Plastic notes for Rs 10 and Rs 20 are expected to roll out next year, offering longer life and better durability.
  • Polymer notes are harder to counterfeit and stay cleaner than paper currency.

India's currency is set for a material upgrade. The Reserve Bank of India's subsidiary, Bharatiya Reserve Bank Note Mudran (BRBNM), has floated a global tender seeking suppliers of polymer substrate for banknotes. This is the clearest signal yet that the country will introduce plastic currency notes for Rs 10 and Rs 20 denominations, with a rollout expected next year.

The move follows years of deliberation within the central bank. Polymer notes are widely used in countries like Australia, Canada, and the UK. They last two to three times longer than paper notes, resist moisture and dirt, and are significantly harder to counterfeit.

Why polymer notes now

Paper currency in India has a short life. A Rs 10 note lasts about a year, while a Rs 20 note survives roughly 18 months. Frequent replacement costs the exchequer crores annually. Polymer notes, by contrast, can last up to four or five years before needing to be pulled out of circulation.

The RBI has been studying the feasibility of polymer notes for over a decade. A pilot project was conducted in 2017, but the plan stalled due to concerns about costs and the impact on the printing industry. The global tender now suggests that the central bank has resolved these issues and is ready to move forward.

Another factor is hygiene. Polymer notes do not absorb sweat, oil, or grime the way paper notes do. In a post-pandemic world, that matters more than ever. The RBI has also noted that plastic notes can be recycled after their life, reducing environmental waste compared to the cotton-and-linen mix used currently.

What the global tender reveals

The tender issued by BRBNM is for the supply of polymer substrate — the raw material used to print the notes. It explicitly seeks offers from manufacturers and suppliers who can meet the security and durability standards set by the central bank. The move is being seen as a precursor to a full-scale production line at the currency printing presses in Mysuru and Salboni.

The tender document does not specify the exact volume of polymer substrate required, but industry sources estimate it could be enough to print 100 crore notes in the initial phase. The RBI is expected to start with Rs 10 and Rs 20 notes because these denominations change hands most frequently and wear out fastest.

If the trial succeeds, the RBI may expand polymer notes to higher denominations. But for now, the focus remains on low-value notes that suffer the most damage in daily use.

Global experience with polymer notes

Australia was the first country to introduce polymer notes in 1988. Since then, over 40 countries have adopted them. Canada switched fully to polymer in 2013, and the Bank of England introduced a polymer £5 note in 2016. In all cases, the notes have lasted longer and remained cleaner than their paper counterparts.

Counterfeiters find polymer notes harder to replicate because they incorporate complex security features like transparent windows, micro-printing, and colour-shifting inks. India, which has faced periodic issues with fake currency, stands to benefit from this added security layer.

The downside is cost. Polymer notes cost roughly 20 to 30 percent more to print than paper ones. But the longer lifespan offsets this premium, making them cheaper over the life of the note.

What happens next

The RBI will evaluate bids from global suppliers and award contracts within the next few months. Once the substrate arrives, printing is expected to begin at BRBNM's presses. The public can expect to see the first polymer notes in circulation by mid-2027.

The transition will be gradual. Paper notes of the same denominations will continue to be accepted as legal tender. The RBI has not announced any plan to withdraw existing paper notes. For now, the polymer note is an addition to the currency mix, not a replacement.

Watch for the next few months as the RBI publishes the list of shortlisted suppliers. That will reveal which global polymer technology — from companies like Innovia, CCL Secure, or Goznak — India has chosen for its currency future.
