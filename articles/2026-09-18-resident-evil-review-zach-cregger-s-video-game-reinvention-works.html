Resident Evil Review: Zach Cregger's Video-Game Reinvention Works

✍️ By TIVRA News Editorial Team 📅 2026-09-18 📂 Movies Original source ↗
Key points
  • Zach Cregger swaps franchise lore for a lean, level-based survival structure that mirrors playing a video game rather than watching one.
  • Austin Abrams anchors the film with a physically committed lead turn that keeps the audience invested through the carnage.
  • Scare craft is practical and precisely timed, though the third act trades dread for volume and the film's R-rating limits its theatrical reach in India.

A Franchise Reboot That Finally Understands the Game

Resident Evil has spent two decades on screen trying to be a mythology. Zach Cregger's take does something simpler and smarter: it treats the material as a game. The film drops its protagonist into a hostile space, hands him limited tools and lets the audience feel every locked door, every dead-end corridor and every resource counted twice. That structural choice, more than any monster design, is what separates this entry from the six Paul W.S. Anderson-era films and the 2021 reboot.

For buyers deciding between a theatrical ticket and a streaming wait, the distinction matters. This is a film built on spatial tension and sound design, the kind of experience that loses roughly half its impact on a phone screen.

What Cregger Changes, and Why It Works

Level-Based Storytelling

Cregger structures the runtime like sequential stages. Each location introduces a new rule, a new threat and a new scarcity problem. Characters solve problems with the logic of a player, not the logic of a soldier, which makes the survival beats feel earned rather than choreographed.

Austin Abrams as the Anchor

Austin Abrams carries the film with a performance that is physical first and emotional second. He sells exhaustion, panic and grim improvisation without tipping into melodrama. In a genre where leads often become invincible by the second act, his vulnerability is the film's most reliable tension generator.

Practical Scare Craft

The set pieces favour practical staging over digital spectacle. Cregger understands that a scare lands when the audience has already mapped the room, so the camera lingers on exits, shadows and objects before they become relevant. Several sequences are genuinely well-executed, and at least two will be discussed spoiler-free in the weeks after release.

Real-World Buyer Implications

Indian audiences should note the certification. The film's horror violence and gore will likely attract an A rating, which restricts family and younger viewers and compresses the potential box office in a market where U/A titles routinely outperform. Expect a shorter theatrical window and a faster digital arrival than a typical franchise tentpole.

Pricing context: a weekend multiplex ticket in metros runs roughly Rs 250 to Rs 450 for standard screens and Rs 600 to Rs 1,100 for premium formats. Given the film's reliance on sound and darkness, IMAX or a large-format screen justifies the premium, while a standard recliner seat at a lesser screen does not.

Key Insights

  • Strengths: Lean structure, committed lead performance, practical scares, no franchise baggage for newcomers.
  • Weaknesses: The third act leans on volume over dread, and supporting characters remain thinly drawn.
  • Best for: Viewers who want a tight, game-logic survival thriller rather than lore-heavy franchise homework.
  • Skip if: You dislike gore, or you prefer horror built on slow-burn psychological dread.
  • Value call: Worth a theatrical ticket for the audio design alone, but not worth a premium-format upcharge if you are seated far from the screen.

Verdict

Resident Evil works because Cregger resists the temptation to explain everything. He builds a machine, sets it running and lets Abrams navigate it. The result is a reboot that feels like a first instalment rather than a franchise obligation, and one that earns a recommendation for genre fans who value craft over canon.
