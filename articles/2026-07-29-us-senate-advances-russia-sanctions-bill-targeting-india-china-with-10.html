US Senate Advances Russia Sanctions Bill Targeting India, China With 100% Tariffs

📅 2026-07-29 📂 Top Stories Original source ↗
Key points
  • The US Senate has advanced a Russia sanctions bill that calls for 100% tariffs on India and China.
  • A US senator has described India and China as the 'main culprits' and key targets of the proposed legislation.
  • The bill is being fast-tracked in the Senate as a tribute to the late Senator Lindsey Graham, a key architect.

The United States Senate has advanced a sweeping Russia sanctions bill that explicitly targets India and China, threatening to impose 100% tariffs on goods imported from both nations. The legislation, which is being fast-tracked through the upper chamber, represents the most aggressive step yet by Washington to penalize countries that continue to purchase Russian oil and gas.

The bill's advancement comes amid a deepening confrontation between the US and major Asian economies over their energy ties with Moscow. A US senator, speaking during the debate, singled out India and China as the "main culprits" and the primary targets of the proposed sanctions regime.

What the Bill Proposes

The legislation, formally titled the "Defending American Security and Prosperity Act," authorises the imposition of tariffs of up to 100% on all goods exported from India and China. The measure is designed to punish both nations for their refusal to join Western-led sanctions against Russia after its invasion of Ukraine.

For India, which has emerged as one of the largest buyers of discounted Russian crude oil since the war began, the bill could have severe economic consequences. Indian refiners have processed record volumes of Russian oil, much of it transported through a shadow fleet of tankers that the US and its allies have sought to disrupt.

A Tribute to Senator Graham

The bill is being advanced as a tribute to the late Senator Lindsey Graham, a Republican from South Carolina who was a key architect of the sanctions framework. Graham, who died earlier this year, was known for his hawkish stance on Russia and his insistence that countries like India and China should face consequences for their energy trade with Moscow.

The Senate leadership has fast-tracked the bill in honour of Graham's legacy, bypassing the usual committee process to bring it directly to the floor for a vote. The move has drawn sharp criticism from some lawmakers who argue the legislation is too broad and could damage relations with two of America's most important trading partners.

India's Vulnerability

For India, the threat of 100% tariffs is a direct challenge to its carefully calibrated foreign policy. New Delhi has maintained that its energy purchases from Russia are driven by national security and economic necessity, pointing to the need to secure affordable fuel for its 1.4 billion citizens.

India's exports to the US were valued at over $85 billion in the last financial year, spanning pharmaceuticals, engineering goods, textiles, and IT services. A 100% tariff would effectively shut Indian products out of the American market, potentially triggering a trade war that neither side can afford.

The Indian government has not yet issued an official response to the bill's advancement. However, diplomatic sources indicate that New Delhi is closely monitoring the situation and is prepared to engage with Washington to find a resolution. Indian officials have previously argued that singling out India for its Russian oil imports ignores the broader global energy market dynamics.

China in the Crosshairs

China faces an even more complex calculus. Beijing has emerged as Russia's most important economic partner since the war, with bilateral trade reaching new highs. The Chinese government has repeatedly condemned unilateral sanctions and has signalled that it will not bow to American pressure.

Analysts warn that the bill could trigger retaliatory measures from Beijing, potentially escalating into a full-blown trade war between the world's two largest economies. China's exports to the US exceeded $500 billion last year, making it the largest source of American imports.

The bill's passage through the Senate is not guaranteed. It faces opposition from some moderate Republicans and Democrats who fear its economic repercussions. However, the momentum behind the legislation, fuelled by the emotional tribute to Graham, makes its eventual passage increasingly likely.

What happens next depends on the final vote in the Senate and, if passed, the bill's journey through the House of Representatives. Indian and Chinese diplomats are expected to intensify their lobbying efforts in Washington to soften the bill's provisions or carve out exemptions. The coming weeks will determine whether this legislative threat becomes a reality.
