Susanne Bier Makes the Case for the Big Screen

Danish director Susanne Bier, who helms the long-gestating sequel to the 1998 cult favourite Practical Magic, has spoken plainly about why the film is being built for cinemas rather than a streaming-first debut. In an exclusive conversation, she framed the choice as creative rather than contractual: watching a movie alongside strangers, she said, is an entirely different experience from watching it alone at home.

That position matters commercially. The original film, starring Sandra Bullock and Nicole Kidman as witch sisters navigating love and family curses, underperformed on release but became a durable home-video and cable favourite. A sequel therefore arrives with a built-in nostalgia audience—and a question every distributor must answer: does nostalgia convert into ticket sales?

Why the Theatrical Window Is the Real Story

Bier's comments place her alongside a growing group of filmmakers pushing back against day-and-date releases. For a film with a female-skewing, adult audience, the theatrical window is not just an artistic preference; it is a pricing decision. Premium large-format screenings and recliner multiplex seats carry higher average ticket prices than standard shows, and that differential is where studios recover marketing spend.

For exhibitors in India and the Gulf, the sequel is attractive because it arrives with recognisable stars and a light fantasy-romance tone that travels well across languages. Dubbed and subtitled versions in Hindi, Tamil, and Telugu would widen the addressable audience beyond the English-speaking metro circuit.

What Buyers and Exhibitors Should Watch

The commercial risk is not the film's quality but its release date. Adult-skewing titles live or die by screen allocation in weeks two and three. If a superhero tentpole opens adjacent, multiplexes will move Practical Magic 2 to smaller auditoriums quickly, compressing its earning curve.

Distributors will also weigh the sequel against the original's modest theatrical gross, adjusted for inflation. That history argues for a disciplined print-and-advertising budget rather than a saturation campaign—targeted digital spends aimed at book clubs, fantasy romance readers, and the original's streaming-era rediscovery audience.

Real-World Highlights

  • Director-led marketing: Bier's theatrical advocacy gives the campaign a clear, quotable hook beyond nostalgia.
  • Format upside: Premium screens and dubbed versions can lift average ticket price meaningfully in India and the Gulf.
  • Window discipline: A theatrical-first stance protects exhibitor relationships and preserves downstream licensing value.
  • Nostalgia ceiling: The 1998 film was never a blockbuster, so expectations must stay grounded.
  • Date sensitivity: A crowded release corridor could cap screen count and shorten the film's theatrical tail.

Value Justification for the Ticket Buyer

For audiences, the proposition is straightforward. A witchy romantic drama with two marquee names, staged by an Oscar-winning director, offers something increasingly rare in the calendar: a mid-budget adult film designed to be watched in a room full of people. At Indian multiplex prices—roughly ₹250 to ₹600 for a standard recliner show, and ₹800-plus for premium formats—the value case rests on the communal reaction, not spectacle.

Whether that case holds will be settled by opening-weekend occupancy. Bier has made her argument. The box office will make the counter-argument.
