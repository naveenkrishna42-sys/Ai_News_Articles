Houthis and Yemen's West Coast: Can They Hold the Red Sea Chokepoint?

Key points
  • The Houthis hold most of Yemen's Red Sea coastline, including the Bab al-Mandab strait, which channels roughly 12 percent of global trade through the Suez route.
  • Yemeni government forces have counterattacked west of Taiz, signalling that the front line is shifting after recent Red Sea setbacks.
  • Brics members are split on the Houthi threat, with Qatar and other Gulf states warning against any forced closure of Bab al-Mandab.

Why Yemen's Western Coast Matters Beyond Yemen

Yemen's western coastline is the single most consequential stretch of real estate in the Red Sea corridor. It hosts the Bab al-Mandab strait, the 26-kilometre-wide gateway between the Gulf of Aden and the Red Sea, through which a substantial share of Europe-Asia container, energy and bulk traffic passes. Whoever controls the shoreline controls the risk premium attached to that traffic.

The Houthis seized most of this coast, including the port city of Hodeidah and the strait's eastern approaches, during the civil war. Holding it is a different proposition from taking it. Terrain, tribal politics, supply lines and external backers all determine whether that control survives the coming year.

The Military Picture: Gains, Counterattacks and Attrition

Yemeni government forces have counterattacked Houthi positions west of Taiz following a run of Red Sea setbacks. Taiz sits inland from the coast and its western districts are the hinge between the highlands and the maritime plain. Pressure here threatens Houthi interior lines rather than the ports directly, but it forces the group to fight on multiple axes.

The Houthis retain the advantage of depth. Their core territory in the northern highlands is difficult to invade, and decades of conflict have hardened their units. The coast, however, is flat, exposed and dependent on sea access for fuel and revenue.

Key Real-World Highlights

  • Bab al-Mandab carries a large share of Suez-route trade; closure or harassment reshapes freight rates within days.
  • Hodeidah port remains the Houthis' main revenue and import lifeline.
  • Counterattacks west of Taiz show the anti-Houthi coalition can still mount local offensives.
  • Brics divisions limit any unified diplomatic pressure on the group.
  • Insurance underwriters price Bab al-Mandab risk separately from broader Yemen risk.

Brics Divisions and the Qatar Warning

The Conversation's reporting on Brics divisions captures a structural problem. The bloc includes states with Red Sea interests that are directly threatened and states that view the Houthis as a useful lever against Western shipping. That split prevents a common position.

Qatar's warning against closing Bab al-Mandab reflects Gulf anxiety about escalation. A full closure would hurt Gulf exporters first. Doha's message is that the strait must remain open even amid political sympathy for Palestinian causes in the region.

Can the Houthis Hold On? Buyer and Analyst Implications

Holding the coast depends on three variables. First, whether the anti-Houthi forces can convert local counterattacks into sustained coastal pressure. Second, whether the Houthis can keep Hodeidah functioning under interdiction. Third, whether external patrons continue supplying the group.

For shipping lines, insurers and energy buyers, the working assumption should be prolonged volatility rather than outright closure. That means war-risk premiums on Bab al-Mandab transits stay elevated, and longer Cape of Good Hope routings remain a live cost line.

Pros and Cons of the Current Stalemate

  • Pro: The strait stays open, avoiding a global freight shock.
  • Pro: Houthi revenue dependency on Hodeidah gives mediators leverage.
  • Con: Persistent harassment keeps insurance and crew costs high.
  • Con: Divided Brics diplomacy leaves no credible enforcement mechanism.
  • Con: A sudden Taiz front collapse could trigger either Houthi consolidation or chaotic fragmentation.

The honest answer to the headline question is qualified. The Houthis can hold the western coast for now, but their grip is financial and maritime more than territorial. The moment Hodeidah's revenue or sea access is seriously disrupted, the coast becomes a liability rather than an asset.
