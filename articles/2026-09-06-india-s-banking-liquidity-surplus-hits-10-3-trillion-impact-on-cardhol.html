India’s Banking Liquidity Surplus Hits ₹10.3 Trillion – Impact on Cardholders

Record Surplus and Its Drivers

In the second quarter of 2026, the Reserve Bank of India disclosed a liquidity surplus of ₹10.3 trillion, the highest since the 2008 financial crisis. The excess stems from aggressive repo‑rate cuts, robust foreign‑exchange inflows, and a slowdown in loan disbursement as consumers defer big‑ticket purchases.

For credit‑card issuers, the surplus translates into cheaper wholesale funding. Lower cost of funds allows banks to revisit the pricing of revolving credit, a key component of card profitability.

Credit‑Card Interest Rate Landscape

Benchmark Shifts

Historically, banks have priced card APRs at a spread of 6‑8 percentage points over the repo rate. With the repo at 6.5 %, the surplus has nudged the effective spread toward the lower end of the band.

Early adopters such as HDFC and SBI have already announced APR reductions of 0.5‑0.8 percentage points on select premium cards. The move is expected to improve affordability for high‑utilisation users who carry balances month‑to‑month.

Real‑World Cost Impact

A typical ₹50,000 outstanding balance at 19 % APR costs roughly ₹7,950 annually. A 0.6 % rate cut saves about ₹300 per year, a modest but tangible benefit for the average cardholder.

Cashback and Reward Strategies

Funding Richer Offers

With funding costs easing, banks can allocate more budget to cashback and reward points without eroding margins. Recent announcements include 5 % cashback on grocery spends for select cards, a jump from the usual 2‑3 %.

However, the benefit is uneven. Premium cards with higher annual fees are positioned to absorb the extra spend, while mass‑market cards may retain conservative cashback rates to protect profitability.

Risk‑Adjusted Pricing

Even as banks expand rewards, they tighten eligibility based on credit scores and utilisation ratios. Consumers with sub‑prime scores may see stricter limits or higher fees, balancing the upside of richer rewards.

Future Outlook and Potential Risks

The surplus is not guaranteed. A resurgence in loan demand or a tightening of RBI policy could reverse the trend, pushing APRs upward and compressing cashback margins.

Stakeholders should monitor three indicators: repo‑rate movements, corporate bond issuance volumes, and the RBI’s liquidity absorption tools.

Key Real‑World Highlights

  • Liquidity surplus of ₹10.3 trillion provides banks with cheaper funding.
  • APR reductions of up to 0.8 % are already visible on premium cards.
  • Cashback offers may rise to 5 % on select categories, but eligibility tightening is likely.
