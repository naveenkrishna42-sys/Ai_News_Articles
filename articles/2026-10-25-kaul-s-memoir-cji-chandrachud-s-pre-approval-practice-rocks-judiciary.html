Explosive Memoir Alleges Pre-Approval Practice by Former CJI Chandrachud

A recent memoir by former Supreme Court Justice Kaul has ignited a significant debate within India's legal and political circles. The book makes a startling claim: during his tenure as Chief Justice of India, D.Y. Chandrachud allegedly sought the government's pre-approval before formal Collegium discussions on judicial appointments commenced. This revelation, first reported by Live Law and elaborated upon by The Indian Express, points to a potentially unprecedented level of executive-judiciary interaction that raises critical questions about judicial independence and the established appointment process.

The Collegium System and Alleged Deviations

India's Collegium system, comprising the CJI and four senior-most Supreme Court judges, holds the primary responsibility for appointing and transferring judges to the higher judiciary. The Memorandum of Procedure (MoP) outlines a structured process, where the Collegium initiates recommendations, which are then forwarded to the government for vetting and final approval. Justice Kaul's account suggests a departure from this established norm, alleging that preliminary consultations with the government occurred before names were formally discussed within the Collegium itself.

This pre-discussion vetting, if true, could fundamentally alter the dynamic of judicial appointments. It implies an executive influence at an initial, formative stage, potentially shaping the pool of candidates even before the Collegium applies its collective mind. Such a practice contrasts sharply with the intended autonomy of the judiciary in identifying and selecting its own members.

Judicial Independence and the Separation of Powers

The core of Justice Kaul's critique, and indeed the broader concern, revolves around the principle of judicial independence. The former judge reportedly characterized Chandrachud as "too deferential" and suggested that "Govt respects judges with spine." This language underscores a perceived erosion of the judiciary's distinct role as an independent pillar of democracy. The doctrine of separation of powers mandates clear boundaries between the executive, legislature, and judiciary.

While the government has a role in the final stages of appointment, including intelligence checks and administrative processing, direct pre-Collegium consultation could be seen as blurring these lines. Critics argue it risks politicizing judicial selections, compromising the impartiality and integrity of the process. The judiciary's ability to act as a check on executive power relies heavily on its perceived and actual independence from governmental influence.
