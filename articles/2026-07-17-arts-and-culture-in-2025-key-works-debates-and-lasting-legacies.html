Arts and Culture in 2025: Key Works, Debates, and Lasting Legacies

The year 2025 will be remembered as a period of intense creative ferment and sharp cultural reckoning in India. From galleries in Mumbai to film festivals in Kerala, artists and audiences grappled with questions of identity, ownership, and the very definition of art in a rapidly changing world.

While the year produced a remarkable body of work, it also laid bare the fault lines within the country's cultural landscape. The conversations that erupted in 2025 are likely to echo for years to come.

Landmark Exhibitions and Performances

The year opened with a bang at the Kochi-Muziris Biennale, which drew record crowds and critical acclaim for its focus on ecological themes and indigenous knowledge systems. Curators wove together works from established names and emerging artists from the Northeast, challenging the mainstream art world's geographic biases.

In Delhi, a retrospective of a pioneering modern artist drew lakhs of visitors, many of them young, sparking a renewed interest in mid-20th century abstraction. Meanwhile, the National School of Drama's festival in New Delhi featured a powerful adaptation of a classic Indian epic, told entirely through the lens of its marginalised characters. The production sold out every night.

Debates on Censorship and Representation

No cultural conversation in 2025 was free from the shadow of censorship. A controversial film, based on a recent political event, was denied a certificate by the Central Board of Film Certification, leading to a fierce public debate. Filmmakers and artists argued that the decision was a blow to creative freedom, while supporters of the board cited the need for maintaining public order.

The question of representation also took centre stage. A major OTT series faced backlash for its casting choices, with critics accusing it of perpetuating stereotypes about a particular community. The show's creators defended their work, but the incident triggered a wider discussion about who gets to tell whose stories, and how.

Digital Art and the AI Question

2025 was the year artificial intelligence firmly entered the Indian art market. An AI-generated artwork sold for a staggering sum at a Mumbai auction, raising eyebrows and questions. Traditional artists expressed concern about originality and the devaluation of human skill, while tech enthusiasts hailed the development as the next frontier of creativity.

Several galleries hosted exhibitions that prominently featured AI as a collaborator, not just a tool. The results were mixed, but the conversation was unavoidable. Legal experts began to seriously examine copyright laws, which remain woefully unprepared for a world where a machine can be the primary creator.

Celebrating Legacies and Mourning Losses

The year also saw the passing of several towering figures in Indian arts. A legendary Kathak dancer, whose career spanned seven decades, died in the summer. Her passing prompted tributes from across the political spectrum, and a state funeral was announced. Her archive, containing rare recordings and photographs, was donated to a national museum.

Similarly, a celebrated novelist, known for her unflinching portrayals of rural life, passed away in the winter. Her books, translated into dozens of languages, had shaped the global perception of Indian literature for generations. Young writers cited her as a primary influence, ensuring her legacy will live on in the next wave of Indian storytelling.

The year 2025 did not resolve the tensions between tradition and technology, or between freedom and regulation. Those battles will continue into 2026, as artists find new ways to push boundaries and audiences decide what art they want to support.
