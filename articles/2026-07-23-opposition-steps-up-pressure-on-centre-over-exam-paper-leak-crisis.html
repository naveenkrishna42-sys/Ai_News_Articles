Opposition Steps Up Pressure on Centre Over Exam Paper Leak Crisis

📅 2026-07-23 📂 Analysis Original source ↗
Key points
  • Rahul Gandhi claims 152 paper leaks in a decade affected 7.5 crore students with zero convictions.
  • Opposition demands PM apology and Education Minister's resignation amid protests by students.
  • BJP's JP Nadda accuses Congress of selective outrage as political battle intensifies.

Context: A Decade of Paper Leaks and Unanswered Questions

Opposition parties in India have escalated their attack on the central government over the continuing crisis surrounding leaked examination papers. Congress leader Rahul Gandhi on Wednesday cited data claiming that 152 paper leaks have occurred in the last ten years, affecting an estimated 7.5 crore students, with not a single conviction secured in any of these cases.

The remarks came as student protests spread across several states, demanding accountability and systemic reforms in the examination process. The opposition has zeroed in on the issue, framing it as a failure of governance that has compromised the future of millions of young Indians.

Gandhi, addressing reporters, said the Prime Minister must apologise to the affected students and called for the resignation of the Education Minister. The Congress party has sought to link the recurring leaks to a broader pattern of administrative negligence.

Background: The Current Wave of Protests

The latest round of protests was triggered by a series of paper leaks in high-stakes competitive examinations, including medical and engineering entrance tests. Students in several cities have taken to the streets, alleging that the integrity of the examination system has been compromised repeatedly without any meaningful action against those responsible.

The opposition's campaign has gained traction as the government struggles to contain public anger. The Congress has been particularly vocal, with party workers joining student demonstrations in multiple states. The issue has also become a flashpoint in Parliament, where opposition MPs have disrupted proceedings to demand a discussion.

Rahul Gandhi's assertion of 152 leaks over ten years — if accurate — points to a systemic vulnerability that successive governments have failed to address. The zero-conviction record cited by the Congress leader raises questions about the investigation and prosecution mechanisms in place.

Why It Matters: Trust in the Examination System at Stake

India's competitive examination system is the gateway to higher education and government jobs for millions of students from modest backgrounds. Any erosion of trust in this system has profound consequences — not just for individual careers but for social mobility and the perception of meritocracy itself.

For the ruling Bharatiya Janata Party (BJP), the controversy comes at a politically sensitive time. The opposition's strategy is to paint the government as indifferent to the struggles of ordinary students, a charge that could resonate with a large and young electorate. The demand for the Education Minister's resignation is a direct challenge to the government's handling of the crisis.

The BJP, however, has pushed back sharply. Party president JP Nadda accused the Congress of selective outrage, pointing to paper leaks that occurred during the previous UPA government. The counter-attack seeks to neutralise the issue by framing it as a bipartisan failure rather than a failure of the current administration alone.

What to Watch: Political and Legal Fallout

The coming days will likely see intensified political sparring. The Congress has indicated it will continue to press the issue both on the streets and in Parliament. Rahul Gandhi's strategy appears to be one of sustained pressure, using data and direct demands to keep the government on the defensive.

On the legal and administrative front, the government may announce new measures to prevent future leaks, such as digital surveillance of examination centres or stricter penalties for offenders. However, without convictions and visible accountability, such measures may fail to restore public confidence.

The key question remains whether the opposition can translate public anger into electoral gain, or whether the ruling party's counter-narrative of shared blame will dilute the impact. Either way, the examination system itself is under a microscope, and the response of both the government and the judiciary will determine whether this crisis deepens or recedes.
