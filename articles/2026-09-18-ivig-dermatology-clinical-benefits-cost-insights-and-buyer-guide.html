Introduction

Intravenous immunoglobulin (IVIG) has moved beyond its traditional immunomodulatory role to become a therapeutic option for several dermatologic conditions. Clinicians now consider IVIG when conventional immunosuppressants fail or pose safety concerns. This article examines the evidence, mechanisms and practical buying considerations relevant to Indian healthcare institutions.

Mechanism of Action in Skin Diseases

IVIG exerts multiple immunomodulatory effects, including Fc‑receptor blockade, neutralization of pathogenic autoantibodies and modulation of cytokine networks. In autoimmune blistering disorders such as pemphigus vulgaris, IVIG reduces autoantibody titers and inhibits complement activation. Inflammatory skin diseases like chronic urticaria benefit from IVIG’s ability to down‑regulate mast cell degranulation and shift Th1/Th2 balance.

Clinical Evidence and Dermatologic Indications

Randomized trials and observational series support IVIG use in refractory pemphigus, bullous pemphigoid, dermatomyositis and severe atopic dermatitis. Typical regimens administer 2 g/kg body weight divided over two to five days, with clinical improvement often noted within two to four weeks. Maintenance dosing, when required, ranges from 0.4 g/kg every three to four weeks.

Common Indications

The most frequently cited dermatologic indications in Indian practice include:

  • Refractory pemphigus vulgaris and foliaceus
  • Bullous pemphigoid unresponsive to corticosteroids
  • Severe dermatomyositis with cutaneous involvement
  • Chronic spontaneous urticaria resistant to antihistamines and omalizumab

Practical Considerations for Buyers

Procurement decisions hinge on product consistency, supply chain reliability and total cost of ownership. Hospitals should verify IgG concentration, stabilizer composition and expiration dating before tender submission. Batch‑to‑batch variability can affect clinical response, making vendor qualification essential.

Key Benefits and Limitations

Understanding the trade‑offs helps clinicians and administrators set realistic expectations and allocate resources effectively.

  • Proven efficacy in antibody‑mediated skin diseases where first‑line agents fail.
  • Steroid‑sparing effect reduces long‑term metabolic complications.
  • Rapid onset of action compared with traditional immunosuppressants.
  • High acquisition cost limits widespread use without formulary controls.
  • Requirement for intravenous administration increases nursing workload.
  • Risk of thromboembolic events, especially in patients with vascular risk factors.
