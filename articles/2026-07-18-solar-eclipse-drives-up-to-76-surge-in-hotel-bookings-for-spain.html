Solar eclipse drives up to 76% surge in hotel bookings for Spain

📅 2026-07-18 📂 Science Original source ↗
Key points
  • SiteMinder data shows hotel bookings in Spain surged up to 76% ahead of the August 12 total solar eclipse.
  • The eclipse path of totality crosses northern and central Spain, driving demand in cities like Madrid, Zaragoza, and Burgos.
  • Travellers are booking rooms with clear sky views, and local businesses are preparing special eclipse-viewing packages.

A total solar eclipse on August 12 is turning into a major tourism driver for Spain. Hotel booking platform SiteMinder reports that reservations in some parts of the country have jumped by as much as 76%, as travellers rush to secure spots along the path of totality.

The celestial event will see the moon completely block the sun, casting a shadow across northern and central Spain. Cities like Madrid, Zaragoza, and Burgos are expected to fall directly under this band, making them prime viewing locations.

The data behind the boom

SiteMinder's analysis looked at booking patterns over the past several weeks. The sharpest increases, around 76%, were recorded in regions along the eclipse's central track. Hotels in these areas report that rooms offering unobstructed western views are selling out first.

Other parts of the country are also seeing a lift. Even cities outside the direct path of totality are experiencing a 20-30% uptick in reservations, as travellers plan day trips or extended stays to witness the phenomenon. The eclipse is falling in peak summer season, which is already a busy period for Spanish tourism.

Why this eclipse matters

While solar eclipses are not rare on a global scale, a total eclipse crossing a major European tourist destination is less common. Spain's last total solar eclipse visible from its mainland occurred in 1905. The next one visible from the country won't happen until 2026 over the Arctic region, and then not again until 2081.

This rarity is driving a surge in so-called 'eclipse tourism'. Visitors are not just booking hotels; they are also renting cars, buying specialised viewing glasses, and planning multi-night stays to maximise their chances of clear skies. Local governments in affected areas are setting up viewing platforms and adjusting traffic plans to manage the expected crowds.

Businesses seize the moment

Hotels are rolling out special packages. Some are offering rooftop viewing parties with telescopes and eclipse-themed cocktails. Others are partnering with astronomy clubs to provide guided commentary during the event. The University of Valencia, for instance, has teamed up with ferry operator Trasmed to organise a floating observatory in the Mediterranean, giving passengers a unique vantage point away from city lights.

Retailers are also cashing in. Supplies of ISO-certified eclipse glasses are reportedly running low in some shops, and online orders have spiked. Restaurants in eclipse-zone cities are planning special menus timed around the few minutes of totality.

Weather remains the biggest unknown. August in Spain typically means clear skies, but local microclimates could still cause disappointment. Meteorologists note that coastal areas may face morning fog, while inland regions offer more reliable visibility. Many travellers are hedging their bets by booking refundable rooms and keeping an eye on short-term forecasts.

The August 12 eclipse promises to be a defining moment for Spain's summer season. Hoteliers expect the booking surge to continue until the last minute. For those still looking for a spot, the advice is simple: check the forecast, pick a clear location, and book soon. The next chance won't come around for decades.
