What an IIT alumnus’s jobless stretch signals about the US job market

NDTV’s report about a 61-year-old IIT Kanpur alumnus being jobless in the US for two years highlights a reality many professionals don’t plan for: seniority doesn’t always guarantee traction in hiring. The individual’s struggle—described as an inability to find work despite around 20 years of experience—fits a broader pattern where older job seekers can face more friction, especially when roles narrow to “immediate impact” hires.

In the US, employers often optimize for short hiring cycles and predictability. When candidates have a long unemployment gap, require sponsorship, or don’t match specific tooling, the process can stall—even when the resume looks strong on paper. For expats, the challenge compounds: rent obligations continue regardless of interview outcomes, while local references and recruitment channels may weaken over time.

Why 60+ job searches can stall despite strong credentials

Experience typically helps with interviews, but job-market headwinds show up earlier than people expect. At 60+, candidates may be filtered out via automated screening, recruiter assumptions about compensation expectations, or perceived reduced “time-to-ROI.” Even when age discrimination isn’t explicitly stated, hiring managers may express it indirectly through preferences for “current” experience or “recent project ownership.”

There’s also a practical issue: US job listings often specify exact tool stacks, industry sub-domains, and reporting lines. If a candidate’s last role is outside the latest demand curve—or if they were away from the exact systems being recruited for—interview conversion can drop. This doesn’t mean the skills are missing; it means the market is searching for very specific evidence.

Network effects matter too. Job seekers who remain active in the market usually accumulate referral momentum. When employment doesn’t materialize, many expats inadvertently reduce outreach frequency, which then reduces inbound opportunities. Over two years, that feedback loop can become painful—especially with ongoing rent and health-cost uncertainty.

Buyer-guide style implications: the “cost of delay” is the real risk

For Indian professionals living abroad, the most useful takeaway is not just the human story—it’s the decision framework. Like any commercial procurement, you’re managing risk, timeline, and budget constraints. In a prolonged job search, the “total cost” isn’t only missed salary; it includes savings burn, mental bandwidth, and the opportunity cost of not repositioning for new roles.

Consider a simplified planning benchmark. If monthly expenses include rent, insurance, utilities, and basic living costs, then every additional month of unemployment increases pressure to accept suboptimal offers—or to pivot to options that may be available faster (contracting, advisory roles, smaller firms). The point of return planning is to avoid last-minute, high-stress decisions when leverage is low.

Returning to India: what changes, what stays, and what to plan for

The report indicates the alum is “considering returning to India.” That choice can reduce cost pressure quickly, but it requires preparation so the move improves job outcomes rather than just pauses the crisis. India’s hiring market can be faster for certain domains, yet competition is intense and resumes may be judged differently when there’s a long employment gap.

A return-to-India plan should include: identifying roles that value seniority while also requiring current domain execution, translating US experience into India’s job taxonomy, and targeting companies where governance/architecture leadership is visible. For many senior professionals, the fastest path can be advisory, program leadership, enterprise consulting, or roles that explicitly need mentorship and stakeholder management—not only hands-on execution.

Pros and cons of a return decision

  • Pros: Lower living-cost pressure; proximity to family support; stronger access to Indian professional networks; more hiring familiarity for local companies.
  • Pros: Easier reactivation of referrals, meetups, and industry groups that improve interview flow.
  • Cons: Employer screening may still penalize the unemployment gap unless explained through a credible repositioning narrative (upskilling, consulting, advisory, caregiving, etc.).
  • Cons: Role expectations can shift toward India’s reporting structures and stack preferences; candidates may need targeted refresh.
  • Cons: If relocation is rushed, compensation may drop relative to last US expectations, especially for premium roles.

The broader lesson for expats: treat job search like a portfolio, not a gamble

This case underscores that job search outcomes are influenced by market timing, screening mechanisms, age-related assumptions, and network dynamics—not just resume strength. For experienced professionals, the most resilient approach is portfolio thinking: maintain multiple application tracks, measure conversions weekly, and be ready to pivot before savings run out.

When NDTV and other outlets surface stories like this, they’re not only reporting hardship; they’re documenting a pattern that can guide better planning. If you’re an expat professional in the later career stage—or any stage—build a runway plan, keep evidence of current capability, and maintain referral momentum so your options don’t collapse during a long, uncertain search.
