Employer Life Cover Explained: Group Term, Riders and Keyman Insurance

Employer-funded life insurance rarely gets the attention it deserves. Most salaried Indians treat group term cover as a line item buried in the offer letter, then forget it exists. That is a costly oversight. Group cover, add-on riders and keyman insurance serve three very different purposes, and understanding which one protects whom decides whether your family is actually covered.

What Group Term Life Cover Actually Delivers

Group term insurance is a single master policy an employer buys for its entire workforce. Underwriters price it on pooled mortality rather than individual health, so premiums land at roughly ₹1,500 to ₹4,000 per employee per year for a ₹25-50 lakh cover. A 35-year-old buying the same protection individually would pay several times that amount.

The trade-off is portability and quantum. Cover typically stops the day you resign, and the sum assured is usually 2-3 times annual CTC, capped by the insurer. Employees with home loans or dependent parents should treat group cover as a floor, not a ceiling.

Riders: Where the Real Gaps Get Filled

Riders are add-ons attached to the base policy for an extra premium, often borne by the employer. The commercially useful ones are accidental death and dismemberment benefit, critical illness cover, and waiver of premium on disability. A critical illness rider pays a lump sum on diagnosis of listed conditions such as cancer or stroke, independent of hospital bills.

Buyers should check the rider schedule in the master policy document, not the HR brochure. Two details matter: whether the rider pays in addition to the base sum assured, and whether it survives a job change.

Keyman Insurance: Protection for the Business, Not the Family

Keyman insurance is a policy a company takes on a critical employee, usually a founder, promoter or revenue-driving executive. The company pays the premium, claims the payout, and books the premium as a business expense under Section 37(1) of the Income Tax Act. The tax treatment of the maturity or death proceeds has been a recurring point of litigation, so documentation of the employee's role and the business rationale is essential.

The employee's family receives nothing from a keyman policy unless a separate agreement exists. That distinction trips up many promoters who assume the cover doubles as personal protection.

Real-World Highlights

  • Group cover is the lowest-cost life insurance most salaried individuals will ever hold.
  • Riders close specific gaps but never replace adequate base cover.
  • Keyman payouts go to the employer, not the employee's family.
  • Cover lapses on resignation, so personal term plans remain essential.
  • Employers should disclose the master policy, not just a summary.

How to Evaluate Your Employer's Package

Ask HR for the master policy number, the insurer's name, the sum assured formula and the rider schedule. Confirm whether cover is flat across grades or linked to CTC, and whether voluntary top-up is permitted at group rates. Employees aged above 45 or with dependents should run a needs analysis separately.

For employers, the calculus is straightforward. A well-structured group plan with two or three relevant riders costs a fraction of salary and materially improves retention. Keyman cover protects continuity when a single individual drives revenue. Both are commercial decisions, and both deserve a proper review rather than a tick-box renewal.
