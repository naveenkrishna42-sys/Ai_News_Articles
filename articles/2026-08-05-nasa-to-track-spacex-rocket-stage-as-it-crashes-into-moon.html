NASA to track SpaceX rocket stage as it crashes into Moon

📅 2026-08-05 📂 Science Original source ↗
Key points
  • A SpaceX rocket stage is on course to crash into the Moon's far side.
  • NASA will track and observe the impact to study crater formation.
  • The event provides a unique chance to learn about the Moon's subsurface.
  • No spacecraft has been lost; the stage was left in orbit after a 2015 mission.

A rare lunar collision

NASA is preparing to track a SpaceX rocket stage as it crashes into the Moon, an event that astronomers say will offer a rare chance to study the lunar surface. The rocket part, left in orbit after a deep-space mission, is expected to hit the far side of the Moon, away from the view of Earth-based telescopes.

The collision is unintentional — the stage was never designed to return to Earth or enter a controlled lunar impact. But scientists are now treating it as a scientific opportunity. The impact is expected to create a fresh crater, and NASA’s Lunar Reconnaissance Orbiter (LRO) will be positioned to observe the aftermath.

What will NASA track?

The agency's focus is on the impact site and the resulting debris plume. By observing how the crash unfolds, researchers hope to learn more about the Moon's subsurface composition — material that is normally buried deep and hard to study.

According to NASA officials, the LRO will attempt to image the impact zone before and after the crash. The before-and-after comparison could reveal details about the consistency of the lunar soil at that location, and how much ejecta is thrown out during a high-velocity impact.

This is not the first time a spacecraft has made an unplanned crash landing on the Moon. In 2009, NASA's LCROSS mission deliberately slammed a rocket stage into a lunar crater and found water ice in the debris. But this upcoming event is different — it is accidental, and the exact impact time and location are still being refined.

Why the far side?

The rocket stage is expected to hit the far side of the Moon, which never faces Earth. That makes direct observation from the ground impossible, and it also means the impact will not be visible to amateur skywatchers. But the LRO, which orbits the Moon, will be in a position to capture the event.

Astronomers have been tracking the object for weeks, using ground-based radar and optical telescopes to refine its trajectory. The stage was originally used to launch a deep-space mission in 2015, and after completing its job, it was left in a chaotic orbit around Earth and the Moon. Over time, gravitational interactions nudged it onto a collision course.

No risk to existing lunar missions

Officials have confirmed that the impact poses no threat to any active lunar spacecraft. The crash site is far from the Apollo landing sites and other historical locations, and the LRO itself will be at a safe distance during the event.

This is a reminder that space debris is not just an Earth-orbit problem. As more missions head to the Moon, the risk of accidental impacts increases. The event has sparked discussions about better tracking and disposal of spent rocket stages in cislunar space.

What happens next

NASA will release preliminary observations after the impact, and the LRO's images will be compared with pre-impact data. Scientists expect to publish a detailed analysis of the crater in the following months.

For now, the crash serves as an unintended experiment — one that could help future missions plan safer landings and better understand the Moon's geology. As lunar traffic grows, every impact, planned or not, adds to our knowledge.
