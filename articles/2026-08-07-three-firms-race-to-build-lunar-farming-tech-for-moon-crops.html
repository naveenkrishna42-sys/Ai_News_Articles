Three firms race to build lunar farming tech for moon crops

📅 2026-08-07 📂 Science Original source ↗
Key points
  • Three companies are developing lunar agriculture technology.
  • Purdue University is leading research on growing plants in space.
  • ISS Expedition 75 is growing red Russian kale and wasabi mustard.
  • These efforts support future long-duration space missions.

Lunar agriculture moves from science fiction to startup race

Growing food on the moon is no longer just a plot point in sci-fi novels. Three companies are now building the technology to cultivate crops in lunar conditions, a development that could prove essential for long-duration space missions and permanent settlements beyond Earth.

The push comes as space agencies and private players alike eye the moon as a staging ground for deeper exploration. If humans are to stay for extended periods, they will need a reliable food source that does not depend on resupply missions from Earth.

Purdue leads the charge in space plant research

Purdue University is at the forefront of this effort, leading research on how to grow plants in the harsh environment of space. Scientists there are studying how microgravity, radiation, and limited resources affect plant growth—knowledge that will be critical for designing lunar greenhouses.

The university's work is not just academic. It feeds directly into the technologies being developed by the three companies, which are racing to turn research into practical, deployable systems.

ISS astronauts are already growing kale and mustard

Onboard the International Space Station, Expedition 75 has brought a new kind of crew member: red Russian kale and wasabi mustard. These aren't just for salads—they are part of experiments to understand how plants adapt to space conditions.

Astronauts have successfully grown sprouts of these leafy greens, marking a step forward in the quest for self-sufficiency in space. The data from these experiments will inform the design of lunar farming systems, where gravity is only one-sixth of Earth's and sunlight is not always available.

The technology behind moon farming

Growing crops on the moon presents unique challenges. The soil, or regolith, is barren and lacks the organic matter plants need. Water is scarce, and the temperature swings are extreme—from scorching days to freezing nights.

The three companies are tackling these hurdles with innovative solutions. Some are developing hydroponic or aeroponic systems that use nutrient-rich water instead of soil. Others are working on artificial lighting that mimics the sun's spectrum, ensuring photosynthesis can occur even during the long lunar night.

One key focus is on closed-loop systems that recycle water and nutrients, minimising waste. Such systems would be vital for a lunar base, where every resource is precious.

Why this matters for future missions

The ability to grow food on the moon is not just about sustenance. It also has psychological benefits for astronauts, who would have a touch of green in an otherwise sterile environment. Plus, fresh produce offers nutritional value that packaged foods cannot match.

Experts say the technology developed for lunar farming could also have applications on Earth, particularly in arid regions or urban vertical farms. The spin-offs could help address food security challenges at home.

As the race to the moon intensifies, with NASA's Artemis programme and other international efforts, the demand for such technology is only expected to grow. The companies involved are positioning themselves to be key suppliers in the emerging lunar economy.

For now, the work remains in the testing phase. But the progress made on the ISS and in labs like Purdue's is laying the groundwork for a future where humans can live and work on the moon.

What happens next will depend on how quickly these technologies can be scaled up and proven reliable. The next few years will be crucial as more missions head to the lunar surface, and the dream of moon farming inches closer to reality.
