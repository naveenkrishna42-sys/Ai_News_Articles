Indian police hospitalise activist Sonam Wangchuk after 20-day hunger strike

📅 2026-07-18 📂 India Original source ↗
Key points
  • Sonam Wangchuk was forcibly hospitalised by Delhi Police after a 20-day hunger strike at Jantar Mantar.
  • His personal doctor disputes Safdarjung Hospital's report citing potassium deficiency.
  • Congress has accused the government of crushing peaceful protest.
  • Legal questions arise over the government's authority to force-feed a hunger striker.

Delhi Police on Saturday forcibly hospitalised climate activist Sonam Wangchuk, who was on a 20-day hunger strike at Jantar Mantar. The activist, known for his work in Ladakh, was taken to Safdarjung Hospital against his will, sparking a political controversy over the right to peaceful protest.

Doctor rejects hospital's medical assessment

Wangchuk's personal physician has publicly rejected the hospital's claim that the activist was suffering from potassium deficiency. Calling the report 'suspicious,' the doctor said the findings did not align with Wangchuk's medical history or his condition before the hospitalisation.

The activist had been consuming only water and salt during the strike. His medical team had been monitoring him throughout and had not flagged any critical imbalance until police intervened.

Political fallout and accusations

The Congress party has accused the central government of using state machinery to crush a peaceful protest. Party spokespersons alleged that the removal of Wangchuk from Jantar Mantar was an attempt to silence dissent over issues affecting the Himalayan region.

Opposition leaders have demanded a full explanation from the Home Ministry. They argue that the forcible hospitalisation sets a dangerous precedent for civil liberties in the country.

Wangchuk's wife, Gitanjali Angmo — a karate black belt and former Russian ballet dancer — has also spoken out against the police action. She said her husband had made his intentions clear and that the government should have respected his choice.

Legal questions around force-feeding

The incident has revived debate over the legality of force-feeding hunger strikers in India. Under current law, the state can intervene if a person's life is in danger, but activists argue that informed consent and the right to protest must be respected.

Legal experts say the Supreme Court has previously held that the right to refuse medical treatment is part of the right to life under Article 21. However, the government can cite the duty to preserve life as grounds for intervention.

Police officials claim Wangchuk's health had deteriorated and that they acted on medical advice. They have released a statement saying the hospitalisation was a precautionary measure.

Wangchuk remains under observation at Safdarjung Hospital. His supporters have gathered outside, demanding his immediate release and vowing to continue the protest.

What happens next depends on the courts. The activist's legal team is expected to challenge the hospitalisation in the Delhi High Court, arguing that the government overstepped its authority.
