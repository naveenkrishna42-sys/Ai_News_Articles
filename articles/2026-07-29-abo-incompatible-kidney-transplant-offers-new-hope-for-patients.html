ABO-incompatible kidney transplant offers new hope for patients

📅 2026-07-29 📂 Health Original source ↗
Key points
  • ABO-incompatible transplants allow kidneys from donors with different blood types.
  • Desensitisation therapy removes antibodies before transplant.
  • Procedure increases donor pool and reduces waiting time.

A breakthrough in kidney transplantation

For years, blood type compatibility was a strict barrier for kidney transplants. A patient could only receive a kidney from a donor with a matching ABO blood group. This rule left many on the waiting list for years, hoping for a miracle.

Now, ABO-incompatible kidney transplants are changing that. The procedure lets patients receive kidneys from donors whose blood types do not match their own. It is giving many a second chance at life.

How desensitisation makes it possible

The key to this transplant is desensitisation therapy. Before the surgery, doctors filter the recipient's blood to remove antibodies that would attack the donor kidney. This process is done over several sessions, using a machine similar to dialysis.

After each session, doctors check antibody levels. Once they drop low enough, the transplant can proceed. The recipient also receives medications to suppress the immune system and prevent rejection.

Expanding the donor pool

India faces a severe shortage of organ donors. According to the Organ Retrieval Banking Organisation, only a fraction of those needing transplants get them. ABO-incompatible transplants can significantly expand the pool of available kidneys.

A patient with blood group O, for example, can now receive a kidney from a group A or B donor. This is especially helpful for those with rare blood types or difficult-to-match tissue types. It reduces the average waiting time from several years to months.

Success rates and risks

Studies show that ABO-incompatible transplants have success rates comparable to compatible ones. The five-year graft survival rate is around 80 per cent. However, the procedure carries higher risks of early rejection and infection due to intense immunosuppression.

Patients must commit to lifelong monitoring and medication. The desensitisation process itself can be physically demanding. But for many, the trade-off is worth it.

Cost and accessibility in India

An ABO-incompatible transplant costs roughly 30-50 per cent more than a standard one. The extra expense comes from desensitisation therapy, specialised lab tests, and stronger anti-rejection drugs. Most major hospitals in cities like Delhi, Mumbai, and Chennai now offer the procedure.

Some state governments and insurance schemes cover part of the cost. However, affordability remains a barrier for many families. Doctors hope that as the technique becomes more common, costs will come down.

The procedure is not for everyone. Patients with high baseline antibody levels or certain medical conditions may not qualify. A thorough evaluation by a transplant team is essential.

What to watch next

As awareness grows, more transplant centres in India are likely to adopt ABO-incompatible protocols. The government is also working on policies to increase deceased organ donation. For now, this technique remains a lifeline for those who have exhausted other options.
