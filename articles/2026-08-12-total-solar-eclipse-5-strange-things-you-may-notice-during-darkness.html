Total solar eclipse: 5 strange things you may notice during darkness

📅 2026-08-12 📂 Science Original source ↗
Key points
  • Shadow bands remain a scientific mystery, with researchers still chasing them during eclipses.
  • Temperature drops, animal behaviour changes, and 360-degree sunsets are among the effects.
  • The eclipse's brief totality offers a rare chance to observe these strange occurrences.
  • Scientists from Pittsburgh and other institutions are actively studying shadow bands.
  • The next total solar eclipse may not be visible from the same region for decades.

The sky goes dark, but not silent

When the Moon slides fully in front of the Sun, the world does not simply go black. It goes strange. The few minutes of totality bring a cascade of oddities that even seasoned skywatchers find unsettling. Temperature can drop by several degrees, streetlights flicker on, and the wind may pick up or die down entirely.

Birds stop singing. Crickets start chirping, thinking dusk has arrived. Domestic animals often show confusion – dogs may howl, and cows have been known to head back to their sheds. These are not hallucinations; they are documented responses to the sudden loss of daylight.

Shadow bands: the elusive stripes

One of the most puzzling phenomena is shadow bands – faint, wavy lines of light and dark that ripple across the ground just before and after totality. They look like the shimmering patterns at the bottom of a swimming pool, but on roads, walls, or even snow.

Scientists have chased these bands for decades. Researchers in Pittsburgh are among those trying to solve the mystery. The bands are believed to result from atmospheric turbulence refracting the thin crescent of sunlight, but their exact behaviour remains unpredictable. Some eclipses produce them clearly; others show nothing at all.

Why are they so hard to study?

Shadow bands are faint, fast, and unpredictable. They last only seconds to a minute, and they require specific atmospheric conditions. This makes them difficult to capture on camera, let alone measure with instruments.

Recent efforts have used high-speed video and sensitive light sensors spread over a wide area. But each eclipse offers only a short window, and the bands often appear where no one is looking.

Baily's beads and the diamond ring

Just before totality, a brilliant flash of light – the diamond ring effect – appears as the last sliver of sunlight shines through valleys on the Moon's edge. Moments later, Baily's beads form: tiny points of light that look like a string of pearls along the Moon's silhouette.

These are caused by sunlight streaming through lunar mountains and craters. They last only a few seconds, so observers need to be ready. Many mistake the diamond ring for the end of the eclipse and remove their glasses too early – a dangerous error.

The 360-degree sunset

During totality, the horizon glows in every direction, like a sunset that surrounds you. This happens because you are inside the Moon's shadow, but areas outside it still see the Sun. The scattered light from those regions creates a warm, eerie glow.

This effect is often described as otherworldly. Some photographers capture it by taking panoramic shots just after totality begins. The colours can range from deep orange to pale yellow, depending on dust and moisture in the air.

What to watch for

If you are in the path of totality, keep your eyes open – but not on the Sun without proper filters. Look at the ground for shadow bands, listen for animals, and note the temperature change. These few minutes are the only time you can safely view the Sun's corona, the outer atmosphere, with the naked eye.

Researchers hope that with more data from this eclipse, the mystery of shadow bands may finally be solved. But the next total solar eclipse visible from a given region could be decades away, so every observation counts.

For now, scientists will continue to chase those faint, flickering lines – and perhaps the next eclipse will reveal their secret.
