Vidyut Jammwal: Time, not talent, is the real divide in action films

From choreography to safety: why “time” matters in action filmmaking

Vidyut Jammwal’s point is refreshingly practical: in action cinema, what separates Indian and Hollywood films is often not talent, but time. His framing shifts the conversation away from romantic notions of “natural skill” and toward the real production variables that shape what lands on screen—rehearsals, blocking, camera planning, and stunt safety.

In high-intensity action, talent is only one input. Precision comes from repetition. When performers and coordinators get adequate days or weeks to iterate on choreography, the action becomes readable—through body language, timing, and spatial consistency. When schedules compress, the film may still look dynamic, but the physics and continuity can feel less controlled.

For viewers, that “control” is often perceived as smoothness: clean transitions, confident landings, and fights that follow a coherent rhythm instead of jumping between beats. For producers and commercial buyers, the “time factor” shows up in risk management—fewer reshoots, less injury downtime, and steadier editing.

How more prep time changes the economics of action

Action filmmaking has a hidden cost structure. The visible expenses—stunt teams, locations, rigging, and VFX—are only part of the story. The bigger question is how many iterations you can afford during pre-production and rehearsal. Time buys problem-solving: adjusting choreography for camera angles, verifying safe distances for close-contact stunts, and ensuring continuity across takes.

In many productions, the fastest path to “good footage” is actually the slower path to “final product.” Adequate time reduces the number of takes needed to get usable angles, lowers the likelihood of stunt-related delays, and supports a more predictable edit. In commercial terms, this can protect release schedules and marketing plans tied to production milestones.

It also affects how action is delivered. Hollywood-style action sequences are frequently built around camera language—tracking routes, pre-marked sight lines, and shot coverage designed before the choreography locks in. That workflow tends to demand time upfront, but it can reduce expensive scrambling later.

What buyers should watch for: production signals behind the scenes

Whether you’re a distributor evaluating a film’s action credibility or a producer planning a slate, “time” should be treated as a measurable production signal, not a vague compliment. Buyers can look for evidence that the film had enough rehearsal cycles and planning depth: longer principal photography for action-heavy schedules, documented stunt rehearsal workflows, and credible action-unit coordination.

From a practical standpoint, action performance quality often correlates with how rehearsals are structured—how many choreography passes occur before camera day, whether stunt performers rehearse with the same camera setup they’ll shoot with, and whether editorial teams can anticipate match cuts and continuity constraints early.

Post-production also benefits. When takes are cleaner and more consistent, VFX and cleanup work require fewer “make-good” fixes. That reduces both cost volatility and schedule pressure—two factors that routinely impact smaller-budget productions trying to emulate blockbuster action aesthetics.

Real-world decision checklist for action-heavy projects

  • Ask whether choreography is rehearsed to camera angles or only to performance marks
  • Check if stunt safety rehearsals are given dedicated time rather than squeezed into shoot days
  • Look for production evidence of shot coverage planning before choreography finalisation
  • Assess whether the schedule allows retakes for continuity—especially for contact points and weapon-like props
  • Evaluate how much VFX “cleanup” is needed to make action readable; heavy cleanup can indicate time pressure during shooting

“Talent vs time” isn’t either/or—it's sequencing

Jammwal’s argument shouldn’t be read as a dismissal of talent. The highest-performing action films typically combine strong performer ability with an efficient, time-backed workflow. The sequencing matters: choreography must be built, tested, and re-tested until it becomes stable under the chaos of production constraints.

In other words, talent determines ceiling, but time shapes reliability. Reliability is what translates to coherent scenes across multiple takes, consistent performance under fatigue, and safe stunt execution with minimal disruption. Those are the features that audiences feel—even if they can’t name them.

For Indian action cinema ambitions, the takeaway is strategic. Closing the gap may not require importing “Hollywood talent” so much as adopting production practices that grant adequate rehearsal and camera-to-action integration time. That may demand budget reprioritisation and schedule redesign, but it can deliver outsized returns in viewer satisfaction and operational efficiency.

Key implications for producers and distributors

  • Scheduling: Action sequences likely need more lead time than dialogue scenes to avoid continuity and coverage gaps
  • Budgeting: More rehearsal days can reduce reshoot probability and stunt downtime costs
  • Editing: Better coverage planning supports smoother assembly and lowers the need for heavy cleanup
  • Safety: Time-backed choreography improves risk controls, benefiting both performers and production continuity
