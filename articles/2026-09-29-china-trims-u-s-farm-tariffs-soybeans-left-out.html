China Trims U.S. Farm Tariffs, Soybeans Left Out

✍️ By TIVRA News Editorial Team 📅 2026-09-29 📂 World Original source ↗
Key points
  • China reduces tariffs on $30 billion of U.S. farm goods excluding soybeans.
  • Soybean exclusion reflects domestic supply concerns and policy priorities.
  • Tariff adjustments aim to stabilise bilateral trade amid broader negotiations.

Overview of the Tariff Adjustment

China announced a reciprocal tariff reduction on $30 billion worth of U.S. goods, focusing on agricultural products while deliberately omitting soybeans. The move follows months of consultation under the Phase One trade framework and aims to ease tensions without compromising China's food security objectives. Both sides framed the adjustment as a confidence‑building measure ahead of wider negotiations.

Details of the Tariff Cuts

The cuts target specific categories including pork, poultry, dairy, fruits, nuts, and processed foods. Average tariff rates on these items will fall from the current range of 12‑20 percent to between 5‑10 percent, depending on the product. The reductions are slated to take effect within 30 days of the official notice, with a review clause after six months.

Product Categories Covered

Covered items include fresh and frozen meat, cheese, butter, apples, grapes, almonds, walnuts, and various canned vegetables. The selection reflects China's import demand gaps and the U.S. export strength in high‑value, processed agriculture. By contrast, bulk commodities such as corn and wheat saw only marginal adjustments.

Why Soybeans Remain Excluded

Soybeans, which account for roughly 60 percent of U.S. agricultural exports to China, were left untouched due to domestic stockpile levels and policy support for local soybean producers. Chinese officials cited the need to maintain a buffer against global price volatility and to protect the livelihoods of millions of farmers in the northeast. The exclusion also preserves leverage in ongoing talks about technology transfer and market access.

Implications for U.S. Farmers and Exporters

U.S. producers of pork, dairy, and specialty fruits stand to gain immediate price competitiveness in the Chinese market, potentially boosting export volumes by 5‑8 percent in the first year. Soybean growers, however, will continue to face the existing 25 percent tariff, limiting any short‑term relief. Analysts note that the selective approach may encourage diversification of U.S. export baskets toward higher‑margin products.

Market Reaction and Price Effects

Following the announcement, Chicago Mercantile Exchange futures for lean hogs rose 1.2 percent, while soybean futures remained flat. Traders interpreted the move as a signal of limited but tangible progress, reducing some risk premium in livestock contracts. Currency markets showed little reaction, indicating that the tariff shift is viewed as a tactical rather than strategic shift.

Broader Trade Context

The tariff adjustment sits within a series of reciprocal concessions that have accumulated since early 2025, covering sectors such as toys, electronics, and household appliances. Collectively, these measures affect approximately $60 billion of bilateral trade, aiming to reduce the average applied tariff from 15 percent to roughly 9 percent across the agreed list. The incremental nature reflects both sides' preference for manageable, verifiable steps.

Historical Tariff Trends

Since the 2018 trade dispute, average U.S.‑China tariffs on agricultural goods have fluctuated between 10 percent and 30 percent, depending on product and political climate. The current round of cuts represents the first meaningful downward movement since the 2020 Phase One agreement, which had left soybean tariffs largely unchanged. Continued monitoring will be essential to assess whether further reductions are forthcoming.
