Plasma gun tech could revolutionise space laundry and sanitation

📅 2026-08-07 📂 Science Original source ↗
Key points
  • Plasma gun technology cleans fabrics and surfaces without water
  • Could slash water usage and waste on long-duration space missions
  • Tests show effectiveness against bacteria and odours in simulated microgravity

A new spin on cleaning in orbit

Astronauts on the International Space Station currently wear clothes until they are too dirty to use, then discard them. Those garments are loaded onto cargo ships that burn up in Earth's atmosphere. It is wasteful, but with limited water and no washing machines in space, there has been little choice.

Now researchers are exploring a different approach: a handheld device that uses a jet of ionised gas, or plasma, to break down dirt and kill microbes on fabric. The technology, described in a recent study, could change how crews manage hygiene on long missions to the Moon or Mars.

How the plasma gun works

The device generates a low-temperature plasma — a gas where atoms have been stripped of some electrons — and directs it onto surfaces. The reactive particles in the plasma attack organic material, lifting grime and neutralising bacteria without needing water or detergent.

In laboratory tests, the plasma gun removed simulated sweat and skin oils from cotton and polyester swatches. It also reduced bacterial counts on the fabric by several orders of magnitude, according to the study published in the journal *Frontiers in Space Technologies*.

The same device could be used to sanitise food-contact surfaces, medical tools, and even air filters on spacecraft, the researchers said.

Why it matters for deep space

Water is one of the heaviest and most precious resources on a spacecraft. Every litre launched adds to the cost and complexity of a mission. Current systems recycle urine and condensation, but they cannot handle laundry effluent easily.

Eliminating the need to wash clothes would save water and reduce the volume of waste. It would also cut the mass of spare clothing that must be carried for a multi-year trip.

For a crew of four on a 600-day round trip to Mars, the savings could amount to several hundred kilograms of clothing and water, the researchers estimate. That is space and weight that could be used for scientific instruments or additional supplies.

Challenges before it flies

Plasma technology is not new — it is already used in industry for sterilising medical devices and treating surfaces. But adapting it for space presents hurdles.

The device must operate safely in a sealed, microgravity environment without producing harmful ozone or other by-products. It also needs to be compact, low-power, and easy to use while wearing gloves.

Tests so far have been conducted on Earth, with simulated conditions. The next step, the researchers say, is to test the gun on parabolic flights that provide brief periods of weightlessness, and eventually aboard the space station.

Beyond laundry: a tool for sanitation

The plasma gun's potential goes beyond clean clothes. On a space station, surfaces are constantly touched and can harbour fungi and bacteria. The closed environment and recycled air make infection control a priority.

Current cleaning methods rely on liquid disinfectants that must be wiped and dried. A plasma-based system could offer a dry, residue-free alternative that is quicker and uses fewer consumables.

The technology could also find uses on Earth — in remote clinics, disaster zones, or places with limited water. But the immediate driver is space exploration, where every gram of payload matters.

What to watch next

The research team plans to refine the prototype and test it under more realistic space conditions. If those trials go well, a flight-ready version could be on the station within a few years.

For now, astronauts will continue to wear their clothes until they fall apart — but a plasma-powered laundry day may not be far off.
