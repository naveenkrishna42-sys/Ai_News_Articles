How the first bird learned to fly in two evolutionary leaps

📅 2026-08-12 📂 Science Original source ↗
Key points
  • Archaeopteryx, the 'first bird,' evolved flight in two distinct steps, not one.
  • Fossil analysis suggests gliding came first, followed by flapping capabilities.
  • The finding reshapes understanding of how dinosaurs transitioned to birds.

A fossil that rewrites avian evolution

For over a century, Archaeopteryx has held a special place in the story of life on Earth. The feathered dinosaur, unearthed from Bavarian limestone, was long celebrated as the earliest known bird. Yet the mechanics of how it actually took to the air have remained a puzzle.

Now, a fresh analysis of its skeletal structure suggests the answer lies in not one, but two distinct evolutionary leaps. Researchers have concluded that Archaeopteryx did not learn to fly in a single stroke of adaptation. Instead, it passed through an intermediate stage, one that separated gliding from flapping, before achieving true powered flight.

Two steps, not one

The study, published in the journal Current Biology, compared the wing and shoulder anatomy of Archaeopteryx with a broad range of living birds and extinct dinosaurs. The team found that the earliest ancestors of birds first developed wings suited for gliding, perhaps for leaping between trees or escaping predators. This phase would have required a different shoulder structure, more flexible and less robust, than what later birds possess.

Only after this gliding stage did selection pressure favour the stronger, more rigid shoulder that enables rapid wing beats. In other words, the flapping flight we see in modern birds came as a second, separate innovation. The two capabilities, gliding and flapping, did not evolve together but sequentially.

What this means for dinosaur flight

The finding challenges the long-held assumption that powered flight emerged directly from a ground-dwelling ancestor. Instead, it suggests a more meandering path, one that involved intermediate forms with limited aerial ability. These intermediate species may have used their wings for a mix of gliding and parachuting, slowly refining their control before the full stroke of flight became possible.

Lead author of the study, Dr. Michael Pittman from the Chinese University of Hong Kong, described the transition as a 'two-stage process'. He noted that the shoulder joint in early birds was initially adapted for folding wings, not for rapid flapping. Only later did it evolve to support the demanding motion of powered flight.

Implications for the bird family tree

This revised timeline also affects how palaeontologists classify ancient species. Some creatures previously considered 'early birds' may have been gliders, not flappers, requiring a re-evaluation of their place on the evolutionary tree. The work underscores that the path from dinosaur to bird was not a straight line but a series of experimental forms, each testing a different way to conquer the air.

The researchers caution that their conclusions rest on a limited fossil record. Archaeopteryx specimens are rare, and the exact sequence of changes may vary across lineages. Still, the new model offers a compelling explanation for why early bird fossils show a mosaic of features, some adapted for gliding, others for flapping.

What to watch next

The team plans to expand its analysis to other early avian fossils, including species from China and South America, to test the two-step hypothesis further. As more specimens come to light, the story of how the first bird learnt to fly may continue to evolve, one fossil at a time.
