Unmasking the 'Analogue' Paneer Fraud in Hyderabad

Food safety authorities in Hyderabad have uncovered a disturbing practice at certain dairy processing units manufacturing 'analogue' paneer using palm oil instead of pure milk solids. This investigation, originally reported by The Hindu, exposes a significant gap in regulatory enforcement and highlights ongoing vulnerabilities within India's vast informal dairy sector. The findings raise serious concerns about consumer health, product labelling standards, and the commercial pressures driving cost-cutting in regional dairy supply chains.

Analogue paneer is fundamentally an imitation cheese product engineered to mimic the sensory characteristics of traditional paneer but manufactured using vegetable fats, starches, protein concentrates, and emulsifying salts rather than milk. The practice of substituting cheaper palm oil for milk fat directly contravenes Food Safety and Standards Authority of India (FSSAI) regulations, which mandate specific compositional standards for products labelled and sold as paneer.

What Is Analogue Paneer and Why It Matters

The distinction between authentic paneer and analogue cheese is critical for both regulatory compliance and consumer transparency. FSSAI's Food Safety and Standards (Food Products Standards and Food Additives) Regulations establish milk fat and milk protein requirements that legitimate paneer must meet. Products deviating from these standards cannot legally be marketed as paneer without clear disclosure.

This Hyderabad incident mirrors similar adulteration cases reported in other Indian metropolitan markets, suggesting a systemic pattern rather than an isolated quality control failure. The economic incentive is substantial: palm oil costs approximately ₹90-110 per kilogram in wholesale markets, while milk solids required for genuine paneer production cost ₹280-350 per kilogram, creating a powerful financial motivation for adulteration.

Health Implications for Consumers

Consuming palm oil-based analogue paneer carries multiple health risks that extend beyond simple economic deception. Paneer is traditionally positioned as a high-protein dairy product rich in calcium, phosphorus, and bioactive peptides. When substituted with vegetable fat, consumers lose these nutritional benefits while unknowingly ingesting high levels of saturated palmitic acid.

Regular consumption of palm oil has been associated in peer-reviewed nutritional studies with elevated LDL cholesterol levels and increased cardiovascular risk markers. For diabetic consumers specifically, palm oil's high saturated fat content can contribute to insulin resistance. Additionally, individuals with legitimate milk allergies who rely on ingredient labelling may experience adverse reactions if vegetable oil cross-contamination is not properly declared.

Key Real-World Insights for Hyderabad Buyers

  • Verify FSSAI licensing: Always check the FSSAI logo and licence number printed on packaged paneer before purchasing; legitimate dairy processors display a 14-digit FSSAI number.
  • Price as a quality indicator: Genuine paneer retail prices in Hyderabad typically range from ₹380-500 per kilogram; significantly cheaper offerings warrant scrutiny.
  • Texture and melt characteristics: Authentic paneer remains firm when heated, while analogue products often soften rapidly or release visible oil.
  • Reporting suspected adulteration: Consumers can file complaints with the Telangana State Food Safety Authority through the FSSAI's Food Safety Connect portal or mobile application.

Regulatory Outlook and Industry Accountability

The Hyderabad detection exposes enforcement gaps that require coordinated response from state food safety authorities, the Dairy Development Department, and consumer protection bodies. Strengthening laboratory testing infrastructure at district-level dairy collection centres would create earlier intervention points in the supply chain. Industry associations representing legitimate dairy processors have consistently advocated for stricter penalties to protect brand-integrity operators who bear compliance costs that negligent competitors systematically avoid.

For Hyderabad consumers, this case reinforces the value of purchasing from established dairy brands with traceable supply chains rather than unbranded loose paneer from informal vendors. The incident also highlights why premium pricing in the dairy sector often reflects genuine milk content rather than mere brand positioning, a critical distinction in markets where adulteration remains commercially tempting.
