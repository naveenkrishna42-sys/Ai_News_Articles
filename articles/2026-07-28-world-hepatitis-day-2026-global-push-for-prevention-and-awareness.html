World Hepatitis Day 2026: Global Push for Prevention and Awareness

📅 2026-07-28 📂 Health Original source ↗
Key points
  • World Hepatitis Day observed on July 28 to raise awareness and push for elimination by 2030.
  • Ripon Buildings in Kolkata illuminated green as part of global awareness campaign.
  • WHO data shows 1.1 million deaths annually from hepatitis, mostly from hepatitis B and C.

Global Observance Marks Day of Action

World Hepatitis Day was observed across the globe on July 28, with governments, health agencies, and communities coming together to highlight the urgent need for prevention, testing, and treatment of viral hepatitis. The day, coordinated by the World Health Organization, aims to turn the spotlight on a disease that remains a major public health threat despite being preventable and treatable.

In India, the Union Ministry of Health and Family Welfare reiterated its commitment to eliminating hepatitis by 2030, aligning with the global target set by the WHO. Health officials stressed the importance of vaccination, safe blood transfusion practices, and better sanitation to curb the spread of the virus.

Ripon Buildings Lit Green in Solidarity

In a symbolic gesture, the historic Ripon Buildings in Kolkata were illuminated in green on Tuesday evening. The lighting was part of a worldwide campaign to "green" iconic landmarks and raise public awareness about hepatitis. Other monuments across countries also participated, sending a clear visual message about the fight against the disease.

The green illumination is a reminder that hepatitis often remains silent until it causes serious liver damage, including cirrhosis and cancer. Public health experts say that millions of people living with hepatitis are unaware of their status, making awareness drives critical.

Understanding Hepatitis: Types and Risks

Hepatitis refers to inflammation of the liver, most commonly caused by viral infections. There are five main types: A, B, C, D, and E. Hepatitis B and C together cause 1.1 million deaths annually worldwide, according to WHO estimates. Hepatitis B is transmitted through blood, sex, and from mother to child. Hepatitis C spreads mainly through blood-to-blood contact, often from unsafe injections or transfusions.

Those at highest risk include healthcare workers exposed to blood, people who inject drugs, men who have sex with men, and newborns of infected mothers. Hepatitis A and E, meanwhile, are typically spread through contaminated food and water, making sanitation and hygiene key preventive measures.

Historical Roots: A Medical Puzzle

The history of hepatitis research stretches back to 1885, when a mysterious outbreak following a smallpox vaccination in Germany first alerted doctors to the existence of a transmissible liver disease. That outbreak, later understood to be hepatitis B, set off a medical investigation that took decades to solve. It wasn't until the 1960s that the virus was identified, leading to the development of the first hepatitis B vaccine in the 1980s.

Today, safe and effective vaccines exist for hepatitis A and B. While there is no vaccine for hepatitis C, modern antiviral therapies can cure over 95% of cases. However, access to testing and treatment remains uneven, especially in low- and middle-income countries.

India's Progress and the Road Ahead

India launched its National Viral Hepatitis Control Program in 2018, aiming to provide free diagnosis and treatment for hepatitis B and C. The program has screened millions and treated lakhs of patients. However, challenges persist in reaching remote rural areas and high-risk populations.

Officials have not yet confirmed the total number of people screened under the program this year, but efforts continue to expand testing facilities and public awareness campaigns. The day's events also included free screening camps, health talks, and social media campaigns urging people to "know your status."

As World Hepatitis Day 2026 draws to a close, the focus now shifts to sustained action. With vaccines, treatments, and political will available, the goal of eliminating viral hepatitis by 2030 remains ambitious but achievable — provided the momentum does not fade.
