OMR Jaywalking: A Daily Gamble for Motorists

Chennai's Old Mahabalipuram Road (OMR), the city's IT backbone, is witnessing a surge in jaywalking incidents, sparking concern among motorists. The 20-km stretch from Madhya Kailash to Sholinganallur sees thousands of pedestrians crossing at will, often ignoring foot overbridges and signalised crossings. With vehicle speeds averaging 50-60 kmph during non-peak hours, the risk of fatal collisions is high. Motorists argue that jaywalking not only endangers lives but also disrupts traffic flow, adding to commute times and fuel costs.

Why Jaywalking Persists on OMR

Infrastructure Gaps

Despite OMR's rapid commercial growth, pedestrian infrastructure lags. Foot overbridges are sparse and poorly maintained; many lack ramps for the elderly and differently-abled. Bus stops are often located mid-block, forcing commuters to cross busy roads. The Chennai Metropolitan Development Authority (CMDA) has proposed 15 new pedestrian crossings, but implementation is slow.

Enforcement Challenges

Traffic police personnel are stretched thin. According to a 2025 study by the Chennai Traffic Police, jaywalking violations peak between 8-10 AM and 5-8 PM, coinciding with IT shift changes. Penalties exist—₹500 fine under the Motor Vehicles Act—but enforcement is sporadic. Many offenders go unchecked, emboldening others.

Real-World Impact: Data and Costs

Jaywalking contributes to a significant share of road accidents on OMR. In 2025, the Greater Chennai Traffic Police recorded 87 pedestrian-involved accidents on OMR, of which 62% were attributed to jaywalking. Motorists bear the brunt: average repair costs for a minor collision range from ₹15,000 to ₹50,000, while insurance premiums can rise by 10-15% after a claim. Beyond financial loss, the psychological stress of sudden pedestrian encounters is immense.

Pros and Cons of Current Measures

  • Pros: Increased police patrolling during peak hours; awareness campaigns by IT parks; installation of CCTV cameras at key junctions.
  • Cons: Inadequate foot overbridges; lack of real-time enforcement; poor lighting at crossing points; absence of dedicated pedestrian zones.

What Can Be Done? Practical Solutions

Experts suggest a three-pronged approach. First, infrastructure: build more foot overbridges with escalators and ramps, and synchronise pedestrian signals with traffic lights. Second, enforcement: deploy AI-powered cameras to detect jaywalking and issue e-challans automatically. Third, education: collaborate with IT companies to run safety workshops and incentivise use of designated crossings. The Chennai Corporation's pilot project at Sholinganallur, which reduced jaywalking by 30% through barricades and signage, offers a scalable model.

Buyer's Guide: What Motorists Can Do

Until systemic fixes arrive, motorists on OMR should adopt defensive driving. Maintain lower speeds in known jaywalking hotspots, especially near bus stops and tech parks. Consider dashcams (₹3,000–₹8,000) to document incidents for insurance claims. Opt for comprehensive insurance with zero-depreciation cover to minimise out-of-pocket expenses. Finally, report persistent jaywalking zones to the traffic police via the Chennai Traffic Police app.

The Road Ahead

Jaywalking on OMR is a symptom of inadequate urban planning. As Chennai expands, integrating pedestrian safety into road design is non-negotiable. The cost of inaction—human lives and crores in economic losses—far outweighs the investment needed. Motorists, pedestrians, and authorities must collaborate to make OMR safer for all.
