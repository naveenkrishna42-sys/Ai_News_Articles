Gulf states quietly accept Iran's control of Strait of Hormuz

📅 2026-08-12 📂 World Original source ↗
Key points
  • Gulf states increasingly acknowledge Iran's effective control over the Strait of Hormuz
  • Iran links reopening of strait to US concessions on multiple demands
  • Oman emerges as key intermediary in shipping negotiations
  • Fresh attacks on shipping reported as war talks remain deadlocked

The Strait of Hormuz, the narrow waterway through which nearly a fifth of the world's oil passes, has become the focal point of a shifting Middle East power equation. Iranian naval vessels now patrol its waters with a confidence that would have been unthinkable just a year ago.

Gulf states, long reliant on the United States to guarantee freedom of navigation, are quietly recalibrating. Diplomatic sources indicate that several Gulf capitals have moved from demanding a confrontation with Tehran to accepting a pragmatic accommodation with the Islamic Republic's de facto control.

A new regional calculus

The change is not born of ideology but of necessity. With the US reducing its naval presence in the region and Iran's missile and drone capabilities rendering any forced passage costly, the Gulf states have concluded that resistance is no longer viable. Saudi Arabia and the UAE, in particular, have begun restructuring their energy export routes and storage facilities to account for a Hormuz that may remain under Iranian sway for the foreseeable future.

Analysts note that the shift reflects a broader recognition: Iran's geographic position makes it the gatekeeper of the Gulf's economic lifeline. No amount of international pressure has altered that reality.

Oman's quiet diplomacy

Oman has emerged as the unlikely intermediary in this standoff. Reports indicate that Iran is nearing a shipping deal with Muscat, which has long maintained cordial ties with Tehran even as other Gulf states kept their distance. The proposed arrangement would see Omani ports serve as a transshipment hub, allowing some Gulf oil to bypass the strait entirely.

Details remain sketchy, but the deal signals a template for coexistence. Iran extracts economic benefits and recognition of its role; Gulf states secure a measure of export continuity. The United States, meanwhile, finds itself increasingly sidelined in a region it once dominated.

Iran's conditions and the impasse

Tehran has made clear that reopening the strait fully is contingent on US concessions. These demands reportedly include the unfreezing of Iranian assets, guarantees against further sanctions, and a commitment to non-interference in the region. Washington has so far balked, and the war talks between the two sides have hit a fresh impasse.

In the meantime, new attacks on shipping have been reported in the Gulf waters. The incidents, attributed by some to Iranian-aligned forces, underscore the risks that persist as long as the diplomatic channel remains blocked. Insurance premiums for tankers transiting the region have soared, and several major shipping lines have rerouted cargoes around Africa, adding weeks to delivery times.

Energy markets on edge

The standoff has injected volatility into global energy markets. Crude prices have swung sharply on each development, and Asian economies, heavily dependent on Gulf oil, are watching nervously. India, which imports a significant share of its crude from the region, has begun diversifying supply sources but remains exposed to any prolonged disruption.

For the Gulf states, the calculus is stark. They can either fight a losing battle against a neighbour with every advantage in a maritime choke point, or they can adapt to a new normal where Iran controls the strait and the terms of passage. Increasingly, they are choosing the latter.

The coming months will test whether this accommodation can hold. If Oman brokers a shipping deal with Iran, it could set a precedent for other Gulf states to follow. But without a broader US-Iran understanding, the strait will remain a flashpoint, and the risk of miscalculation will persist.
