What Motilal Oswal’s 40-stock “buy” list really means

Motilal Oswal has named 40 stocks to consider during the Q2 earnings season, including well-known names such as Reliance Industries Limited (RIL), ICICI Bank, and Infosys. Lists like this typically reflect analyst conviction around earnings visibility, valuation comfort, and expected operating performance—yet they are not the same as an execution plan for every investor.

As a buyer’s guide approach, the key is to treat the list as a filter for research, not a substitute for it. The practical question for investors is whether the expected earnings trajectory and balance-sheet resilience you’re buying into still holds after results, management commentary, and guidance revisions.

Q2 is also when “story stocks” can move sharply on narrative changes. That means the real value in the shortlist is coverage: it nudges investors toward companies where catalysts (pricing, volumes, cost actions, credit growth, deal wins, or capex execution) are likely to be discussed with enough specificity to update expectations quickly.

How to use the list during earnings season (a checklist)

Even when a brokerage recommends “buy,” the buyer implication is different for each business model—banks react to credit costs and NIM stability, IT services to deal pipelines and utilisation, consumer and industrials to margin and demand signals. Before you allocate, run a consistent, outcome-based checklist across the names you care about.

1) Earnings quality: Look beyond headline profit. Track operating cash flow, working-capital swings, and whether margins are sustained rather than temporary.

2) Guidance and commentary: Management tone matters—especially on demand visibility, pricing discipline, and cost control. If guidance softens, brokerage convictions often take time to re-rate.

3) Balance-sheet and risk: For banks and NBFC-linked ecosystems, credit cost normalisation and asset-quality trends matter more than one quarter’s growth. For manufacturing and capex-heavy businesses, watch debt maturity and capex payback credibility.

4) Valuation sanity check: A “buy” can still be a bad trade if multiples have expanded faster than earnings estimates. Compare valuation to your own base-case earnings trajectory.

Why these names often appear in “buy” lists

Big, liquid companies frequently feature in brokerage shortlists because they offer multiple, monitorable levers during earnings season. RIL, for instance, typically draws attention to refined-product and chemicals cycles, energy transition capex, and cash generation. ICICI Bank’s inclusion reflects how closely investors track credit growth, NIM direction, and asset-quality durability.

Infosys and other large IT services firms usually matter because quarterly results can update the market on deal momentum, utilisation, and margin management. For large caps, the buyer implication is that while upside may be incremental, downside can also be easier to model—if guidance and margins hold, markets often stabilise quickly after earnings.

That stability is not guaranteed for smaller or mid-cap names on the list. In such cases, the “buy” thesis can be more sensitive to execution, working-capital swings, and financing assumptions—so the checklist above becomes even more important.

Real-world pros and cons for investors using a 40-stock shortlist

  • Pros: Consolidates analyst conviction into a starting universe, reducing time spent finding candidates before earnings.
  • Pros: Encourages a disciplined earnings checklist (guidance, margins, cash flow, balance-sheet risk) rather than headline chasing.
  • Pros: Helps you diversify across sectors represented in a single curated view.
  • Cons: “Buy” lists can become stale quickly if macro conditions shift or companies change guidance post-results.
  • Cons: High-conviction names may be crowded trades; near-term volatility can rise around earnings even if fundamentals are intact.
  • Cons: Without a valuation sanity check, investors may overpay for expected improvements that are already priced in.

Investor playbook: turn the list into a decision, not a habit

If you’re using Motilal Oswal’s 40-stock coverage, set rules before the earnings calendar turns chaotic. Decide which stocks you will verify against your base-case assumptions (not just brokerage optimism), and define what would invalidate your thesis after results.

For example, if you’re considering a stock because you expect margin stability, quantify what margin range would confirm or break that expectation. If you’re considering a bank, map your decision to credit cost and asset-quality direction, not just loan growth. This turns the list into actionable diligence—and reduces the risk of reacting to price moves without updating your reasoning.

Motilal Oswal’s inclusion of RIL, ICICI Bank, Infosys, and others can provide a strong research shortlist for Q2 earnings. The buyer’s edge comes from how you use it: measure what changes in the quarter, validate durability, and size your exposure according to risk, not just recommendation headlines.
