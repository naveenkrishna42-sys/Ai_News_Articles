A 25-Hour Window, and Why the Clock Is the Story

Nepal's Prime Minister Balendra Shah arrives in New Delhi on November 22 for a state visit that wraps up in roughly 25 hours. The Ministry of External Affairs has confirmed the November 22-23 dates, with delegation-level talks between Shah and Prime Minister Narendra Modi scheduled for November 23 before the Nepali leader departs.

For a first trip abroad, the compressed schedule is a deliberate signal. A 25-hour visit leaves no room for the ceremonial circuit of Banaras, Bodh Gaya or an address to a business chamber. It is a working stop designed to produce a joint statement, a short list of signed agreements, and a clear read on how Kathmandu's new government intends to deal with its largest trading partner.

Shah built his public profile as Kathmandu's mayor and, before politics, as a rap artist. His mandate rests on anti-corruption rhetoric and municipal service delivery rather than foreign policy experience. Delhi is therefore the first real test of whether that domestic style converts into negotiated outcomes.

The Agenda: Power, Pipelines and the $2-Billion Question

Electricity trade and the petroleum link

Hydropower is the most concrete deliverable on the table. Nepal runs a monsoon surplus and India needs evening-peak power, which is why the long-term India-Nepal power trade framework agreed in January 2024 - targeting 10,000 MW over a decade - is the commercial backbone of this relationship. Expect discussions on expediting new transmission corridors and on whether more Nepali projects get approval to sell into Indian exchanges.

On hydrocarbons, the Motihari-Amlekhgunj pipeline has carried fuel into Nepal since 2019 and cut truck-borne diesel traffic on the East-West Highway. The extension toward Chitwan is the practical item officials will push, since it directly affects landed fuel costs for Nepali consumers and transporters.

Gorkha recruitment and the 1947 tripartite arrangement

Recruitment of Gorkha soldiers into the Indian Army is governed by the 1947 India-Nepal-UK tripartite agreement. Nepal paused recruitment after India introduced the Agnipath scheme in 2022, and resumption requires a revised understanding on terms of service and pensions. This is a politically sensitive file in Kathmandu, and any movement will be read closely by ex-servicemen's associations.

Boundary, connectivity and the trade gap

The Kalapani-Lipulekh-Limpiyadhura and Susta boundary questions remain unresolved, and the Eminent Persons' Group report submitted in 2018 has never been made public. Neither side expects a settlement in 25 hours. What matters is whether the joint statement retains or softens earlier language. Nepal's structural trade deficit with India - India supplies the bulk of its imports, from petroleum to machinery - will also shape the conversation on transit rights and rail connectivity.

What It Means in Practical Terms

For Nepali households, the tangible outcomes are fuel pricing and load-shedding, not communiques. A faster pipeline extension lowers transport costs on diesel and petrol; more power export approvals bring revenue to the Nepal Electricity Authority and ease pressure on state finances.

For Indian businesses operating in Nepal - telecom, cement, hydropower EPC, banking - the signal to watch is whether the reported $2-billion package is signed as a formal instrument or merely "agreed in principle." The distinction determines whether it becomes disbursed capital or a headline.

For travellers and students, nothing changes. Movement between the two countries continues under the 1950 Treaty of Peace and Friendship, and no visa or permit revision is on the table.

Key Takeaways

  • A deliberately short visit signals a transactional, deliverables-first approach from Kathmandu.
  • Energy - both hydropower exports and the pipeline extension - is the most likely area for signed outcomes.
  • Gorkha recruitment resumption needs a revised understanding on Agnipath terms, not just political goodwill.
  • The boundary dispute will not be resolved; the wording of the joint statement is the real indicator.
  • Treat the $2-billion figure as unconfirmed until a formal agreement is tabled.

What to Watch After November 23

Three signals will define the visit's value. First, whether the joint statement names Kalapani and Susta explicitly or reverts to generic language on "outstanding boundary issues." Second, whether energy and pipeline agreements are signed rather than referred to a joint technical committee. Third, whether Shah commits to a reciprocal visit framework that keeps ministerial-level dialogue running between summits. A 25-hour trip can still produce a durable calendar - that, more than any single cheque, is the measure of success.
