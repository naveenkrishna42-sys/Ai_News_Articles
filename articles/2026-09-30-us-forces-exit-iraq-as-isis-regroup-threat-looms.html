US Forces Exit Iraq as ISIS Regroup Threat Looms

✍️ By TIVRA News Editorial Team 📅 2026-09-30 📂 World Original source ↗
Key points
  • The exit closes a 23-year US military chapter that began with the 2003 invasion
  • Intelligence warnings point to Islamic State cells regrouping in Anbar, Diyala and Kirkuk provinces
  • Iran-aligned armed groups stand to absorb the security vacuum faster than Iraqi state forces
  • Iraq's political timeline and militia accountability will decide whether the power shift is reversible

What the withdrawal actually changes

United States-led forces are leaving Iraq 23 years after the 2003 invasion, ending a deployment that survived a 2011 drawdown, the 2014 war against the Islamic State, and years of militia rocket fire on bases housing American troops. Reporting by NDTV, The Times of Israel, Telegraph India and The Washington Post converges on one point: the departure is a handover, not a collapse, and its consequences depend almost entirely on what Baghdad can hold together after the last coalition vehicles cross the border.

The immediate question is not whether ISIS reclaims cities. Its territorial caliphate was dismantled between December 2017 and March 2019. The operative risk is the insurgency it never stopped running — sleeper cells, extortion networks and kidnapping squads operating out of the Hamrin mountain belt and the desert corridors between Anbar, Diyala and Kirkuk. Those are precisely the areas where Iraqi counter-terrorism forces have historically depended on coalition air surveillance, ISR drones and targeting intelligence.

The ISIS resurgence calculus

Insurgencies recover through three inputs: ungoverned space, a grievance narrative and a funding stream. Iraq still supplies the third in abundance — ISIS levies protection money from farmers, fuel smugglers and mobile networks across the north. What the coalition exit removes is the persistent overwatch that made those transactions dangerous. Iraqi forces can raid; they have limited capacity to track, pattern and sustain pressure over months.

The second input is political. Sunni communities in Nineveh and Anbar that aligned with the state against ISIS have repeatedly complained that promised reconstruction and de-Ba'athification reforms stalled. An insurgency rebranding itself as Sunni resistance finds fertile ground wherever that neglect persists.

Iran's proxies and the balance of power

The other side of the ledger is the Popular Mobilization Forces and its Iran-aligned factions — Kataib Hezbollah, Asa'ib Ahl al-Haq and the Iraqi wing of Hezbollah. These groups were legitimised into Iraq's security architecture after 2014, receive state salaries, and now inherit not just physical bases vacated by coalition troops but political space. The Washington Post's framing of a shifted balance is economically literal: salaries, weapons pipelines and border crossing rents accrue to groups whose loyalty runs to Tehran before Baghdad.

  • Strategic gain: End of a costly, open-ended deployment and reduced exposure of US personnel to militia rocket and drone attacks.
  • Strategic gain: Formal recognition of Iraqi sovereignty, removing a grievance ISIS and Iranian-aligned politicians both exploit.
  • Risk: Loss of ISR and targeting support precisely where ISIS cells are reconstituting leadership.
  • Risk: Militias face no credible domestic disarmament mechanism, strengthening Tehran's leverage over Iraqi politics.
  • Risk: A weakened central government in Baghdad historically correlates with cross-border spillover into Syria's Euphrates valley.
  • Watch item: Whether the promised bilateral US-Iraq advisory arrangement survives the withdrawal intact or degrades into a purely diplomatic mission.

What determines the outcome

Three variables decide whether this reads as an orderly transition or a security vacuum. First, whether Iraqi forces sustain coalition-funded air support and intelligence pipelines. Second, whether Baghdad moves to subordinate the Popular Mobilization Forces to formal command and cut their independent smuggling revenue. Third, whether reconstruction in Sunni provinces resumes at scale before ISIS recruitment does. Each is a policy choice, not a capability gap — and none is yet settled.
