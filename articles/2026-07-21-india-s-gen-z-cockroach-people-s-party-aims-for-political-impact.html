India's Gen Z 'Cockroach People's Party' aims for political impact

📅 2026-07-21 📂 Education Original source ↗
Key points
  • Started as an internet joke, the 'Cockroach People's Party' now has a growing Gen Z following.
  • The movement uses humor and memes to criticize corruption and inaction on climate change.
  • It is not a registered political party but functions as a decentralized advocacy group online.

What began as a meme on Indian social media has quietly turned into something more tangible. The 'Cockroach People's Party,' a name deliberately absurd, now claims thousands of Gen Z followers who say they are done waiting for older generations to fix the country's problems.

The group's origin is pure internet irony. A few college students in Delhi started a WhatsApp group last year, calling themselves cockroaches because, as one member put it, 'We survive everything and no one can get rid of us.' The joke spread. Soon, there were Instagram pages, Twitter handles, and even a crude logo.

From satire to seriousness

But the tone shifted during the 2025 Delhi heatwave, when temperatures crossed 49 degrees Celsius for two weeks. 'We were joking about politics, but the heat was real,' said a 20-year-old coordinator who helps manage the group's online presence. 'That's when we started posting actual demands.'

The party's core issues are straightforward: stricter air pollution laws, free public transport for students, and a ban on single-use plastics by 2030. None of these are original, but the delivery is. Posts mix policy points with memes about corrupt politicians and viral dance trends.

How it works

The 'Cockroach People's Party' is not a registered political entity. It has no office, no bank account, and no official leadership. Decisions are made through polls on Telegram channels, where membership has crossed 40,000 users, most of them between 16 and 25 years old.

Activists say the lack of hierarchy is deliberate. 'Every political party in India has a dynastic leader,' said a 22-year-old from Bengaluru who helps design campaign posters. 'We don't want one. We want to be impossible to co-opt.' Critics, however, question whether such a loose structure can achieve anything concrete.

Reactions and challenges

Older political analysts have dismissed the movement as a fad. One former bureaucrat called it 'keyboard activism' in a recent interview. But the group's ability to mobilize attention is real. Last month, their #SurviveNotThrive hashtag trended nationally for hours after they posted a mock manifesto comparing air quality data to fast food menus.

There have also been practical hurdles. The group has struggled to get permits for small street protests in Mumbai and Kolkata. Some members have faced online harassment from supporters of established parties who accuse them of being 'foreign agents.' The coordinators say they have no foreign funding and operate on small donations from members, typically under 500 rupees each.

What lies ahead

The group's immediate plan is to field a symbolic candidate in the next Delhi municipal elections, under the name 'Cockroach People's Party.' The candidate will likely be a 19-year-old college dropout who works at a call centre. They know they won't win. But they say that's not the point.

For now, the movement is watching to see if its online energy can translate into offline votes. The next few months will reveal whether India's Gen Z is ready to turn a joke into a real voice.
