India's Chairmanship Tested at the 18th BRICS Summit

India hosted the 18th BRICS Summit and closed it with the New Delhi Declaration, a negotiated document that reflects the grouping's widening agenda: critical minerals, payment systems, technology sovereignty and reform of multilateral institutions. Holding the chair meant India had to convert a fractured room into a single text, and that is the central achievement of the summit.

The political aftermath has been equally sharp. Opposition figures, including Congress' Pawan Khera, invoked the 2012 BRICS Summit to question whether Prime Minister Narendra Modi's relatively low public profile at this edition signalled influence or reticence. The Telegraph framed it bluntly: India had the chair but whose voice carried?

What the New Delhi Declaration Actually Commits To

The declaration's substance matters more than its ceremony. Critical minerals moved to the centre of the BRICS economic agenda, with member states acknowledging that clean energy, semiconductors and defence manufacturing all depend on concentrated supply chains. India's own push for mineral security—lithium, cobalt, rare earths—now has a multilateral hook.

Technology weaponisation also entered the text. New Delhi flagged the risk of export controls, sanctions regimes and unilateral tech restrictions being used as geopolitical instruments. For Indian firms buying cloud infrastructure, advanced chips or industrial software, that language is a signal that alternative sourcing and domestic capacity will be prioritised.

Buyer and Industry Implications

For Indian manufacturers, the declaration's mineral language supports the National Critical Mineral Mission and bilateral sourcing deals already under negotiation with resource-rich partners. Buyers of battery components, solar modules and specialty alloys should expect policy support for domestic processing rather than a sudden drop in import prices.

On technology, the anti-weaponisation language does not remove export controls imposed by Western suppliers. It does, however, strengthen the commercial case for Indian alternatives in cloud, cybersecurity and semiconductor packaging. Procurement teams should model dual-sourcing scenarios over a 24 to 36 month horizon, not a quarterly one.

Key Real-World Highlights

  • Critical minerals are now a formal BRICS economic priority, aligning with India's domestic mission.
  • Technology weaponisation entered the negotiated text, validating India's stated concerns.
  • Payment and settlement diversification remains a workstream, not a delivered system.
  • Domestic political scrutiny of India's chairmanship will continue through the news cycle.

Pros and Cons of the Outcome

  • Pro: India demonstrated it can herd divergent members—Russia, China, Brazil, South Africa and new entrants—into one declaration.
  • Pro: Mineral and tech language gives Indian industry a multilateral reference point in bilateral negotiations.
  • Con: Declarations are non-binding; delivery depends on domestic policy and bilateral deals.
  • Con: Visibility questions around the Prime Minister's engagement have handed ammunition to the Opposition.
  • Con: No concrete pricing or tariff relief for buyers emerged from the summit.

The Road Ahead

The New Delhi Declaration will be judged by what India does with it. Critical mineral processing capacity, payment interoperability and technology self-reliance are the three metrics that matter. For buyers and industry, the summit is a directional signal, not an instant cost benefit.

India's chairmanship has ended with a consensus text in hand. Whether that consensus translates into leverage depends on the follow-through over the next two years.
