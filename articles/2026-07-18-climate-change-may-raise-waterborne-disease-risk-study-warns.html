Climate Change May Raise Waterborne Disease Risk, Study Warns

📅 2026-07-18 📂 Health Original source ↗
Key points
  • A new study warns that climate change could increase waterborne disease risks by altering rainfall, temperature, and water quality.
  • Changing weather patterns make seasonal threats like dengue and flu less predictable across India.
  • Progress in reducing waterborne illnesses faces new challenges from extreme weather events.
  • The study underscores the need for adaptive public health strategies and stronger water infrastructure.

A new study warns that climate change could significantly increase the risk of waterborne diseases, threatening decades of progress in public health. As weather patterns shift across the globe, scientists say the impact on water quality and pathogen survival is becoming harder to ignore.

In India, the findings resonate strongly. The country has long battled illnesses like cholera, typhoid, and hepatitis A, which spread through contaminated water. Now, researchers suggest that rising temperatures and erratic rainfall could make these diseases more common in areas that were previously less vulnerable.

Changing Seasons, Changing Risks

Climate change is shaking up waterborne disease risks, warn scientists. Warmer temperatures can help bacteria and viruses survive longer in water sources. Heavy rains and floods, which are becoming more frequent, often overwhelm drainage systems and contaminate drinking water supplies.

The study, reported by multiple outlets including The Indian Practitioner, BusinessLine, and The Times of India, points out that traditional seasonal patterns for diseases like dengue and flu are no longer reliable. These illnesses, once confined to specific months, are now appearing year-round in many parts of India.

Progress Under Threat

India has made notable gains in reducing waterborne diseases over the past few decades. Better sanitation, vaccination drives, and improved access to clean drinking water have cut infection rates. But climate change could undermine these achievements, the study finds.

Extreme weather events, such as cyclones and unseasonal downpours, damage water infrastructure. In rural areas, where many still rely on wells and ponds, contaminated runoff can cause sudden outbreaks. Urban centres are not immune either, as flooded streets often mix sewage with stormwater.

Dengue and Flu: No Longer Seasonal

NDTV reports that dengue and flu are no longer just seasonal threats in India. The changing climate is creating conditions where mosquitoes, which carry dengue, can breed for longer periods. Similarly, flu viruses may survive better in altered humidity levels.

Health officials are now grappling with a more complex disease calendar. Hospitals that once prepared for dengue only after the monsoon now see cases throughout the year. This puts additional strain on an already burdened healthcare system.

A Wider Impact on Health

The study also touches on a related concern: antimicrobial resistance, or AMR. As waterborne diseases become more common, the use of antibiotics may rise. This could accelerate the development of drug-resistant bacteria, making infections harder to treat.

24-7 Press Release Newswire notes that climate change may be reshaping the fight against AMR in animal diseases as well. Livestock, often treated with antibiotics, can become reservoirs for resistant pathogens that spread to humans through water or food.

Officials have not yet confirmed specific policy changes in response to the study. However, public health experts are calling for more investment in climate-resilient water systems and better disease surveillance networks.

What happens next will depend on how quickly governments and communities adapt. The window to protect past gains is narrowing, and the coming years will test how well India can prepare for a warmer, wetter, and more unpredictable future.
