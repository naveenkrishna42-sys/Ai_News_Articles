9-Inch Stick Insect Can Fly, Clone Itself, and Fight Back

Key points
  • The stick insect can grow up to 9 inches in length.
  • It is capable of flight, a rare trait among stick insects.
  • Females can reproduce through parthenogenesis, cloning themselves.
  • The insect uses spines on its legs to fight back against predators.

Discovery of a Remarkable Species

Scientists have documented a stick insect species in India that defies common expectations. The insect, which can reach up to 9 inches in length, possesses abilities rarely seen in the insect world. It can fly, reproduce without a mate, and actively defend itself.

Researchers observed the creature in forested regions, noting its unusual behaviour. The findings, shared by experts, highlight a creature that challenges typical notions of stick insects as slow, passive creatures.

Flight and Cloning Capabilities

Most stick insects are known for their camouflage and limited movement. This species, however, has fully developed wings that allow it to fly short distances. Flight helps it escape predators and find new habitats.

Even more striking is its reproductive strategy. Females can produce offspring through parthenogenesis, a form of asexual reproduction. This means a single female can clone herself, creating genetically identical young without a male. This ability ensures the species can thrive even in isolated environments.

Defensive Mechanisms

The insect does not rely solely on camouflage. It has evolved sharp spines on its legs, which it uses to strike at attackers. When threatened, it can deliver a painful jab, deterring birds, lizards, and other predators.

This active defence is rare among stick insects. Most rely on blending in or playing dead. The combination of flight, cloning, and fighting makes this species a standout in evolutionary terms.

Habitat and Behaviour

Experts found the insect in dense vegetation, where its green-brown body provides excellent camouflage. It is most active at night, feeding on leaves. During the day, it remains still, mimicking twigs.

Males, when present, are smaller and less common. The species appears to have a flexible social structure, with females often dominating populations due to their cloning ability.

Implications for Science

The discovery offers new insights into insect evolution. The ability to clone itself allows rapid population growth, while flight enables dispersal. Defensive spines add another layer of survival strategy.

Researchers are now studying the insect's genetics to understand how these traits evolved. The findings could inform broader studies on adaptation and reproduction in insects.

What happens next? Field studies will continue to map the insect's range and behaviour. Scientists also plan to investigate whether similar species exist in other parts of India.
