Centre Softens FCRA Bill Stance After Minority Groups' Appeals

📅 2026-08-12 📂 Top Stories Original source ↗
Key points
  • FCRA Amendment Bill, 2026 referred to a Joint Parliamentary Committee
  • Move follows appeals by minority groups and opposition uproar
  • Union Minister Kiren Rijiju defends bill, says India not a 'banana republic'

Government Defers to Parliamentary Scrutiny

In a notable shift, the Centre has softened its position on the Foreign Contribution (Regulation) Amendment Bill, 2026, choosing to refer the contentious legislation to a Joint Parliamentary Committee (JPC). The decision comes after sustained appeals by minority groups and a vocal opposition campaign demanding the bill's complete withdrawal.

The move signals a tactical retreat by the government, which had earlier pushed for swift passage of the bill in Parliament. By sending it to a JPC, the government has effectively paused the legislative process, allowing for deeper scrutiny and wider consultation.

Opposition and Minority Groups Cry Foul

Opposition parties have been united in their criticism, arguing that the bill disproportionately targets minority communities. They have accused the government of using the FCRA framework to muzzle dissent and curb the operations of non-governmental organisations working on minority rights.

Minority groups, in their appeals to the government, contended that the proposed amendments would severely restrict their access to foreign funds, hampering their ability to run educational, health, and welfare programmes. The government's decision to refer the bill to a panel is being viewed as a direct response to these appeals.

Rijiju's Defence: Keeping an Eye on Foreign Funds

Union Minister Kiren Rijiju, however, has remained steadfast in defending the bill's intent. In a recent statement, he asserted that India is "not a banana republic" and stressed the importance of the FCRA in keeping a watchful eye on foreign funding. His comments were aimed at countering the narrative that the bill is discriminatory.

Rijiju's remarks underline the government's position that the legislation is necessary to ensure transparency and national security. He argued that unchecked foreign contributions could undermine the country's sovereignty, a stance that has drawn sharp rebuke from civil society and opposition ranks.

What the Bill Proposes

While the specifics of the 2026 amendment bill have not been fully disclosed, reports suggest it aims to tighten the existing FCRA framework. The original FCRA, enacted in 2010 and amended in 2020, regulates the acceptance and utilisation of foreign contributions by individuals and organisations in India.

The proposed amendments are believed to include stricter reporting requirements, enhanced powers for authorities to suspend registrations, and possibly categorising certain minority-run NGOs as high-risk. Critics fear these provisions could be misused to silence legitimate voices.

JPC: A Chance for Course Correction?

The referral to the JPC is seen as an opportunity for stakeholders to present their concerns directly to lawmakers. The committee will likely hear from minority groups, NGOs, legal experts, and government officials, potentially leading to modifications in the bill's language or scope.

However, the JPC process is often lengthy, and there is no guarantee that the final version will address all objections. The opposition has already indicated it will continue its push for withdrawal, even as the panel begins its work.

What to Watch Next

All eyes will now be on the JPC's composition and the timeline for its report. The government's flexibility on this bill may be tested further as the committee deliberates, and any compromise will be closely watched by both minority groups and the opposition.

The coming months will reveal whether this softening of stance is a genuine course correction or merely a tactical pause in a larger legislative push.
