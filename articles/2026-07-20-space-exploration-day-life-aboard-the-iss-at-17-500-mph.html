Space Exploration Day: Life Aboard the ISS at 17,500 mph

📅 2026-07-20 📂 Science Original source ↗
Key points
  • Astronauts aboard the ISS witness 16 sunrises and sunsets every 24 hours.
  • Many astronauts describe the Earth's atmosphere as a thin, fragile blue line from orbit.
  • The ISS orbits Earth at roughly 17,500 mph, completing a full lap every 90 minutes.

The View That Changes Everything

From the International Space Station, the world looks fundamentally different. Astronauts orbiting 400 kilometres above the Earth watch the sun rise and set not once, but 16 times a day.

Many of them say the hardest thing to describe back home is how thin the atmosphere appears. From that vantage point, it is not a vast sky — it is a bright blue line, almost delicate enough to rub out with a thumb. That sight leaves a lasting impression on everyone who sees it.

Living in a High-Speed Lab

The ISS travels at roughly 17,500 miles per hour. That speed means it completes a full orbit around the Earth every 90 minutes. For the astronauts on board, day and night cycle through in less time than a movie.

Life inside the station is a carefully choreographed routine. Crew members wake up, exercise for two hours daily to fight muscle loss, conduct experiments, and maintain the station's systems. Eating, sleeping, and even using the bathroom require adjustments in microgravity.

What the Day Looks Like

A typical day starts with a wake-up call from mission control. Breakfast is often rehydrated food or thermostabilised pouches. Coffee is sipped from a sealed bag through a straw. Lunch and dinner follow the same pattern.

Most of the workday is spent on science. The ISS is a floating laboratory where researchers study everything from fluid dynamics to human biology. The results have direct applications for life on Earth and for future deep-space missions.

Communicating with Earth

Astronauts stay connected with their families through video calls and email. But the 90-minute orbit cycle means they lose contact with ground stations for stretches of time. Messages queue up until the station passes over a relay satellite.

Many crew members say the hardest part of the mission is not the danger or the isolation. It is the inability to share the view in real time. Photographs and videos do not capture the visceral experience of seeing the planet rotate below.

The Fragile Blue Line

That thin band of atmosphere is the only thing separating humans from the vacuum of space. Astronauts often report a heightened sense of Earth's vulnerability after seeing it firsthand. The line looks so narrow that any major disruption — a volcanic eruption, a nuclear event — could scar it visibly.

This perspective has been described by many as a profound shift in worldview. It is a reminder that the planet's resources are finite, and its protective blanket is remarkably thin.

As Space Exploration Day highlights humanity's achievements in orbit, the ISS continues to circle the Earth, its crew witnessing 16 sunrises and 16 sunsets every single day. The station's mission remains focused on science and international cooperation. What comes next — a permanent presence on the Moon or a journey to Mars — will depend on the lessons learned from the men and women living on this high-speed laboratory.
