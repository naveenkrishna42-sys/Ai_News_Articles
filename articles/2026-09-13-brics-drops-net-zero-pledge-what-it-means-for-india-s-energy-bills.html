BRICS Drops 'Net Zero' Pledge: What It Means for India's Energy Bills

✍️ By TIVRA News Editorial Team 📅 2026-09-13 📂 India Original source ↗
Key points
  • The BRICS declaration reaffirms the Paris Agreement but deliberately avoids the phrase 'net zero', signalling a shift from Western decarbonisation templates.
  • The bloc formally opposed unilateral trade measures like the EU's Carbon Border Adjustment Mechanism (CBAM), which could have raised costs for Indian steel, aluminium and cement exporters.
  • India's climate strategy now leans on adaptation finance and disaster resilience rather than absolute emission-cut deadlines, with direct implications for energy pricing and industrial competitiveness.

A Deliberate Omission, Not an Accident

The latest BRICS declaration has done something Western climate diplomacy rarely does: it has stayed silent on "net zero." The bloc reaffirmed its commitment to the aims of the Paris Agreement, but pointedly refused to adopt the net-zero framing that has dominated G20 and COP language for the past six years. That omission is the story.

For Indian buyers and businesses, this is not abstract geopolitics. It signals that the country's climate pathway will be defined by adaptation, energy access and industrial competitiveness rather than by absolute emission deadlines imported from Brussels or Washington.

Why the West's Climate Model Is Being Questioned

The Western climate template rests on two pillars: legally binding net-zero target years and carbon border tariffs. BRICS has now pushed back on both. The declaration explicitly opposes unilateral trade steps such as the European Union's Carbon Border Adjustment Mechanism (CBAM), which taxes imports based on embedded carbon.

Indian steel, aluminium, cement and fertiliser exporters face CBAM levies on shipments to Europe. Industry estimates suggest Indian exporters could pay anywhere between ₹25,000 crore and ₹40,000 crore annually once the mechanism fully phases in. By contesting CBAM at the BRICS level, India is seeking collective bargaining weight rather than fighting alone.

Adaptation Finance Takes Centre Stage

Climate finance, adaptation and disaster resilience featured prominently in the declaration. This reflects a hard reality: developing economies need capital to protect coastlines, farms and cities before they can afford deep decarbonisation. India's own adaptation spending runs into lakhs of crores, and the bloc is pressing developed nations to honour the $100 billion annual climate finance pledge that has repeatedly fallen short.

Real-World Buyer Implications

If BRICS pressure delays or dilutes CBAM enforcement, Indian manufacturers avoid a cost shock that would otherwise be passed to consumers through higher steel and cement prices. Construction costs, vehicle prices and appliance prices all carry embedded carbon costs.

On the energy side, the omission of net zero gives India room to expand coal capacity as a baseload backup while scaling solar and wind. That reduces the risk of grid instability and price spikes, but it also delays the retirement of older thermal plants.

What to Watch

  • Pro: Collective BRICS pushback gives India leverage against unilateral carbon tariffs.
  • Pro: Adaptation finance focus aligns with India's flood, heatwave and cyclone resilience needs.
  • Pro: Flexibility on net-zero timelines protects energy affordability for households and MSMEs.
  • Con: Absence of firm emission targets may slow green investment and delay clean-tech price declines.
  • Con: Trade friction with the EU could still escalate if CBAM proceeds unchanged.
  • Con: Weak language on fossil-fuel phase-out risks locking in higher long-term transition costs.

The Bottom Line for Indian Consumers

This declaration is a negotiating position, not a final policy. It signals that India will prioritise growth, jobs and energy security while meeting Paris commitments on its own terms. For buyers, the near-term effect is stability in energy and construction costs. The long-term effect depends on whether BRICS can convert its collective weight into actual concessions from the EU and other Western blocs.
