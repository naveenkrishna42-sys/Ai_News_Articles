Pluto's Heart Shows Signs of Recent Liquid Nitrogen Flow, New Study Finds

📅 2026-08-09 📂 Science Original source ↗
Key points
  • Analysis of New Horizons images suggests liquid nitrogen may have flowed on Pluto's surface recently.
  • The discovery focuses on Sputnik Planitia, the icy plain forming Pluto's heart-shaped feature.
  • Findings imply Pluto retains internal heat and is more geologically active than previously thought.
  • Researchers studied images from the 2015 flyby, with new modeling supporting the liquid flow hypothesis.

Nearly a decade after NASA's New Horizons spacecraft zipped past Pluto, scientists are still uncovering surprises from the data it sent home. The latest analysis suggests that the dwarf planet's famous heart-shaped glacier may not be as frozen in time as once believed. Images captured during the 2015 flyby now show signs of recently flowing liquid nitrogen on the surface.

The study, reported by outlets including The Brighter Side of News and Labroots, points to features on Sputnik Planitia—the vast nitrogen-ice plain that forms the left lobe of Pluto's heart. Researchers say the patterns observed there are best explained by the movement of liquid nitrogen, possibly in the geologically recent past.

Frozen Heart, Flowing Veins

Pluto's surface temperature hovers around minus 230 degrees Celsius, cold enough to keep nitrogen locked in solid form. Yet the new analysis suggests that under certain conditions, this ice can melt and flow, carving channels and creating smooth deposits across the terrain.

Scientists have long debated whether Pluto is geologically alive or a relic of the early solar system. This latest evidence tilts the balance toward activity. The flowing nitrogen would require a source of heat from beneath the surface, hinting at a warmer interior than previously assumed.

The researchers used images from the New Horizons spacecraft, which remains the only mission to have visited Pluto. Although the probe flew past in 2015, its data continues to yield fresh insights. The team applied new modeling techniques to reinterpret the images, revealing surface features consistent with liquid nitrogen flow.

What the Images Show

Close examination of Sputnik Planitia reveals lobate structures—tongue-like extensions of ice that resemble flow features seen on Earth's glaciers. These formations suggest that nitrogen ice may have oozed out and spread across the plain, much like a slow-motion river on a frigid world.

Other clues include pits and troughs that appear to have been carved by moving liquid. The patterns are not random; they align with the direction of gravitational pull, indicating a controlled flow rather than chaotic disruption.

Researchers also noted that these features appear relatively fresh, lacking the cratering that would accumulate over millions of years. This suggests the flows occurred recently in geological terms—perhaps within the last few million years, or even more recently.

Implications for Planetary Science

If confirmed, these findings would make Pluto one of the few places in the outer solar system with evidence of recent liquid activity. This would place it alongside other dynamic worlds like Saturn's moon Titan and Jupiter's Europa, where subsurface processes drive surface changes.

The discovery also raises questions about the internal structure of dwarf planets. Pluto's radioactive heat, combined with residual warmth from its formation, could be enough to melt nitrogen in localized pockets. Over time, this liquid could migrate to the surface and refreeze, creating the observed flow patterns.

Scientists caution that the evidence is indirect, based on surface morphology rather than direct observation of liquid. However, the consistency of the features with fluid dynamics strengthens the case. Future missions to the Kuiper Belt may provide more definitive answers.

What's Next for Pluto Research

The New Horizons data set remains a treasure trove for planetary scientists. As analytical techniques improve, more secrets may emerge from the dwarf planet's icy surface.

No new mission to Pluto is currently approved, but proposals for orbiters or landers have been floated in scientific circles. Such missions could directly sample the surface and confirm whether liquid nitrogen still flows beneath the frozen crust.

For now, the images from 2015 continue to reshape our understanding of Pluto. What was once seen as a static, frozen world is increasingly looking like a dynamic place with a pulse of its own. The next steps will involve further modeling and perhaps a closer look from future explorers.
