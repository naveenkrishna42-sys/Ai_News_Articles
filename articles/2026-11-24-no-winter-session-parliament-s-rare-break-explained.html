No Winter Session? Parliament's Rare Break Explained

✍️ By TIVRA News Editorial Desk 📅 2026-11-24 📂 Top Stories Original source ↗
Key points
  • Article 85 requires no more than six months between sittings, but a Winter Session is convention, not constitutional obligation.
  • Reporting indicates the Winter Session has been skipped only four times since 1952, with 1975 and 2020 among the documented exceptions.
  • Because the Monsoon Session has not been prorogued, the government retains the option to reconvene the House without a fresh summoning order.
  • A cancelled Winter Session removes roughly 20–25 sitting days and compresses ordinance replacement and committee tabling into the Budget Session.

No Winter Session: What the Constitution Requires — and What It Does Not

Parliament's Winter Session, conventionally held between the last week of November and late December, has not been scheduled this year. The Monsoon Session has not been prorogued, which means the government has kept open the option of reconvening the House without issuing a fresh summoning order. More than 100 days have passed since the last sitting.

The Constitution does not mandate three sessions a year. Article 85(1) empowers the President to summon each House at such time and place as they think fit. The only hard floor sits in Article 85(2): six months cannot intervene between the last sitting of one session and the first sitting of the next. That makes the Winter Session a convention of parliamentary practice rather than a constitutional obligation.

The Three-Session Calendar and Its Four Documented Exceptions

The standard cycle runs through the Budget Session (late January to May), the Monsoon Session (July–August) and the Winter Session (November–December). Reporting on the current impasse notes that the Winter Session has been skipped only four times since the first Lok Sabha convened in 1952 — which is precisely why the omission is being read as politically deliberate rather than administrative.

Documented exceptions include 1975, when the Emergency was in force, and 2020, when the COVID-19 pandemic disrupted the calendar. Each instance coincided with a period in which the executive's legislative programme could advance without the sustained scrutiny of a full sitting calendar.
