Younger Behçet’s patients skip medication, study finds

📅 2026-07-31 📂 Health Original source ↗
Key points
  • Younger patients with Behçet’s syndrome show significantly lower medication adherence.
  • Non-adherence increases risk of disease flares and organ damage.
  • Researchers call for targeted interventions to improve adherence in this age group.

Age gap in adherence

A fresh analysis has flagged a worrying trend: younger people living with Behçet’s syndrome are far less likely to stick to their prescribed medication regimens compared to older patients. The findings, published in the European Medical Journal, underscore a critical gap in the management of this rare, chronic inflammatory disorder.

Behçet’s syndrome, which causes inflammation of blood vessels, can lead to painful mouth sores, skin lesions, and in severe cases, damage to the eyes, brain, or digestive tract. The disease follows a relapsing-remitting course, meaning consistent treatment is essential to keep flares at bay.

Why younger patients skip doses

The study did not single out a specific reason for the low adherence, but experts point to a mix of factors common in younger demographics. Busy lifestyles, forgetfulness, and a perception that the disease is under control when symptoms subside are frequently cited in adherence research.

There is also the burden of side effects. Many drugs used to treat Behçet’s—such as colchicine, corticosteroids, and immunosuppressants—come with unpleasant effects that can be harder to tolerate over the long haul.

“When you’re young and otherwise healthy, taking daily medication feels like a constant reminder of a chronic illness,” noted one rheumatologist familiar with the study, speaking on condition of anonymity. “The motivation to stay on therapy often dips when there are no immediate symptoms.”

Risks of non-adherence

The consequences of skipping medication in Behçet’s are not abstract. Poor adherence can trigger more frequent and severe flares, accelerate disease progression, and increase the likelihood of irreversible organ damage. For a condition that already carries a high burden of morbidity, this is a serious public health concern.

The study’s authors argue that clinicians should not assume younger patients are following their prescriptions. They recommend routine, non-judgmental conversations about adherence during every clinic visit, and suggest using technology—such as reminder apps or pill organisers—to support patients who struggle to stay on track.

What can be done

Patient education is central. Many younger patients may not fully grasp that Behçet’s requires lifelong management, even when they feel well. Clear communication about the long-term benefits of adherence, and the risks of interruption, can help shift attitudes.

Shared decision-making also matters. When patients feel involved in choosing their treatment plan, they are more likely to commit to it. Simplifying regimens—for instance, once-daily dosing where possible—can further reduce the burden.

The study adds to a growing body of evidence that age-specific approaches are needed in chronic disease care. What works for a 60-year-old may not resonate with a 25-year-old, and treatment plans must adapt accordingly.

Looking ahead

Researchers are now calling for larger, prospective studies to identify the most effective interventions for improving adherence in younger Behçet’s patients. In the meantime, doctors are urged to keep the lines of communication open—and to ask the simple question, “Are you actually taking your medicine?”—at every opportunity.
