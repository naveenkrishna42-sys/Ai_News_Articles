Married Man's Consensual Live-In Not A Crime: High Court

📅 2026-07-18 📂 Crime & Law Original source ↗
Key points
  • Rajasthan High Court quashes FIR against a married man in a consensual live-in relationship.
  • Court says consensual live-in is not an offence under Indian law unless it involves bigamy or cruelty.
  • Judgment clarifies that moral policing cannot be grounds for criminal prosecution.

The Rajasthan High Court has ruled that a married man entering into a consensual live-in relationship with another woman does not constitute a criminal offence. The judgment came while quashing an FIR lodged against a man under charges of cheating and criminal intimidation.

Justice Anoop Kumar Dhand observed that live-in relationships, even when one partner is married, are not illegal under Indian law unless they amount to bigamy or cruelty to the legal spouse. The court emphasised that consenting adults have the right to choose their living arrangements.

What The Court Said

“Mere consensual live-in relationship between two adults, even if one of them is married, does not attract the offence of cheating under Section 420 of IPC unless there is a clear intent to deceive from the beginning,” the bench noted.

The court also clarified that such relationships are a matter of personal choice and morality cannot be enforced through criminal law. “The state cannot intrude into the private lives of individuals unless there is a clear violation of law,” it added.

Background Of The Case

The case originated from a complaint filed by the woman’s father, who alleged that the man had promised to marry his daughter while already being married. The woman, however, told the court that she was aware of his marital status and had entered the relationship voluntarily.

She stated that she had been living with him for over two years and had no grievances. The court noted that the woman’s statement did not support the allegations of cheating or coercion.

Legal Precedents Cited

The High Court relied on earlier Supreme Court judgments that recognised live-in relationships as valid under the right to privacy and personal liberty. In several rulings, the top court has held that such arrangements are not unlawful.

The judgment also distinguished between a live-in relationship and the offence of bigamy. “For a charge of bigamy to stick, there must be proof of a valid marriage in the legal sense. Cohabitation alone does not prove marriage,” the judge explained.

Implications For Future Cases

Legal experts say this ruling could discourage frivolous complaints filed by family members against consenting adults. It reinforces the idea that criminal law should not be used to settle moral or personal disputes.

However, the court also warned that the protection does not extend to cases where the live-in relationship involves cruelty, harassment, or deception. “Each case must be examined on its facts,” the judgment said.

The case is expected to be cited in similar matters across the country. The court’s stance aligns with the growing judicial recognition of individual autonomy in personal relationships.
