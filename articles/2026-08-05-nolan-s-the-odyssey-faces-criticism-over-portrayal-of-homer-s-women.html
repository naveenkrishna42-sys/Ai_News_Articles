Nolan's 'The Odyssey' faces criticism over portrayal of Homer's women

📅 2026-08-05 📂 Entertainment Original source ↗
Key points
  • Scholars praise Nolan's film as one of the most faithful adaptations of Homer's epic.
  • Critics, including translator Emily Wilson, argue the movie fails to do justice to its women.
  • The film has boosted UK poetry sales, linking cinema to classical literature.
  • Nolan wrote the screenplay himself, a choice Wilson says was a mistake.

Christopher Nolan's The Odyssey has arrived with the force of a tidal wave, dominating box offices and igniting conversations worldwide. Yet, beneath the spectacle of CGI monsters and sun-drenched Aegean seascapes, a quieter storm is brewing among classicists and literary critics. Their charge? The film, for all its grand ambition, stumbles where it matters most: its women.

Reviews are split. Some outlets, like Le Monde, have called it 'probably one of the most faithful cinematic adaptations of the Homeric text' to date. But faithfulness to the original poem, as it turns out, may be part of the problem.

A fidelity that falters

Nolan's reputation rests on intricate plots and intellectual puzzles, and The Odyssey is no different. The film painstakingly recreates the epic's structure, its wanderings, its monsters, its gods. But critics argue this slavish devotion to Homer's narrative arc overlooks the nuanced, often tragic depth of its female figures.

Penelope, the faithful wife, is rendered with stoic patience but little interiority. Circe, the enchantress, is reduced to a plot device. Nausicaa, the young princess, appears as little more than a footnote in Odysseus's journey. The complexity and agency these women possess in the text, scholars argue, are flattened into archetypes.

This is not a matter of simple omission. It is a question of interpretation. Homer's women are not passive; they scheme, they mourn, they rule. Nolan's camera, however, seems more interested in the mechanics of Odysseus's return than in the emotional cost of his absence.

Emily Wilson's challenge

The most prominent voice in this debate is Emily Wilson, the celebrated translator whose modern English versions of Homer have redefined how readers engage with the classics. Wilson has been blunt in her assessment: Nolan should not have written the screenplay himself.

In interviews and public statements, she has suggested that a writer more attuned to the subtleties of character, particularly female character, might have avoided the film's pitfalls. Her critique has resonated, sparking a wider conversation about who gets to tell these ancient stories and how.

The New York Times has framed Wilson's intervention as a 'takedown' that 'ignited a debate'. It is a debate that extends beyond a single film, touching on issues of adaptation, authorship, and the enduring relevance of classical texts.

An unexpected literary boom

Despite the criticism, or perhaps because of it, The Odyssey has had a remarkable side effect: a surge in poetry sales across the UK. The Guardian reports that the film has given an 'epic boost' to the genre, with translations of Homer flying off the shelves.

Readers, it seems, are rushing to compare Nolan's vision with the original text. Bookshops report a spike in demand for Emily Wilson's translation in particular, suggesting that the controversy may be driving curiosity as much as the film's spectacle.

For publishers and poets, this is an unexpected windfall. For the cultural conversation, it is a reminder that cinema can still send audiences back to the page.

What the debate misses

The disagreement over The Odyssey is not merely academic. It reflects a broader tension in how we adapt canonical works. Should a filmmaker prioritise literal fidelity, or should they take liberties to serve contemporary sensibilities? Nolan chose the former, and critics say the result is a film that honours the letter of Homer but betrays his spirit.

Wilson's point, at its core, is about craft. Writing a screenplay requires a different set of skills than adapting a poem. Nolan's strengths lie in structure and concept, not necessarily in rendering the quiet grief of a woman waiting two decades for a husband who may be dead.

The debate shows no signs of cooling. Scholars have weighed in, translators have argued, and audiences are forming their own opinions. What is clear is that The Odyssey has succeeded in one thing: making Homer relevant again, whether for better or worse.

As the awards season approaches, the film is likely to remain in the spotlight. Whether the criticism will influence Nolan's future projects, or whether the controversy will simply add to the film's mystique, remains to be seen.
