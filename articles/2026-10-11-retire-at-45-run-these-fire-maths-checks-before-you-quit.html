Early retirement at 45 starts with one task: the maths

Dreaming about quitting your job at 45 is a common aspiration, but the difference between a fantasy and a plan is arithmetic. A proper FIRE (Financial Independence, Retire Early) calculation forces you to define your target lifestyle, estimate how long the money must last, and choose an investment approach you can stick with through bad years.

In practical terms, the key outputs are (1) your target retirement corpus, (2) the withdrawal rate you’d rely on each year, and (3) whether that withdrawal rate remains survivable if markets fall right after you retire. Those three checks determine whether “retiring at 45” is a timetable or a wish list.

Step 1: Estimate retirement spending with inflation and “unknowns”

Start with your current annual spending, then project it forward to age 45. Don’t just assume a single growth rate; separate categories. Core costs (food, utilities, baseline housing) often track inflation, while discretionary costs can flex during downturns.

Two buyer-style questions matter here: what minimum lifestyle keeps you satisfied, and what is the comfortable “stretch” lifestyle you’re aiming for? Your FIRE maths becomes more accurate when you compute both scenarios, because sequence risk (bad returns early in retirement) is easier to handle with flexibility.

  • Core spending estimate: annual essentials you can’t realistically cut much
  • Discretionary estimate: travel, upgrades, entertainment (cuttable in downturns)
  • Healthcare and insurance buffer: plan for rising medical costs and premiums
  • Taxes: treat them as a recurring cost, not an afterthought

Inflation assumption: use consistency, not optimism

If you assume inflation will be low, you may underestimate required corpus. A defensible approach is to use a long-run inflation view consistent with your country’s macro history, then review it annually. The goal isn’t precision to the rupee—it’s avoiding big, silent errors.

Step 2: Convert spending into a target corpus

Most FIRE calculations use the rule-of-thumb corpus formula: target corpus ≈ annual spending × (1 / safe withdrawal rate). The withdrawal rate reflects how much you can withdraw each year without running out too early. Lower withdrawal rates generally increase “safety” but require a larger corpus.

For early retirement, use more conservative withdrawal-rate thinking than you would for retiring at 60. Your money has more years to cover, and you’re retiring into a higher sequence-risk window (a market crash early in retirement can permanently change outcomes).

Withdrawal-rate benchmarks (for stress-testing)

These are not guarantees, but they are widely used planning benchmarks for stress tests. The right choice depends on whether your portfolio is likely to be stable (e.g., higher bond allocation) or growth-heavy, and on how flexible your spending is in downturns.

Buyer implication: if your calculated withdrawal rate is above what you’d feel comfortable sustaining during a rough decade, your options are usually straightforward—increase savings rate, delay retirement, lower spending targets, improve portfolio risk control, or combine all three.

Step 3: Pressure-test “sequence risk” before you quit

Sequence risk is the risk that poor market returns occur at the start of retirement, forcing withdrawals when the portfolio is already depressed. Many people do the math for average returns; fewer model the path dependence of real markets.

A practical stress test is to assume an initial drawdown period (for example, a multi-year equity slump) and check whether you can still maintain withdrawals—or whether you’ll cut discretionary spending, pause withdrawals, or rebalance.

  • Market downturn scenario: What if your portfolio drops 20%–40% shortly after you retire?
  • Spending flexibility: Can you reduce discretionary spend by 10%–30% temporarily?
  • Rebalancing capacity: Do you have a disciplined process to rebalance without panic selling?
  • Income backstops: Do you have part-time earning, rental income, or guaranteed income streams?

Taxes and healthcare can break “clean” models

In India and many comparable markets, tax treatment can change depending on account types, withdrawal timing, and holding periods. Healthcare spending also tends to rise with age and can be lumpy. Including these costs in the retirement spending baseline reduces the chance that your corpus “looks enough” on paper but fails in the real world.

What to check about your job exit plan (besides the corpus)

Before you quit, evaluate the employment-specific benefits that can materially alter your FIRE timeline: employer provident fund contributions, gratuity, health insurance coverage, stock-based compensation (if any), and severance arrangements. These benefits often affect both your starting corpus and your risk tolerance.

Also examine your current savings rate and whether it can rise. FIRE success is not only about investment returns; it’s also about building a large enough gap between what you earn and what you spend—consistently over time.

Real-world readiness checklist

  • Portfolio resilience: Can you stick with the plan through a multi-year drawdown?
  • Withdrawal rules: Do you have a written method to reduce spending when markets fall?
  • Emergency buffer: Maintain a cash/liquid buffer to avoid selling assets at the worst time.
  • Reassessment schedule: Re-run the maths annually or after major life changes.

Retiring at 45 is achievable for some people, but it’s not a single calculation—it’s a system of assumptions you test, refine, and revisit. Run the numbers with conservatism, stress-test the path, and only then treat early retirement as a decision, not a daydream.
