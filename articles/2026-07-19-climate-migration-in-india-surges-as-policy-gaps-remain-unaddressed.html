Climate migration in India surges as policy gaps remain unaddressed

📅 2026-07-19 📂 Environment Original source ↗
Key points
  • Climate change is driving large-scale internal migration in India, particularly from rural and coastal areas.
  • The government currently lacks a dedicated policy to manage or support climate migrants.
  • Experts warn that the crisis will worsen as extreme weather events become more frequent.

Every year, thousands of families across India pack their belongings and leave their homes. The reasons are increasingly climatic: floods that swallow villages, droughts that turn fields to dust, cyclones that flatten entire settlements. Yet, despite the growing numbers, the country has no formal policy to count, assist or resettle those displaced by climate change.

A recent report by Frontline Magazine highlights the magnitude of the problem. India is now one of the most climate-vulnerable nations on earth, and internal migration driven by environmental factors is rising sharply. But without a legal framework, these migrants remain invisible in official data and excluded from relief mechanisms.

Millions on the move, but no one is counting

Estimates suggest that tens of millions of Indians have already moved due to climate-related reasons over the past decade. Farmers from parched regions of Maharashtra and Rajasthan head to cities. Fisherfolk from the Sundarbans relocate inland as rising seas erode their islands. Residents of flood-prone Bihar and Assam move seasonally or permanently.

Yet there is no central registry or classification for climate migrants. The Census and National Sample Survey do not distinguish between economic migrants and those forced to move by environmental collapse. This gap makes it impossible to design targeted relief or rehabilitation programs.

Policy vacuum amid growing crisis

The absence of a national policy is striking. India has laws and schemes for disaster response, rural employment and urban housing, but none that specifically address the needs of climate migrants. They often fall through the cracks — ineligible for local benefits in their new destinations, and unable to return home.

State governments have started to acknowledge the problem. Odisha, for instance, has mapped climate-vulnerable villages and begun pilot relocation projects. But such efforts are piecemeal and lack the backing of a comprehensive national framework. Experts argue that without a clear policy, local initiatives will remain inadequate.

What drives the exodus

The triggers are becoming more frequent. In 2024 alone, India witnessed record-breaking heatwaves, severe flooding in the northeast and a devastating cyclone on the eastern coast. Each event pushes more people off their land. The slow-onset disasters — creeping desertification, groundwater depletion, sea-level rise — are equally powerful drivers.

These environmental shocks compound existing vulnerabilities. The poorest, who often live in the most exposed areas, are hit hardest. They lack savings to buffer against crop failure or home loss. Migration becomes not a choice but a survival strategy.

The urban dimension

Most climate migrants head to cities, adding pressure on already strained infrastructure. Slums in Chennai, Mumbai and Delhi are swelling with climate-displaced populations. They face precarious living conditions, poor access to water and sanitation, and low-wage informal work. Cities, in turn, struggle to provide basic services.

Urban local bodies have no mandate or resources to absorb climate migrants. The result is a crisis within a crisis: displaced populations living in vulnerable urban pockets, at risk from the same climate hazards they fled.

What is clear is that the problem will not wait. As global temperatures rise, extreme weather events will intensify. India’s climate migration is set to accelerate. Without a policy, the country is sleepwalking into a humanitarian challenge that demands urgent attention.
