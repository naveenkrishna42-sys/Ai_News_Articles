RBI Eases Open-Offer Financing: What It Means for M&A Deals

📅 2026-07-22 📂 Banking Original source ↗
Key points
  • RBI's new norms allow banks to finance open offers under acquisition financing
  • Move expected to simplify deal structures and reduce reliance on promoter funds
  • Legal experts say the change could boost M&A activity in India

The Reserve Bank of India has quietly changed the rules for financing open offers. Banks can now lend to acquirers for the mandatory public announcement that follows a substantial share purchase. The move is being called a significant liberalisation of acquisition financing.

Until now, acquirers had to fund open offers from their own pocket or through non-bank sources. Banks were largely kept out. That restriction made large takeovers more expensive and complex. The new circular changes that.

What the RBI Changed

The RBI amended its master direction on acquisition financing. Banks can now provide loans specifically for open offers under the Takeover Code. The loan must be tied to the overall acquisition and not just the open offer component.

The central bank has not removed all guardrails. Banks must still follow prudential norms on leverage and exposure. But the key barrier—that open offers were outside the ambit of acquisition finance—is gone.

Legal experts at Cyril Amarchand Mangaldas, who reported on the change, say the circular brings clarity. It aligns the financing framework with the realities of deal-making in India.

Why This Matters for M&A

Open offers are a mandatory requirement under Sebi's Takeover Code. Anyone acquiring 25 per cent or more in a listed company must make an offer to buy another 26 per cent from public shareholders. That costs money—often crores of rupees.

Earlier, an acquirer had to arrange this sum separately. If the main acquisition was funded by a bank loan, the open offer still had to be funded from equity or promoter funds. This created a financing gap. Many deals were structured with complex bridge loans or phased payments.

With the RBI's liberalisation, a single financing package can cover both the primary acquisition and the open offer. That reduces cost and simplifies execution.

Impact on Deal Structures

Bankers expect more leveraged buyouts in the mid-market segment. Private equity firms, in particular, will find it easier to take controlling stakes in listed companies without tying up large equity capital.

Promoters looking to consolidate holdings or fend off hostile bids may also benefit. They can now approach banks for open offer funding without triggering regulatory hurdles.

The change does not apply to all lenders. Only banks that are authorised to provide acquisition finance can participate. Non-banking finance companies remain outside the scope unless separately notified.

What Experts Say

Legal professionals at Cyril Amarchand Mangaldas have noted that the circular reduces ambiguity. Earlier, there was debate over whether open offer financing fell under 'acquisition finance' or 'capital market exposure'. The RBI has now settled that question.

The move is also seen as a step towards deepening the corporate bond and loan markets. If banks can fund open offers, the cost of capital for takeovers may drop. That could trigger more consolidation in sectors like pharmaceuticals, IT, and manufacturing.

However, caution remains. Banks will still assess the acquirer's creditworthiness and the target's financial health. The liberalisation does not mean automatic approval.

What Happens Next

Market participants expect a flurry of deal announcements in the coming quarters as acquirers test the new framework. The RBI may also issue further clarifications on loan-to-value ratios and repayment terms. For now, the signal is clear: the regulator wants to make acquisitions easier to finance.
