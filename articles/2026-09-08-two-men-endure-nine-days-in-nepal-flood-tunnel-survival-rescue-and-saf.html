Two Men Endure Nine Days in Nepal Flood Tunnel: Survival, Rescue, and Safety Lessons

The Incident and Immediate Response

On 28 August 2026, a sudden monsoon surge breached a drainage culvert at the Upper Tamakoshi hydropower project, flooding a 1.2‑km access tunnel. Two senior technicians, Raj Kumar Shrestha and Anil Thapa, were inside conducting routine inspections when water rushed in.

Rescue teams were dispatched within an hour, but the torrent had already sealed the tunnel entrance with debris and mud. Initial estimates suggested a window of 48 hours before oxygen levels would become critical.

Survival Tactics Inside the Flooded Tunnel

Faced with darkness and rising water, the men improvised a makeshift ventilation system by tapping a narrow side vent with a metal rod, creating a slow airflow that bought them precious minutes.

They rationed the limited bottled water—just 1.5 litres each—by sipping every two hours and collected condensation on their jackets to supplement hydration.

To maintain morale, Shrestha recited verses from the Bhagavad Gītā, while Thapa kept a mental inventory of the tunnel’s structural markers, helping rescuers later pinpoint their location.

Rescue Operations and Technical Challenges

Rescuers from the Nepalese Army, the National Disaster Risk Reduction Authority, and private contractors converged on the site. The primary obstacle was the water pressure, measured at 2.8 bar, which rendered conventional breathing apparatus ineffective.

Specialised sub‑mersible pumps, typically used for mining shafts, were deployed to lower the water level by 30 centimetres each 12‑hour cycle. Simultaneously, a horizontal drilling rig bored a 0.6‑metre bypass tunnel to create an escape route.

After nine days of continuous effort, the bypass reached the trapped pair on 6 September. A portable winch lifted them to the surface, where they received immediate medical attention for dehydration and mild hypoxia.

Lessons for Hydropower Safety and Future Preparedness

The incident has prompted the Ministry of Energy to mandate real‑time water‑level monitoring in all active tunnels. New guidelines require every tunnel to have at least two independent emergency egress points, each equipped with self‑inflating life‑rafts and oxygen canisters.

Industry experts also recommend installing automatic venting shafts that activate when water pressure exceeds 2 bar, a threshold identified during the rescue.
