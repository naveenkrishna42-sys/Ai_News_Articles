RBI stress test: Banks pass, NBFCs and oil shocks raise red flags

📅 2026-07-28 📂 Banking Original source ↗
Key points
  • Banks have passed the RBI's latest stress tests, showing resilience against credit and liquidity shocks.
  • The central bank flags rising stress in the NBFC sector, especially in unsecured lending and wholesale funding.
  • A sharp rise in global oil prices is identified as a key external risk to India's financial stability.

Banks clear the stress test hurdle

India's banking system has come out clean in the Reserve Bank of India's latest round of stress tests. The central bank's Financial Stability Report, released on Tuesday, shows that scheduled commercial banks have sufficient capital buffers to withstand severe credit and liquidity shocks.

The tests assumed a sharp rise in non-performing assets and a sudden drying up of wholesale funding. Under the worst-case scenario, the system-wide capital adequacy ratio still remained above the regulatory minimum of 9%. Public sector banks, which were traditionally seen as vulnerable, showed marked improvement in their asset quality and provisioning coverage.

RBI officials noted that the results reflect the cumulative effect of tighter recognition norms and better risk management practices adopted by lenders over the past two years. The report explicitly states that the system is resilient and well-placed to handle adverse economic conditions.

NBFCs: the weak link in the chain

While the banking sector has strengthened, the report sounds a clear warning on non-banking financial companies. Stress in the NBFC sector has risen, with several large firms showing higher levels of delinquencies in unsecured loan portfolios.

The central bank's analysis points to a growing mismatch in asset-liability durations for many NBFCs. These entities rely heavily on short-term wholesale funding to finance long-term assets, a model that proved fragile during the 2018 IL&FS crisis. The report notes that a sudden spike in borrowing costs or a liquidity squeeze could trigger a cascading effect across the shadow banking network.

RBI has asked NBFCs to shore up their capital buffers and reduce reliance on market-based funding. Supervisors have been directed to keep a stricter watch on entities with high exposure to consumer credit and real estate lending.

Oil shock: the external wild card

The financial stability report also flags a sharp rise in global crude oil prices as a key external risk. India imports over 80% of its oil requirements, making the economy and financial system highly sensitive to energy price shocks.

A sustained spike in oil prices would widen the current account deficit, put pressure on the rupee, and stoke imported inflation. The RBI's models show that a $20 per barrel increase in crude could shave off nearly 0.5% from GDP growth and push retail inflation above the upper tolerance band of 6%.

Higher fuel prices also directly impact corporate margins, especially in sectors like aviation, logistics, and chemicals. Banks with large exposure to these industries could face a rise in stressed assets if the oil shock persists. The report advises lenders to conduct their own scenario analysis and keep additional provisions for sectors most vulnerable to energy price volatility.

Regulators have not yet indicated any specific policy action on oil risk, but the central bank's stress test framework now explicitly includes an oil price shock scenario as part of its routine surveillance. Market participants will be watching the next Monetary Policy Committee meeting for any forward guidance on how the RBI plans to manage the trade-off between growth and inflation in the event of crude prices spiking.
