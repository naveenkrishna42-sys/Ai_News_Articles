Chennai study shows rooftop gardens cut heat by several degrees

📅 2026-08-11 📂 Chennai Original source ↗
Key points
  • Rooftop gardens reduce surface temperatures by several degrees in Chennai, per a new study.
  • The research highlights green roofs as a viable strategy to combat urban heat island effects.
  • Findings support city-level policies encouraging rooftop greening for thermal comfort.
  • Experts suggest scaling up such interventions across Chennai's dense neighborhoods.

Green roofs cool Chennai's concrete heat

Chennai's battle with rising temperatures may have found an unlikely ally on top of its buildings. A new study has found that rooftop gardens can cut surface heat by several degrees, offering a simple, natural solution to the city's intensifying urban heat island problem.

The research, conducted across multiple sites in Chennai, measured temperature differences between bare concrete roofs and those covered with vegetation. The results, published this week, showed a marked drop in heat on green roofs—a finding that could reshape how the city plans its future infrastructure.

How the study was done

Researchers placed temperature sensors on both conventional and garden-covered rooftops, monitoring them over several weeks during the peak summer months. They recorded surface temperatures at regular intervals, capturing the daily cycle of heating and cooling.

The data revealed that green roofs stayed significantly cooler throughout the day, especially during the afternoon when solar radiation is strongest. The cooling effect was most pronounced on days with high ambient temperatures, suggesting that the benefits grow as the climate warms.

While the study did not specify exact temperature figures, it described the reduction as "several degrees"—enough to make a noticeable difference in a city where summer mercury routinely crosses 40°C.

Why rooftop gardens work

The cooling mechanism is straightforward. Plants shade the roof surface, blocking direct sunlight, and release moisture through transpiration, which absorbs heat from the air. This dual action lowers both surface and ambient temperatures, reducing the need for air conditioning inside buildings.

For Chennai, where most buildings have flat concrete roofs that absorb and radiate heat, the potential is significant. The study's authors argue that widespread adoption of rooftop gardens could reduce the urban heat island effect, where cities are considerably warmer than surrounding rural areas.

"Rooftop gardens are a low-cost, high-impact intervention," one researcher noted, emphasizing that they require minimal maintenance compared to other cooling technologies.

A scalable solution for the city

Chennai has seen rapid urbanization, with concrete replacing green cover at an alarming pace. This has exacerbated heat stress, particularly in densely populated neighborhoods where open spaces are scarce. Rooftop gardens offer a way to reclaim lost green space without acquiring new land.

The study's findings align with broader efforts by the Chennai Corporation to promote sustainable building practices. Some residential complexes and commercial buildings have already experimented with terrace gardens, but the practice remains niche. The study could give policymakers the evidence needed to incentivize greener rooftops.

City officials have yet to comment on the study, but urban planners are likely to take note. Retrofitting existing buildings with rooftop gardens is feasible, though it requires structural assessments to ensure load-bearing capacity and waterproofing.

Challenges and the road ahead

Despite the promise, adoption faces hurdles. Initial installation costs, water requirements, and maintenance concerns can deter building owners. The study suggests that these barriers can be overcome with government subsidies and awareness campaigns.

Water availability is a critical issue, especially during Chennai's dry months. However, researchers point out that drought-resistant native plant species can thrive with minimal irrigation, making the approach viable even in water-scarce periods.

The city is already exploring other heat mitigation measures, such as cool roofs and increased tree cover. Rooftop gardens could complement these efforts, creating a multi-pronged strategy against rising temperatures.

As Chennai heads into another hot season, the study offers a concrete, actionable solution. The next step is for civic bodies to translate this research into policy—perhaps by offering property tax rebates or fast-tracked permits for green roof installations.

What remains to be seen is how quickly the city embraces this green transformation. If scaled effectively, Chennai's skyline could become not just cooler, but also more livable, setting an example for other Indian cities grappling with extreme heat.
