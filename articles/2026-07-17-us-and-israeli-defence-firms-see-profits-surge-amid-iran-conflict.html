US and Israeli defence firms see profits surge amid Iran conflict

📅 2026-07-17 📂 Wars & Conflicts Original source ↗
Key points
  • US defence giants like Lockheed Martin and Raytheon have seen stock prices rise sharply.
  • Israeli companies such as Israel Aerospace Industries and Elbit Systems are also major beneficiaries.
  • Analysts question the ethics of profiting from conflict while civilians bear the cost.

The ongoing military conflict with Iran has proven to be a windfall for a select group of defence contractors on both sides of the Atlantic. Companies in the United States and Israel are reporting record order backlogs and soaring share prices as governments rush to replenish munitions and deploy advanced weaponry.

US defence giants lead the charge

Lockheed Martin, the world's largest defence contractor, has seen its stock climb over 18% since the start of operations. The company's F-35 fighter jets and THAAD missile defence systems are in high demand. Raytheon Technologies, another American heavyweight, is also riding a wave of new contracts for its Patriot missile batteries and precision-guided bombs.

Northrop Grumman and General Dynamics have similarly benefited. The former supplies B-2 bombers and advanced drones, while the latter produces the M1 Abrams tank and other armoured vehicles. Collectively, the top five US defence firms have added over $120 billion in market capitalisation since the conflict escalated.

Israeli contractors see a surge

On the Israeli side, companies like Israel Aerospace Industries (IAI) and Elbit Systems are reporting unprecedented demand. IAI's Iron Dome and David's Sling interceptors have been used extensively to counter Iranian missile and drone attacks. Elbit Systems, which manufactures precision artillery and drones, has seen its quarterly revenues jump by nearly 40%.

Rafael Advanced Defense Systems, another state-backed firm, is also cashing in. Its Spike anti-tank missiles and Trophy active protection systems for tanks are being fast-tracked to the front lines. The Israeli defence ministry has placed emergency orders worth billions of shekels since the war began.

The ethics of wartime profits

Critics argue that such financial gains come at a steep human cost. Human rights groups have pointed out that civilian casualties in Iran and neighbouring regions have mounted as these weapons are deployed. "War should not be a profit centre for shareholders," said one analyst who tracks defence spending. "The incentives are misaligned when companies benefit from prolonged conflict."

However, industry executives defend their role. They argue that supplying advanced weapons helps end wars faster by giving their side a decisive edge. "Our systems save lives by neutralising threats more precisely," a spokesperson for a major contractor told reporters. The debate over war profiteering is unlikely to fade as long as the fighting continues.

What to watch next

As diplomatic efforts to de-escalate the conflict remain stalled, defence stocks are expected to stay elevated. Investors will be watching for the next quarterly earnings reports, which are due in two weeks. Any signs of a ceasefire could trigger a sharp sell-off in these shares, but for now, the war economy is booming.
