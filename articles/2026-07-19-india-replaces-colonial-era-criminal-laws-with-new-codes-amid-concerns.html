India replaces colonial-era criminal laws with new codes amid concerns

📅 2026-07-19 📂 Crime & Law Original source ↗
Key points
  • India's three new criminal codes came into effect on July 1, replacing British-era laws.
  • Changes include tougher penalties, revised sedition law, and new rules for bail and police custody.
  • Critics raise concerns about potential misuse and impact on civil liberties.

New criminal codes take effect

India on Monday officially replaced its colonial-era criminal laws with three new codes, marking the biggest overhaul of the country's justice system in decades. The Bharatiya Nyaya Sanhita, the Bharatiya Nagarik Suraksha Sanhita, and the Bharatiya Sakshya Adhiniyam now replace the Indian Penal Code of 1860, the Code of Criminal Procedure of 1898, and the Indian Evidence Act of 1872.

The government says the new laws will make the justice system faster and more transparent. Home Minister Amit Shah has called the changes a "complete break from the colonial past."

Key changes and concerns

The new codes introduce harsher penalties for crimes against women and children. Mob lynching now carries the death penalty. Separate provisions allow for death sentences in rape cases where the victim dies or is left in a vegetative state.

Sedition, a colonial-era law widely used to suppress dissent, has been replaced with a new offence called "acts endangering sovereignty, unity and integrity of India." Critics argue the new provision still gives the state wide latitude to target political opponents and activists.

Bail rules have been tightened. In some cases, police can now hold suspects in custody for up to 90 days without filing charges. Legal experts say this could lead to prolonged detention for marginalised groups who cannot afford legal representation.

Another controversial change: the new codes allow police to attach property of an accused person before conviction. Human rights groups warn this could be used to pressure vulnerable communities.

Trials can now be conducted via video conferencing. The rules on electronic evidence have been updated to include emails, social media posts, and digital records. But critics say the provisions on digital evidence are vague and could lead to misuse.

Reactions from legal experts and opposition

Many lawyers and judges have expressed mixed feelings. Some say the overhaul is long overdue but caution that implementation will be patchy. Courts are already clogged with millions of pending cases, and the new codes require training for police, prosecutors, and judges.

The opposition Congress party has called the new laws a "black day for democracy." It says the government rushed the changes through Parliament without adequate debate.

Several state governments have said they are not ready to implement the codes fully. Police forces in some states lack the digital infrastructure needed for video trials and electronic evidence management.

Civil society groups point out that the laws were passed in December 2023 but many police stations and courts only received the final text of the codes days before they took effect. Legal aid organisations have reported confusion among lawyers and litigants.

What happens next

The Supreme Court and high courts will now interpret the new provisions as cases come up. The government has promised to review the laws after one year. For now, India begins a new chapter in its criminal justice history — one that will be watched closely for how it balances speed with fairness.
