WFH Adds 45 Minutes of Sitting Daily: What the Turku Study Means for You

✍️ By TIVRA News Science & Health Desk 📅 2026-09-12 📂 Health Original source ↗
Key points
  • Remote workers sit roughly 45 minutes longer per day than office-going peers, per University of Turku research
  • WFH improves sleep duration and quality, creating a mixed health picture rather than a purely negative one
  • Employers must redesign wellness programmes around home ergonomics, movement breaks and step targets

The Core Finding: 45 Extra Minutes of Sitting, Better Sleep

A study from the University of Turku in Finland has quantified what many remote workers suspect about their own bodies: working from home adds approximately 45 minutes of sedentary behaviour per day compared with commuting to a workplace. The same research shows a compensatory benefit — remote workers tend to sleep longer and report better sleep quality, largely because they reclaim commuting time.

The headline number matters because sedentary time is an independent health risk. Prolonged uninterrupted sitting is linked to cardiometabolic strain, reduced insulin sensitivity and musculoskeletal complaints, regardless of whether a person exercises later in the day. An extra 45 minutes daily compounds to roughly 15 hours a month — a meaningful exposure shift for a workforce that now spends two to five days a week at home.

What the Research Actually Measured

The Turku team tracked activity patterns across office and remote working days, using objective movement data rather than self-reported surveys. Two distinct shifts emerged: total physical activity fell on remote days, while sitting bouts lengthened, and sleep duration rose. Separate coverage of the same research notes remote days were linked to roughly 33% less variability in energy expenditure — a signal that home days are more uniform, and more static, than office days.

Why Home Environments Encourage Sitting

The Commute Was Accidental Exercise

For lakhs of Indian professionals in Bengaluru, Pune, Gurugram and Hyderabad, the daily commute — walking to a metro, standing on a crowded platform, climbing office stairs — quietly contributed thousands of steps. Remove it and daily movement collapses unless deliberately replaced.

Home Ergonomics Are Rarely Office-Grade

Most home setups lack adjustable desks, proper monitor height and supportive chairs. Workers compensate by sitting longer in one position, which worsens posture and reduces spontaneous movement breaks. The absence of colleagues walking past a desk removes the social nudges that break up office sitting.

Sleep Gains Are Real but Partial Compensation

Better sleep supports recovery, cognition and mood, and should not be dismissed. However, sleep cannot offset the metabolic effects of prolonged sitting during waking hours. The two findings must be read together, not selectively.

Real-World Implications for Employees and Employers

  • Pros of remote work per this study: longer sleep duration, better sleep quality, time saved on commuting, and more schedule control for focused work.
  • Cons and risks: 45 extra sedentary minutes daily, lower overall physical activity, reduced day-to-day energy variability, and higher risk of back, neck and metabolic issues over time.
  • Employer action: subsidise ergonomic chairs and standing desks, mandate short movement breaks between long meetings, and set step or movement targets in wellness programmes.
  • Employee action: anchor two 10-minute walks to fixed points in the day, stand during calls, and treat the reclaimed commute time as scheduled movement rather than extra screen time.

Practical Benchmarks to Track

Use measurable targets instead of vague intentions. Aim for a minimum of 7,000 steps on remote days, at least one standing or walking block every 60 minutes, and no single uninterrupted sitting bout beyond 45 to 60 minutes. A basic fitness tracker or smartwatch delivers this data at entry-level prices, making self-monitoring accessible to most salaried households.

Hybrid arrangements should be evaluated on health metrics, not just output. Companies that rotate employees between home and office days may unintentionally concentrate sedentary behaviour on remote days, so structured movement policies matter more than location mandates.

The Verdict

The University of Turku research reframes the WFH debate. Remote work is not inherently unhealthy, but its default setting — fewer steps, longer sitting, better sleep — demands deliberate countermeasures. Employers who invest in home ergonomics and movement culture will retain healthier, more productive teams. Employees who treat walking as a non-negotiable calendar item will capture the sleep benefits without paying the sedentary penalty.
