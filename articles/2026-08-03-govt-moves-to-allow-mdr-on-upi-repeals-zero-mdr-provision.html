Govt moves to allow MDR on UPI, repeals zero-MDR provision

📅 2026-08-03 📂 Business Original source ↗
Key points
  • Bill seeks to repeal the earlier amendment that mandated zero MDR on UPI transactions.
  • Move could allow payment companies to charge merchants, potentially affecting small businesses.
  • Experts say impact on users may be limited if charges are only on large merchants.
  • Government has not yet announced any timeline for implementation or specific fee structure.

The Union government has taken the first formal step toward levying a Merchant Discount Rate (MDR) on Unified Payments Interface (UPI) transactions, a move that could reshape India's digital payments ecosystem. A bill introduced in Parliament proposes to repeal the earlier amendment that had mandated zero MDR on UPI, effectively opening the door for payment service providers to charge merchants.

The proposal, reported by Moneycontrol and other outlets, marks a significant policy shift. Since UPI's explosive growth, the government had kept the service free for merchants to encourage digital adoption. Now, with the ecosystem mature, the Finance Ministry appears ready to let market forces determine pricing.

What the bill proposes

The draft legislation seeks to amend the Information Technology Act to remove the statutory bar on MDR. This would allow banks and payment gateways to negotiate fees with merchants, similar to how credit and debit card transactions are priced.

Officials have not yet confirmed the exact fee structure or the timeline for implementation. The bill's language suggests the government wants flexibility rather than a fixed rate, leaving room for the Reserve Bank of India (RBI) to set guidelines later.

Who pays, who benefits

For the average UPI user, the immediate impact may be negligible. Industry watchers say charges are likely to apply only to large merchants with high transaction volumes, not to small shopkeepers or peer-to-peer transfers.

Banks and payment companies, which have long complained about the cost of maintaining UPI infrastructure, stand to gain. The National Payments Corporation of India (NPCI) has repeatedly flagged the sustainability of zero-MDR UPI, especially as transaction volumes cross the 10-billion mark monthly.

Small businesses, however, could feel the pinch if the fee is passed down. A kirana store accepting UPI may see its margins shrink if MDR is set at even 0.5 percent. That risk has prompted consumer groups to urge caution.

Why the reversal now

The earlier zero-MDR amendment was introduced in 2019 as a sweetener to boost digital payments. At the time, UPI was still finding its feet, and the government wanted to undercut cash transactions.

That goal has been achieved. UPI now dominates retail payments, and the infrastructure costs are mounting. The Finance Ministry's move signals a recognition that someone must pay for the rails, and that someone is likely to be merchants.

Critics argue this could undo years of progress. If fees push merchants back to cash, the digital push could stall. Supporters counter that a small fee is a fair price for the convenience and security UPI offers.

What happens next

The bill must clear both houses of Parliament and receive presidential assent before becoming law. Once enacted, the RBI and NPCI will likely frame operational rules, including any cap on MDR rates.

The government has not indicated whether it will exempt small merchants or cap charges for low-value transactions. Those details are expected once the bill is deliberated in the monsoon session.

For now, UPI remains free. But the direction is clear: the era of zero cost may be ending, and the debate over who bears the burden is just beginning.
