Why Bollywood Still Can't Quit Nusrat Fateh Ali Khan: From Mirzapur to Dhurandhar

✍️ By TIVRA News Editorial Team 📅 2026-09-13 📂 Movies Original source ↗
Key points
  • Nusrat Fateh Ali Khan's qawwali catalogue remains Bollywood's most licensed South Asian vocal archive, powering trailers and background scores from Mirzapur The Movie to Dhurandhar.
  • Sync licensing costs for a marquee Nusrat track can run into crores, yet producers justify the spend through trailer virality and music-streaming multipliers.
  • Estate and label clearances are the biggest bottleneck; unlicensed remixes invite takedowns and statutory damages under India's copyright regime.
  • The trend is commercially rational, not nostalgic: qawwali's 6-10 minute crescendo structure maps perfectly onto modern trailer and montage editing.

The Enduring Pull of the Khan Qawwali Archive

Bollywood's relationship with Nusrat Fateh Ali Khan predates the streaming era. From the 1990s jugalbandi of Bandit Queen and Dhadkan to the global crossover of Dead Man Walking, his voice has been treated as a dramatic instrument rather than mere background music. Two upcoming projects, Mirzapur The Movie and Dhurandhar, confirm the trend has not cooled. Both are using the Khan catalogue to signal scale, tragedy and old-world gravity that a freshly composed track rarely delivers at the same speed.

The commercial logic is straightforward. A recognisable qawwali gives a trailer an instant emotional hook, cutting through the noise of a crowded release calendar without spending on a new star-singer combo.

What the Two Films Actually Signal

Mirzapur The Movie extends a franchise built on lawless masculinity and folk-flavoured menace. A Nusrat track fits that grammar: the hamd and naat tradition carries spiritual weight that, when placed against on-screen violence, produces the kind of irony editors chase. Dhurandhar, on the other hand, leans into period-scale storytelling, where a qawwali sequence functions as both set piece and cultural authenticity marker.

The two projects are not isolated. Music supervisors across Mumbai treat the Khan archive as a reliable emotional shortcut, especially when a film needs to establish a North Indian, Sufi-adjacent or pre-liberalisation milieu.

Buyer's Guide: Licensing Costs and Clearance Realities

For producers and music supervisors, the Khan catalogue is not a plug-and-play asset. Rights are split across the estate, original labels and, in several cases, multiple publishing entities. Clearance timelines of three to six months are common. Budgets vary wildly depending on the track's prominence and territory.

Pros and Cons for Filmmakers

  • Pros: Instant emotional recognition; trailer virality; cross-generational appeal; no dependency on a contemporary singer's schedule or fee.
  • Pros: Streaming re-release revenue can partially offset licence cost; qawwali structure suits long montages and climax sequences.
  • Cons: Expensive and slow to clear; overuse risks creative laziness and audience fatigue.
  • Cons: Estate approvals can be restrictive on lyrical alteration or remix treatment.

Why This Trend Will Outlast the Current Cycle

Bollywood's reliance on the Khan archive is driven by a structural gap. Contemporary composers can produce chart-toppers, but few can replicate the devotional intensity of a live qawwali recorded with a full party of harmonium, tabla and clapping chorus. That sonic texture is difficult to synthesise convincingly. Until the industry develops a comparable vocal tradition at scale, the Khan catalogue will remain a premium, if costly, short cut.

For buyers, the practical takeaway is to negotiate early, budget for multi-territory rights, and treat the licence as a marketing line item rather than a music cost. For audiences, the payoff is simpler: a voice that has outlived every trend cycle it has been drafted into.
