Difficult-to-Manage Axial Spondyloarthritis: Rare, High Burden, Real Costs

✍️ By TIVRA News Science & Health Desk 📅 2026-09-15 📂 Health Original source ↗
Key points
  • Difficult-to-manage axSpA affects a small subset of patients but consumes a disproportionate share of rheumatology resources.
  • Biologic and targeted synthetic DMARDs cost between ₹15,000 and ₹80,000 per month in India, with limited insurance coverage.
  • Early referral to a rheumatologist and objective monitoring of inflammation are critical to prevent irreversible spinal damage.

Why Difficult-to-Manage Axial Spondyloarthritis Matters

Axial spondyloarthritis (axSpA) is a chronic inflammatory disease that primarily affects the spine and sacroiliac joints. While most patients respond to first-line treatments like NSAIDs and physical therapy, a small but significant proportion develop difficult-to-manage disease. This subset fails multiple biologic therapies, experiences persistent inflammation, and faces progressive structural damage. A recent review in EMJ underscores that although difficult-to-manage axSpA is rare, its burden on patients, caregivers, and healthcare systems is disproportionately high.

For Indian patients, the challenge is compounded by limited access to advanced diagnostics, high out-of-pocket costs for biologics, and a shortage of rheumatologists. Understanding this condition is not just a clinical imperative but a commercial and policy priority.

What Defines Difficult-to-Manage axSpA?

Rheumatologists classify axSpA as difficult-to-manage when a patient has active disease despite treatment with at least two different TNF inhibitors, or when they have contraindications to conventional therapies. Key markers include elevated C-reactive protein (CRP), persistent pain, and MRI evidence of inflammation. The EMJ review notes that these patients often cycle through multiple drugs, incurring high costs and risking irreversible fusion of vertebrae.

Real-World Buyer Implications for Patients and Providers

For patients, the financial toxicity of difficult-to-manage axSpA is severe. A year of treatment with a biologic can cost upwards of ₹9 lakh, pushing many families into debt. Biosimilars have reduced prices by 30–50% in recent years, but access remains uneven across tier-2 and tier-3 cities. Rheumatologists must weigh efficacy, safety, and cost when selecting among TNF inhibitors, IL-17 inhibitors, and JAK inhibitors. The EMJ review stresses that switching between drug classes should be guided by objective markers, not just patient-reported pain.

Key Real-World Highlights

  • Diagnostic delay: Indian patients wait an average of 7–10 years for a correct diagnosis, by which time spinal damage may be irreversible.
  • Cost burden: Biologics consume 40–60% of household income for many middle-class families.
  • Biosimilar uptake: Cheaper biosimilars for adalimumab and etanercept are now available, but prescription patterns vary widely.
  • Monitoring gap: Only 30% of Indian rheumatology clinics routinely use MRI or CRP to track treatment response.
  • Multidisciplinary care: Physiotherapy and psychological support are often neglected, worsening outcomes.

Pros and Cons of Current Treatment Pathways

  • Pros: TNF inhibitors achieve low disease activity in 60–70% of patients; IL-17 and JAK inhibitors offer alternatives for non-responders; biosimilars improve affordability.
  • Cons: No cure exists; long-term immunosuppression raises infection risk; high cost and limited insurance create access barriers; delayed diagnosis reduces treatment efficacy.

What Needs to Change

Improving outcomes for difficult-to-manage axSpA requires a three-pronged approach. First, primary care physicians must refer suspected cases to rheumatologists earlier. Second, the government should include biologics under national health schemes and negotiate bulk pricing. Third, registries that track real-world drug performance in Indian populations are essential. The EMJ review serves as a wake-up call: rare diseases can have outsized impact, and ignoring them is both a clinical and economic mistake.
