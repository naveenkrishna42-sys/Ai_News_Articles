The killing of Indian-origin woman Shalini Thakur in Sacramento, California, has moved a familiar consumer-tech anxiety into the realm of violent crime. According to police accounts reported by NDTV and ABC10, the man accused of her murder had allegedly tracked her movements for months using an Apple AirTag and a duplicate key to her home. He had reportedly entered the United States illegally in 2023, and Sacramento deputies had probable cause to arrest him weeks before the shooting.

What the Case Records Show

Thakur, a California resident of Indian origin, was shot dead in what investigators describe as the culmination of a sustained stalking campaign. Her mother told reporters that the "system failed her" — a reference to the missed window in which law enforcement could have intervened. KCRA reported that deputies had sufficient legal grounds to arrest the alleged gunman weeks earlier. The case now sits at the intersection of immigration enforcement, policing gaps, and consumer surveillance technology.

For buyers, the technical detail matters: an AirTag is a Rs 3,500-class coin-sized Bluetooth tracker designed to help find keys and bags. It was not designed as a personal safety device, and its anti-stalking features are reactive, not preventive.

Why AirTags Are a Stalking Risk

AirTags rely on Apple's Find My network, which uses nearby iPhones to relay location data. That makes them accurate in dense urban areas and cheap to deploy. Apple has added anti-stalking alerts — an unknown AirTag travelling with an iPhone triggers a notification, and Android users can use Tracker Detect. But these warnings only fire after the tag is already tracking. There is no hardware kill switch, no mandatory registration, and no way for a victim to disable a tag they cannot physically find.

For Indian buyers, the practical benchmark is simple: a tracker that costs less than a mid-range smartwatch can deliver weeks of location history. That asymmetry is the core risk.

Pros, Cons and Real-World Takeaways

  • Pro: AirTags are inexpensive, reliable, and easy to set up for legitimate lost-item tracking.
  • Pro: Cross-platform detection tools exist for Android and iOS users.
  • Con: Anti-stalking alerts are reactive; they do not stop tracking.
  • Con: No mandatory owner identity, making misuse hard to trace.
  • Con: Victims often discover trackers only after repeated sightings or police checks.
  • Takeaway: If you suspect tracking, use a Bluetooth scanner app, check bags and vehicles manually, and file a police complaint immediately.

Value Justification and Buyer Guidance

At roughly Rs 3,500, an AirTag is a value purchase for luggage and keys. The same price point makes it a low-cost tool for surveillance. Buyers should treat it as a convenience device, not a security device. For personal safety, a dedicated GPS tracker with geofencing and SOS costs Rs 8,000–15,000 and offers clearer accountability. The Thakur case shows that technology alone cannot substitute for timely police action — and that the cheapest tracker can carry the highest human cost.
