Why More Non-Smokers in India Are Getting Lung Cancer

📅 2026-08-11 📂 Health Original source ↗
Key points
  • Lung cancer is increasingly diagnosed in non-smokers in India
  • Risk factors include second-hand smoke, air pollution, and genetic predisposition
  • Experts urge screening criteria to go beyond pack-years and include AI-driven case-finding

A Shift in the Profile

Lung cancer has long been branded a smoker's disease. But doctors across India are now seeing a growing number of patients who have never touched a cigarette. Oncologists say the profile of the typical lung cancer patient is changing, and it demands a rethinking of how the disease is detected and treated.

Dr Prriya Eshpuniyani, an oncologist, puts it plainly: "Lung cancer is not just a smoker's disease." She points to a mix of environmental and genetic factors that are pushing non-smokers into the risk zone, often without any obvious warning signs until the disease has advanced.

Beyond the Smoke: Hidden Risk Factors

Second-hand smoke remains a major concern, especially for women and children who share homes with smokers. But it is not the only culprit. Air pollution, exposure to cooking fumes, and occupational hazards like asbestos and diesel exhaust are increasingly being linked to lung cancer in non-smokers.

Genetic mutations also play a role. Some non-smokers carry inherited changes that make their lung tissue more vulnerable to malignancy, even in the absence of any external trigger. Doctors say these cases often go undetected because the standard screening criteria are built around smoking history.

The Problem with Pack-Years

For decades, screening guidelines have relied on "pack-years"—a calculation of how many cigarettes a person has smoked over time. That approach, experts argue, is missing a large segment of the population. A non-smoker with a persistent cough or chest pain is rarely referred for a CT scan, delaying diagnosis by months.

By the time symptoms become undeniable, the cancer is often at an advanced stage. This is one reason why lung cancer survival rates in India remain poor, even as treatment options improve.

Call for AI-Driven Case-Finding

Health technology experts are now pushing for a shift from traditional screening to AI-driven case-finding. Instead of asking only "Have you smoked?", algorithms can analyse a combination of factors—age, family history, exposure to pollution, and even subtle changes in routine chest X-rays—to flag high-risk individuals who would otherwise slip through the cracks.

Such tools are already being tested in some hospitals, and early results are promising. But their adoption remains uneven, particularly in smaller cities and rural areas where access to advanced diagnostics is limited.

Delayed Diagnosis: A Silent Crisis

Reports from Kashmir and other regions highlight a worrying trend: patients often ignore early symptoms like a persistent cough or hoarseness, attributing them to seasonal infections or tuberculosis. By the time they see a specialist, the disease has spread.

This is compounded by a lack of awareness. Many people still believe that if they have never smoked, they are not at risk. Doctors say this misconception is costing lives.

What Needs to Change

Oncologists and public health experts are unanimous on one point: screening must be broader, more inclusive, and more intelligent. A 50-year-old woman who has never smoked but has been exposed to biomass fuel for decades may be at greater risk than a light smoker. Yet current guidelines would not flag her for early screening.

There is also a need for public education. The message that lung cancer can strike anyone—regardless of smoking status—must be amplified through campaigns and primary care consultations. General physicians, who are often the first point of contact, need training to recognise red flags in non-smokers.

As India's air quality worsens and urban lifestyles change, the number of non-smoking lung cancer patients is likely to rise. The question is whether the healthcare system will adapt in time. Over the coming months, experts will be watching how hospitals integrate AI tools into routine practice, and whether policy makers revise screening guidelines to reflect the new reality.
