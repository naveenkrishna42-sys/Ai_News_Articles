Taiwan studies Iran, Ukraine wars for air defence lessons

📅 2026-07-17 📂 Wars & Conflicts Original source ↗
Key points
  • Taiwan is studying air-defence tactics from the Ukraine and Iran conflicts.
  • Key lessons include layered systems, drone threats, and civilian preparedness.
  • Taiwan aims to adapt strategies to its geography and resources.

Learning from conflict: Taiwan’s air-defence review

Taiwan is drawing critical lessons from the wars in Ukraine and Iran to shape its air-defence strategy. Analysts and military planners are studying how these nations have countered aerial threats, from missiles to drones, to bolster the island’s defences.

The conflicts highlight the importance of layered air-defence systems. Ukraine’s use of mobile, low-cost systems against Russian cruise missiles and drones has shown how flexibility can offset numerical disadvantages.

Iran’s approach: layered systems and decoys

Iran’s air-defence strategy, refined over decades, emphasises redundancy and deception. The country has integrated Russian and domestic systems, creating overlapping coverage. Decoys and electronic warfare have also played a key role in confusing adversaries.

Taiwanese officials are particularly interested in Iran’s ability to protect critical infrastructure. The use of underground facilities and rapid repair teams has kept key sites operational despite repeated strikes.

Drone threats and countermeasures

Drones have emerged as a major challenge in both Ukraine and Iran. Small, cheap drones can overwhelm traditional defences. Taiwan is investing in counter-drone technology, including jammers and net guns, to address this threat.

Civilian preparedness is another focus. Ukraine’s volunteer networks and rapid reporting systems have shown how non-military actors can contribute to situational awareness. Taiwan is exploring similar initiatives to improve resilience.

Adapting to local geography and resources

Taiwan’s geography—a narrow island with dense urban areas—poses unique challenges. Missile launches from the mainland could saturate defences. Planners are prioritising mobile systems that can relocate quickly to avoid detection.

Budget constraints remain a concern. Taiwan is seeking to balance cost and capability, focusing on systems that offer the best value for money. Domestic production is being ramped up to reduce reliance on foreign suppliers.

What comes next

Taiwan will continue to refine its air-defence strategy based on evolving threats. Exercises and drills are expected to test new tactics, while cooperation with allies, particularly the United States, will remain vital. The coming months will show how these lessons translate into concrete deployments.
