RBI Bars Banks From Selling Stressed Assets to Defaulting Borrowers

📅 2026-07-18 📂 Banking Original source ↗
Key points
  • RBI prohibits sale of stressed assets to defaulting borrowers or related parties.
  • Move aims to prevent borrowers from regaining control at discounted prices.
  • Lenders must ensure arms-length transactions and proper due diligence.
  • Regulation effective immediately for all new transactions.
  • Industry experts say it will improve discipline in asset resolution.

The Reserve Bank of India (RBI) has directed all commercial banks and non-banking financial companies (NBFCs) to stop selling stressed assets back to the same borrowers who defaulted on those loans. The circular, issued on Thursday, aims to close a loophole that allowed defaulters to regain control of assets at steep discounts.

The central bank's order applies to all sale of stressed assets, including those classified as non-performing assets (NPAs), and covers both secured and unsecured exposures. Lenders have been told that any such sale must be to a third party not connected to the original borrower.

Why the RBI Stepped In

Banking sources say the move follows instances where promoters of defaulting companies used shell entities to buy back their own stressed loans from lenders at a fraction of the original value. This allowed them to write off debt without actually repaying it, undermining the entire resolution process.

The RBI's circular explicitly states that the sale cannot be to the borrower, their relatives, or any entity where the borrower has a controlling stake. Lenders must also ensure that the transaction is at arm's length and based on a proper valuation report from an external agency.

Impact on Asset Reconstruction Companies

Asset reconstruction companies (ARCs) are also affected. They cannot sell assets back to the original borrower or related parties even after acquiring them from banks. The RBI wants to prevent any indirect route that could allow the defaulter to regain control.

Industry experts say this will force ARCs to focus on genuine recovery and resolution rather than acting as a conduit for asset stripping. The new rule applies immediately to all fresh transactions, though existing deals are not affected.

Lenders to Verify Buyer Background

Banks must now conduct enhanced due diligence on the buyer before selling any stressed asset. They need to check whether the buyer has any indirect links to the original borrower, including through family members, business partners, or corporate structures.

If a lender is found to have violated these norms, the RBI can impose penalties, including restrictions on further sale of assets. The central bank has also asked lenders to put in place internal policies to ensure compliance.

Bankers say the new rule could slow down the sale process initially, as verifying the buyer's background will take time. But they believe it will lead to cleaner resolution in the long run.

Reactions From the Banking Sector

Senior bankers have welcomed the move. One public sector bank executive said, 'This was a glaring loophole that needed to be closed. Promoters were gaming the system, and the RBI has rightly stepped in.'

However, some in the industry point out that the new rule could reduce the pool of potential buyers for stressed assets. They argue that sometimes the original borrower is the only party willing to pay a reasonable price, especially for specialized assets like factories or machinery.

The RBI has not provided any transition period. Banks must comply with the new norms immediately for all new sale transactions. Existing sale agreements that are already in the pipeline but not yet completed will also come under the new rules.

Going forward, lenders will have to explore other options like restructuring, recovery through legal channels, or selling to third-party investors. The central bank's focus remains on ensuring that the stressed asset resolution process is transparent and that defaulters do not benefit from their own default.
