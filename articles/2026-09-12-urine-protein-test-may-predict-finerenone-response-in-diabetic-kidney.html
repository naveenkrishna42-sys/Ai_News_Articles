Urine Protein as a Response Marker for Finerenone in DKD

Emerging clinical evidence indicates that urine protein levels—specifically albuminuria—can help clinicians identify which patients with diabetic kidney disease (DKD) are most likely to respond to finerenone, a non-steroidal mineralocorticoid receptor antagonist (ns-MRA). The finding, reported by EMJ, carries direct implications for Indian nephrology and endocrinology practice, where DKD accounts for a substantial share of dialysis initiation.

Finerenone is approved for patients with type 2 diabetes and chronic kidney disease (CKD) to reduce the risk of sustained eGFR decline, end-stage kidney disease, and cardiovascular events. However, not every patient responds equally. Using urine protein as a stratification tool could sharpen patient selection and avoid unnecessary expenditure on a therapy that costs several thousand rupees per month.

Why Albuminuria Matters in Treatment Decisions

Albuminuria reflects glomerular damage and is already a validated prognostic marker in DKD. Trials such as FIDELIO-DKD and FIGARO-DKD demonstrated that finerenone reduces albuminuria and slows CKD progression, with the greatest absolute benefit observed in patients with higher baseline urine albumin-to-creatinine ratio (UACR). This suggests that urine protein testing—widely available across Indian diagnostic labs—can function as both a prognostic and predictive biomarker.

For clinicians, the practical takeaway is straightforward: baseline and serial UACR measurements should guide initiation and continuation of finerenone, alongside serum potassium and eGFR monitoring.

Real-World Buyer and Prescriber Implications

For patients paying out of pocket, finerenone represents a meaningful monthly commitment. If urine protein profiling can identify likely non-responders early, it prevents months of futile spending and allows resources to be redirected toward alternative or combination strategies such as SGLT2 inhibitors.

Hospitals and clinics benefit too. A simple urine protein test costs a fraction of a month's medication, making it one of the most cost-effective decision-support tools in DKD management. Payers and corporate health programmes can incorporate UACR-based criteria into prior authorisation pathways without adding significant administrative burden.

Key Real-World Highlights

  • Low-cost stratification: UACR testing is inexpensive and universally available, unlike genetic or advanced biomarkers.
  • Higher baseline albuminuria, greater benefit: Patients with elevated urine protein appear to gain more kidney and cardiovascular protection.
  • Serial monitoring adds value: Tracking UACR over time can confirm therapeutic response or prompt reassessment.
  • Potassium vigilance remains essential: Finerenone requires periodic serum potassium checks, especially in advanced CKD.
  • Not a standalone test: Urine protein should complement, not replace, eGFR and clinical judgement.

Limitations and Clinical Caution

Albuminuria can fluctuate due to infection, exercise, hyperglycaemia, or hypertension, so a single reading should not dictate therapy. Confirmatory testing and correlation with eGFR trends are necessary. Additionally, the evidence supports urine protein as a response marker rather than a definitive predictive test, and larger prospective studies in Indian populations would strengthen generalisability.

What This Means for Indian Patients and Clinicians

India carries a heavy DKD burden, with many patients reaching nephrology care late. Incorporating urine protein assessment into routine diabetes follow-up offers a pragmatic route to earlier, more targeted finerenone use. Clinicians should document baseline UACR, counsel patients on cost versus benefit, and schedule structured follow-up to measure response.

As evidence accumulates, urine protein testing may shift from a routine monitoring parameter to a genuine treatment-selection tool—helping ensure that finerenone reaches the patients most likely to benefit, while sparing others the expense and risk of ineffective therapy.
