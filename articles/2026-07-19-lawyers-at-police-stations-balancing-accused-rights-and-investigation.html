Lawyers at Police Stations: Balancing Accused Rights and Investigation Integrity

📅 2026-07-19 📂 Crime & Law Original source ↗
Key points
  • Supreme Court has held that accused has no fundamental right to have lawyer present during interrogation
  • Police argue lawyers at station can intimidate witnesses and obstruct investigation
  • Legal experts say presence of lawyer can prevent custodial torture and ensure fair process

The Clash of Rights and Process

Can a lawyer sit inside a police station while their client is being questioned? That question has sparked a fierce debate among legal professionals and law enforcement in India. The issue sits at the intersection of two competing interests — the accused's right to a fair trial and the police's need to conduct an unhindered investigation.

The Supreme Court has made its position clear on several occasions. In the landmark case of Nandini Satpathy v. P.L. Dani (1978), the Court held that the right against self-incrimination under Article 20(3) of the Constitution does not extend to a right to have a lawyer present during police interrogation. The Code of Criminal Procedure, 1973, under Section 161, allows police to examine any person supposed to be acquainted with the facts of a case. The law does not mandate the presence of a legal counsel during this stage.

Police Concerns: Interference and Intimidation

Law enforcement agencies argue that allowing lawyers inside police stations during interrogation can disrupt the investigative process. Officers claim that legal counsel often prompt their clients to remain silent or give evasive answers. This, they say, makes it harder to extract crucial information.

There is also the fear of witness intimidation. Police officials point out that if a lawyer is present when a witness is being questioned, the lawyer could potentially alert the accused or other parties. This could lead to tampering with evidence or influencing witnesses. "The presence of a lawyer changes the dynamics of the interrogation room," a senior police officer told TIVRA News on condition of anonymity. "It shifts the focus from gathering facts to legal maneuvering."

The Case for Legal Presence: Preventing Custodial Torture

On the other side of the argument, human rights activists and defence lawyers point to the grim reality of custodial violence. India has a long history of police brutality and forced confessions. The National Human Rights Commission records hundreds of custodial deaths every year, though official figures are often disputed.

In the 2010 case of D.K. Basu v. State of West Bengal, the Supreme Court laid down guidelines to prevent custodial torture. These include the right to inform a relative, the right to have a lawyer present during interrogation, and the right to a medical examination. However, these guidelines are not always followed. "A lawyer in the station is not an obstacle to justice — it is a safeguard against abuse," said senior advocate Indira Jaising, speaking at a recent legal seminar. "Without legal oversight, the line between interrogation and torture can easily blur."

What the Law Says Now

The current legal position remains nuanced. The Supreme Court has not granted an absolute right to have a lawyer present during police questioning. But the D.K. Basu guidelines, though not a statutory right, are binding on all police stations across India. In practice, many police stations allow lawyers to wait outside the interrogation room but not inside.

The Law Commission of India, in its 152nd Report (1994), recommended amending the Code of Criminal Procedure to allow the presence of a lawyer during interrogation. That recommendation was never implemented. The debate continues, with no clear legislative solution in sight.

The Way Forward

The question of lawyers at police stations is unlikely to be resolved anytime soon. The Supreme Court may eventually be called upon to clarify the extent of the right to counsel at the pre-arrest and interrogation stages. Until then, the balance between protecting the accused and preserving investigative integrity will be struck case by case, station by station.
