Why Australia's Finke River Is Called the Oldest on Earth

📅 2026-07-20 📂 Science Original source ↗
Key points
  • The Finke River in central Australia is frequently described as the world's oldest river.
  • Geologists date its ancient course to over 350 million years ago, before the breakup of Gondwana.
  • The claim is not universally accepted, as defining 'oldest river' involves complex geological criteria.

A River Older Than the Himalayas

Deep in the red heart of Australia, the Finke River meanders through the MacDonnell Ranges. It is not a mighty waterway like the Amazon or the Nile. For most of the year, it is a dry, sandy bed. Yet, this modest river carries a heavyweight title: it is frequently called the oldest river on Earth.

The claim is not recent clickbait. It has appeared in scientific literature and travel guides for decades. But what is the actual evidence behind this geological boast, and does it hold up to scrutiny?

Where the Age Claim Comes From

Geologists point to the Finke River's ancient course. The river flows through a landscape that has remained remarkably stable for hundreds of millions of years. Unlike most of the planet's surface, which has been reshaped by tectonic collisions and volcanic activity, central Australia has been geologically quiet for a very long time.

The key evidence lies in the river's path. The Finke cuts through the MacDonnell Ranges, which were formed more than 350 million years ago. Geologists argue that the river must already have been there when the ranges rose, meaning it is older than the mountains themselves. This is a rare phenomenon. Most rivers are younger than the landscapes they flow through.

Reading the Rocks

Further evidence comes from sedimentary deposits. Scientists have studied ancient sand and gravel layers left by the Finke. These deposits suggest the river has been flowing along a similar route since the Paleozoic Era, long before dinosaurs walked the Earth.

This continuity is what makes the Finke special. Many rivers are geologically young, having formed only within the last few million years due to ice ages or continental drift. The Finke's drainage system, by contrast, appears to have survived the breakup of the supercontinent Gondwana largely intact.

A Contested Title

Not everyone agrees that the Finke deserves the crown. The problem is defining what 'oldest river' means. Do we mean the oldest continuous water flow? The oldest valley shape? The oldest course alignment?

If the definition is the oldest continuous river valley, the Finke is a strong contender. But other rivers, such as the New River in the United States and the Meuse in Europe, also make ancient claims. The science is not settled. Each river has a different geological history, and direct comparison is difficult.

What the Science Says Today

The Finke River remains the most popular candidate among geologists for the title of Earth's oldest river. The evidence for its ancient course is solid. However, scientists are careful not to present the claim as absolute fact.

The river's age is estimated, not proven with a precise date. What is clear is that the Finke has been a feature of the Australian outback for an extraordinarily long time, outlasting entire mountain ranges and climate shifts.

For now, the Finke River's reputation as the oldest river on Earth rests on strong geological reasoning. But as with many scientific titles, it comes with a footnote: the answer depends on how you ask the question. Researchers continue to study its ancient sediments, hoping to refine our understanding of how landscapes persist through deep time.
