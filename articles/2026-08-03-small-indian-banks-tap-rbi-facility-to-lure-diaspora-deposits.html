Small Indian banks tap RBI facility to lure diaspora deposits

📅 2026-08-03 📂 Banking Original source ↗
Key points
  • Small banks use RBI's currency swap facility to manage forex risks
  • Diaspora deposits offer cheaper funding for smaller lenders
  • Competition for NRI deposits is heating up among Indian banks

Mumbai: A quiet shift is underway in India's banking sector. Small banks, often squeezed for low-cost funding, are now turning to a Reserve Bank of India (RBI) facility to woo deposits from the Indian diaspora. The strategy, which leverages a currency swap window, is helping these lenders tap into the billions of dollars parked by non-resident Indians (NRIs) in overseas accounts.

The move comes as larger banks have traditionally dominated the NRI deposit market, using their brand presence and branch networks abroad. Smaller lenders, however, are finding a backdoor through the RBI's forex swap mechanism, which allows them to hedge currency risks on foreign-currency deposits. By converting these deposits into rupees at favourable rates, they can offer competitive returns to diaspora savers.

The Currency Swap Advantage

The RBI's facility, typically used by banks to manage liquidity, is now being repurposed as a marketing tool. Under this mechanism, banks can swap dollars or other foreign currencies for rupees with the central bank, with an agreement to reverse the transaction at a future date. This reduces the exchange-rate risk that often deters small banks from accepting large foreign-currency deposits.

For small banks, the appeal is clear. Diaspora deposits are sticky and often come with lower interest costs than domestic bulk deposits. In a tight liquidity environment, these funds offer a stable base for lending. "The facility makes it viable for us to accept NRI deposits without worrying about currency swings," a banker familiar with the strategy said, speaking on condition of anonymity.

Competition for NRI Money

The Indian diaspora, estimated to remit over $100 billion annually, has become a coveted source of funding. Large banks like State Bank of India and HDFC Bank have long dominated this segment, with dedicated NRI branches and aggressive marketing campaigns. But smaller lenders are now challenging their grip, using the RBI facility to match or even beat the rates offered by their larger peers.

This intensifying competition is reshaping the deposit landscape. NRIs are increasingly shopping around for the best returns, and small banks are eager to oblige. Industry watchers note that the trend could pressure margins at bigger banks, which have traditionally paid lower rates on NRI deposits.

Risks and Rewards

While the strategy offers a lifeline to small banks, it is not without risks. The RBI's swap facility is not an open-ended tool; it comes with costs and conditions. Banks must pay a premium for the swap, and any sharp movement in the rupee could still expose them to losses if the hedge is not perfectly matched.

Moreover, the reliance on diaspora deposits could create a concentration risk. If global economic conditions sour or the rupee weakens sharply, these funds could flow out as quickly as they came in. Regulators have previously cautioned banks against over-reliance on foreign-currency liabilities.

What Lies Ahead

For now, the strategy appears to be paying off. Small banks are reporting a steady uptick in NRI accounts, and the RBI has shown no sign of tightening access to the facility. The central bank, which has been encouraging banks to diversify their funding sources, may view this as a positive development.

As the competition heats up, the coming months will reveal whether small banks can sustain this momentum. The RBI's stance on the swap facility, and the trajectory of the rupee, will be key factors to watch. For the diaspora, the growing options mean more choices and better returns—a win in an otherwise competitive banking landscape.
