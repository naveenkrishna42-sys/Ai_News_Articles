US-Iran Deal Collapse Leaves Oil Markets on Edge as Brent Crosses $90

📅 2026-07-21 📂 Breaking News Original source ↗
Key points
  • Brent crude oil prices surged past $90 per barrel as US-Iran conflict intensified.
  • Diplomatic mediation efforts have so far failed to de-escalate the situation.
  • Stock markets showed mixed reactions, with bonds gaining as investors sought safe havens.

Oil markets are staring at heightened uncertainty after the collapse of the US-Iran nuclear deal triggered a fresh wave of military strikes. Brent crude topped $90 per barrel on Monday, marking a 3% jump as the conflict entered its ninth consecutive day.

The rapid escalation has caught traders off guard. Just weeks ago, hopes of a renewed diplomatic framework had kept prices in check. That optimism has evaporated.

Brent Breaches $90 as Supply Fears Mount

Brent crude futures crossed the psychologically significant $90 mark for the first time in months. The spike came after reports confirmed continued US strikes on Iranian positions, with no sign of a ceasefire.

The Strait of Hormuz, through which about a fifth of the world's oil passes, remains a flashpoint. Any disruption there could send prices soaring further. Analysts warn that the market is pricing in a prolonged conflict.

Mediation Efforts Fail to Cool Tensions

Multiple mediation attempts by regional powers have failed to produce a breakthrough. Oil prices dipped briefly on Monday morning after reports of fresh diplomatic talks, but the relief was short-lived.

“The market is extremely sensitive to any headline,” a Singapore-based trader told Reuters. “But without a concrete truce, the risk premium stays.” The White House has not signalled any intent to halt operations, raising the prospect of a protracted standoff.

Safe-Haven Assets Gain as Equities Wobble

Stock markets in Asia and Europe showed mixed results. While some indices edged higher on bargain buying, bond markets saw a clear flight to safety. US Treasury yields fell as investors sought shelter from geopolitical turbulence.

“When oil jumps this much, it acts like a tax on growth,” said a market strategist in London. “Central banks now face a tougher trade-off between inflation and growth.”

How Exposed Are Oil Markets?

The core question for traders is how much spare capacity exists. OPEC+ has been struggling to meet its own production targets. Iran, under sanctions, has been exporting less than a million barrels per day. A full shutdown of Iranian exports would remove roughly 1.5 million barrels from global supply.

That may not sound catastrophic, but combined with existing Russian sanctions and low global inventories, the margin for error is razor-thin. The International Energy Agency has warned that the market could tip into deficit by the fourth quarter.

India, the world's third-largest oil consumer, is particularly exposed. New Delhi imports over 80% of its crude needs. A sustained rally above $90 will widen the current account deficit and put pressure on the rupee.

For now, all eyes are on Washington and Tehran. Any diplomatic breakthrough would trigger a sharp sell-off in oil. But if the strikes continue, $100 a barrel is no longer unthinkable. Markets are holding their breath.
