India’s Life Expectancy Rises Slightly — Healthier Living Still a Challenge

National Snapshot: Modest Gain in Longevity

India’s life expectancy at birth increased slightly to 70.8 years in 2023, according to the WHO Global Health Estimates released this year. The rise — marginal at just one year over two years — reflects incremental improvements in infant mortality, maternal care, and infectious disease control. However, the pace of progress remains slow compared to high-income nations where life expectancy averages 81 years.

Infant and Maternal Milestones

Infant mortality declined to 27 deaths per 1,000 live births in 2023, down from 32 in 2021. Maternal mortality fell to 97 deaths per 100,000 live births, edging closer to the SDG target of 70. These gains are largely attributed to expanded immunization drives, institutional deliveries, and the Janani Suraksha Yojana scheme.

Disease Burden Shifts: Rise of Non-Communicable Conditions

Non-communicable diseases (NCDs) now account for over 70% of all deaths in India, according to the latest WHO data. Cardiovascular diseases, diabetes, chronic respiratory disorders, and cancer are overtaking traditional killers like tuberculosis and diarrhoea. This epidemiological transition places immense strain on public health infrastructure, which was historically geared toward infectious disease management.

Regional Disparities in Longevity

Wide disparities persist across states. Kerala maintains the highest life expectancy at 75.2 years, while Bihar trails at 65.8 years — a gap of nearly a decade. Southern states generally outperform due to stronger health systems, higher literacy rates, and better sanitation coverage.

Healthier Living: Policy Gaps and Behavioral Barriers

Despite medical advances, behavioral risk factors continue undermining longevity gains. Unhealthy diets high in salt and refined oils contribute to hypertension and heart disease. Tobacco use affects over 200 million adults, while air pollution — both indoor and ambient — claims an estimated 1.6 million lives annually.

Preventive Care Lags Behind

National Family Health Survey-5 data shows that only 14% of adults aged 15 and above are tested annually for hypertension. Diabetes screening coverage is even lower. Public awareness campaigns remain fragmented, and preventive care is often deprioritized in favor of curative treatment within the public health system.

Way Forward: Investment and Integration

India must accelerate health system reforms by boosting public spending beyond the current 4.1% of GDP. The National Health Protection Scheme (AB-AYUSHMAN) covers over 500 million beneficiaries but excludes many NCD treatments. Experts advocate for integrating NCD screening into primary health centers and launching population-wide salt reduction initiatives.
