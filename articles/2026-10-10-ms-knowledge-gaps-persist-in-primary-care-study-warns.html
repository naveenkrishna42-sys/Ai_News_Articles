MS Knowledge Gaps Persist in Primary Care, Study Warns

Why primary care still struggles with multiple sclerosis

Multiple sclerosis (MS) is often introduced to patients through non-specific early symptoms—fatigue, tingling, vision changes, or balance problems. In primary care, where clinicians manage broad differential diagnoses, that uncertainty can translate into inconsistent recognition patterns. A report published by the European Medical Journal highlights that knowledge gaps persist, even as MS care has matured with better diagnostic imaging, disease-modifying therapies, and symptom support.

For buyers of healthcare systems—hospital networks, insurers, and clinic administrators—the practical issue is not whether MS is complex. It’s whether front-line teams can reliably identify red flags, coordinate referrals, and implement guideline-aligned early management while diagnosis is underway. When the handoffs are weak, patients spend months cycling through tests rather than receiving timely neurologist assessment and symptom planning.

Where the knowledge gaps show up in real-world practice

MS care has two timelines: the diagnostic timeline and the lived-experience timeline. Primary care is involved in both. Clinicians often see patients before definitive MRI findings or neurology confirmation, meaning early decisions—what to test, how urgently to refer, and how to document neurological deficits—carry long downstream effects.

Common gap areas reported across MS-focused reviews include: differentiating MS relapses from mimics (such as migraine variants or peripheral neuropathies), understanding when to escalate to urgent neurology referral, and selecting evidence-based supportive treatments for symptoms like spasticity, neuropathic pain, bladder issues, and mood changes. Another recurring weakness is familiarity with the principles of relapse assessment—particularly distinguishing new neurological symptoms from unrelated fluctuations.

Even when clinicians recognize possible MS, fragmented pathways can blunt impact. If referral triggers are unclear, or if primary care teams are unsure which information neurologists need (timeline of symptoms, objective exam findings, prior imaging), patients may experience preventable delays.

Buyer implications: delays are not just time lost

From a commissioning and operations standpoint, diagnostic delay has measurable consequences. Patients with unresolved neurological symptoms frequently present repeatedly to primary care and urgent services, increasing appointment churn and investigations. Meanwhile, unmanaged symptoms can reduce work capacity, increase caregiver burden, and drive avoidable escalation to emergency care.

Systems that invest in primary care training and standardized referral documentation often reduce variation across clinicians. That “variation cost” shows up as inconsistent referral urgency, uneven patient education, and inconsistent follow-up after initial investigations.

What evidence-based MS primary care should include

Guideline-aligned primary care support does not replace neurology care. It prepares patients for it. Effective practice includes early red-flag identification, structured documentation, and a reliable handoff to specialist pathways.

Clinicians should be able to: recognize suggestive patterns of demyelinating disease (for example, focal neurological deficits with a relevant temporal pattern), assess severity and functional impact, and communicate what is happening diagnostically in plain language. Equally important, primary care should manage symptomatic treatment while diagnosis is being clarified—because symptom relief is not contingent on a final label.

For administration leaders, the most actionable interventions are operational: referral criteria checklists, shared care templates, and brief education modules paired with audit/feedback. These are low-lift compared with system-wide redesigns and can be implemented within months.

Practical benchmarks you can use now

There are no universal national “pass rates” for MS knowledge in primary care, but you can benchmark process indicators that reflect competence. Consider tracking referral timeliness, completeness of documentation, and follow-up rates after initial MS workup. The goal is not to speed every case regardless of evidence—it’s to reduce avoidable friction when MS is plausible.

Training and protocol changes that actually help

The most sustainable fixes target both knowledge and workflow. A short, focused education program—paired with templates and referral pathways—can improve consistency without requiring clinicians to become MS subspecialists. The key is that training must be operational: what to ask, what to examine, what to document, and when to escalate.

Audit and feedback tighten the loop. For example, review recent referrals for completeness and appropriateness, then revise checklists accordingly. Clinics that share aggregated lessons with clinicians tend to reduce repeat errors because the “why” is visible and measurable.

  • Pros: Standardized referral triggers reduce delays when MS is plausible and improve specialist readiness.
  • Pros: Symptom-focused interim management helps patients function while diagnosis is underway.
  • Pros: Templates improve documentation quality and reduce administrative back-and-forth.
  • Cons: Training alone may not change outcomes without workflow changes and audit/feedback.
  • Cons: Over-urgent referral without clear criteria can strain neurology capacity.

What patients and systems should demand next

Patients deserve consistent messaging: suspected MS should lead to structured evaluation, not uncertainty cycles. Systems should provide primary care teams with clear referral criteria, fast access routes where needed, and a shared-care approach for symptoms.

The European Medical Journal’s warning about persistent knowledge gaps is not just a clinical concern—it’s a delivery-of-care concern. When primary care is equipped with practical pathways, the benefits extend beyond diagnosis timelines to quality of life, continuity of support, and more efficient use of specialist services.
