Sebi's CAS Proposal: Why Expiry-Day Pricing Is Back on the Table

SEBI has proposed a revised Closing Auction Session (CAS) framework and, crucially, offered two alternative options for settling contracts on expiry day. The move follows sustained industry feedback that the current closing-price mechanism — largely anchored to the volume-weighted average price (VWAP) of the final trading window — is prone to distortion during the closing minutes.

The consultation matters because index and stock derivatives expire weekly and monthly, and every expiry settles against a single reference price. If that reference price can be nudged, the entire risk-transfer chain — hedgers, arbitrageurs, market makers and retail option buyers — absorbs the cost.

The Two Options Under Consideration

Option A: Closing Auction Session With a Defined Order Book

Under this model, trading in the underlying cash segment pauses briefly before close, then reopens for a short, rule-bound auction. Orders are matched at a single clearing price that maximises executable volume. This concentrates liquidity into one transparent window, reducing the ability of a single large order to skew the settlement reference.

Option B: Extended VWAP With Safeguards

The second option retains a VWAP-style calculation but widens the observation window and introduces price-band and volume-ratio filters. Trades executed outside a defined band, or abnormally large prints relative to recent averages, would be excluded or capped in the final computation.

Real-World Impact for Traders and Investors

For retail options traders, the practical effect shows up in slippage. A cleaner closing print narrows the gap between theoretical and realised settlement value, which matters most for near-the-money strikes where a few basis points decide whether a position expires in or out of the money.

For institutional desks running delta-hedged books, the change alters end-of-day execution cost. An auction-based close typically offers deeper liquidity at one price point but demands precise order timing; a safeguarded VWAP spreads execution but leaves residual uncertainty about which trades count.

Key Real-World Highlights

  • Two distinct CAS options give SEBI room to calibrate based on liquidity depth across indices versus single stocks.
  • Auction-based settlement generally improves price discovery but can penalise traders who cannot participate in the short window.
  • Safeguarded VWAP is operationally simpler for brokers but relies on filter thresholds that must be tuned carefully.
  • Market makers may need to revise quoting algorithms, raising short-term technology costs.
  • Arbitrage spreads between cash and futures could compress if the closing reference becomes more reliable.
  • Final implementation timelines remain unconfirmed until the consultation concludes.

Pricing and Value Context

The commercial stakes are large. India's derivatives segment turns over tens of lakh crore in notional value each month, and expiry-day activity accounts for a disproportionate share of that volume. Even a marginal reduction in settlement distortion translates into crores of rupees saved annually across the ecosystem in hedging error and adverse selection costs.

Brokers, meanwhile, face a compliance and technology bill. Exchanges must upgrade matching engines to support an auction phase, and members must test order-type compatibility. Those costs will ultimately feed into transaction charges, making the choice between the two options a commercial decision, not merely a technical one.

What Buyers and Traders Should Watch

Until SEBI issues final rules, traders should avoid building strategies that depend on a specific closing methodology. Track the consultation outcome, review exchange circulars on mock-auction sessions, and stress-test expiry-day positions under both models. The framework that finally emerges will shape execution costs and risk for every participant in India's derivatives market.
