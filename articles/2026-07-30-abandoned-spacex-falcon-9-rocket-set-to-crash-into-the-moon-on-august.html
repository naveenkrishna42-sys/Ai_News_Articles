Abandoned SpaceX Falcon 9 rocket set to crash into the Moon on August 5

📅 2026-07-30 📂 Science Original source ↗
Key points
  • The Falcon 9 upper stage was left in a chaotic orbit after a 2015 mission.
  • Impact is expected on August 5 at a speed of over 9,000 km/h.
  • Scientists will study the debris plume to learn more about the Moon's composition.

An abandoned SpaceX rocket stage is hurtling toward the Moon and will crash into the lunar surface on August 5. The Falcon 9 upper stage, roughly the size of a five-storey building, has been drifting in space since 2015.

Astronomers say the impact will carve a new crater into the Moon's far side. No one will see it happen from Earth, but orbital trackers are watching the final trajectory closely.

How the rocket got lost

The rocket stage was used to launch the DSCOVR climate satellite for the National Oceanic and Atmospheric Administration. After deploying the satellite, the upper stage ran low on fuel and was left in a highly elliptical orbit.

Ground controllers did not have enough propellant to guide it back into Earth's atmosphere or send it toward a stable graveyard orbit. Over the years, gravitational nudges from the Earth, Moon, and Sun pushed the stage onto a collision course.

Bill Gray, an independent astronomer who tracks near-Earth objects, first identified the impending impact. He told Astronomy Magazine that the object's path became clear only recently.

What happens on impact

The Falcon 9 upper stage will hit the Moon at a speed of more than 9,000 kilometres per hour. The collision will create a fresh crater, likely a few metres wide, and kick up a plume of dust and debris.

Scientists are not entirely certain what to expect. The rocket body is a hollow cylinder of aluminium alloy, so it may crumple differently than a dense asteroid would.

Several lunar orbiters, including India's Chandrayaan-2 and NASA's Lunar Reconnaissance Orbiter, may later be tasked with imaging the new crater. However, the impact site is on the far side, so direct observation from Earth is impossible.

No disposal rules for deep space

The incident highlights a regulatory gap. The United States Federal Communications Commission recently introduced a new 'cislunar' category for licences, but it does not yet include disposal mandates for objects beyond geostationary orbit.

Current space debris rules focus on Earth orbit. Rockets and satellites sent toward the Moon or deeper into space have no binding end-of-life requirements. This SpaceX stage is the first known unintentional lunar impact by a man-made object, but it may not be the last.

Some experts argue that as lunar missions multiply, the lack of disposal rules could lead to more uncontrolled crashes. Private companies and space agencies are already planning dozens of Moon landers and orbiters in the coming decade.

For now, the Falcon 9 impact is a scientific opportunity. Researchers will study the debris plume's composition and behaviour to learn more about the Moon's surface and subsurface. The data could help plan future human landings under NASA's Artemis programme.

The crash is set for August 5. Orbital trackers will confirm the exact time in the days before impact. No one on Earth is at risk, but the event serves as a reminder that what goes up does not always come down neatly.
