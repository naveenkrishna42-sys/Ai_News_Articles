Apple iPhone prices may rise as memory costs surge: report

Key points
  • Report suggests up to $100 price increase for iPhone 18 lineup
  • Rising NAND and DRAM costs driven by AI demand and supply constraints
  • India may face additional import duties, amplifying the hike
  • Apple likely to absorb some cost to protect market share

The Cost Pressure Behind the Price Tag

Apple is reportedly weighing a price increase for the upcoming iPhone 18 series, with some estimates pointing to a $100 hike across the lineup. The culprit: surging memory chip costs, particularly NAND flash and DRAM, which have climbed sharply over the past year due to AI-driven demand and constrained supply.

This is not a fresh problem. Memory prices have been volatile for years, but the current spike is different in scale. Suppliers like Samsung and SK Hynix have shifted production towards high-bandwidth memory (HBM) used in AI accelerators, squeezing supply for consumer devices. Analysts estimate that memory costs now account for a significantly larger share of a smartphone's bill of materials than ever before.

Why a $100 Hike Matters More in India

For Indian consumers, the potential increase is doubly painful. Beyond the dollar-denominated rise, import duties on smartphones in India already range between 15-20%, and the rupee's weakness against the dollar adds another layer. A $100 bump could translate to roughly ₹9,000-10,000 more at retail, pushing the base iPhone 18 model closer to the ₹80,000 mark.

This is a critical juncture for Apple in India. The company has been aggressively courting the Indian market, with local assembly via partners like Foxconn and Wistron helping to avoid some duties. However, a steep price hike could dampen the momentum just as Apple is gaining traction among premium buyers. The iPhone 17 series saw strong initial sales in India, but a repeat performance is not guaranteed if prices climb too high.

Market Dynamics: Absorbing vs. Passing On Costs

Apple has historically been cautious about price increases. The company prefers to absorb minor cost fluctuations to maintain its premium positioning and customer loyalty. Yet, the scale of the memory cost surge might force a different approach. According to industry insiders, Apple is evaluating a tiered strategy: absorbing part of the cost while passing on the rest, resulting in a smaller net increase than the full $100.

This is a delicate balancing act. Competitors like Samsung and Google are also facing the same memory cost pressures, but they have more flexibility in their pricing structures. Apple's ecosystem lock-in gives it some cushion, but there is a threshold beyond which even loyal customers will balk.

What This Means for Buyers

For those planning to upgrade, the timing matters. If the price hike materializes, the iPhone 17 series could become a more attractive option, especially as retailers offer discounts to clear inventory before the new launch. However, waiting for the iPhone 18 might still be worthwhile for the rumored camera and battery improvements, provided you can stomach the price.

The Bigger Picture: AI Is Driving Up Costs

The root cause of this price pressure is not just a cyclical bump. The explosion of on-device AI features, like Apple Intelligence, requires more memory. The iPhone 18 is expected to feature larger RAM and faster storage, which further increases costs. This is a structural shift, not a temporary blip.

Some analysts argue that a $100 increase is justified given the added capabilities. Others counter that it risks pricing out a significant chunk of the market. The truth likely lies in between. Apple's brand strength allows it to charge a premium, but it cannot ignore the reality of consumer budgets, especially in emerging markets like India.

  • Pros of price hike: Maintains margins, funds R&D, signals premium quality
  • Cons: Hurts demand, especially in price-sensitive markets, benefits competitors

Ultimately, Apple's decision will be a test of its pricing power. If the company can push through a $100 increase without losing customers, it sets a precedent for the industry. If not, it will have to find other ways to manage costs, perhaps by cutting corners elsewhere or expanding local manufacturing in India to offset duties.
