How India's crime gangs expanded operations worldwide

📅 2026-07-17 📂 Crime & Law Original source ↗
Key points
  • Indian crime syndicates have established cells in North America, Europe, Southeast Asia and the Middle East.
  • Criminal activities now include cyber fraud, drug smuggling and money laundering, often using encrypted communication.
  • Law enforcement agencies globally are struggling to keep pace with the transnational nature of these networks.

A quiet expansion beyond borders

Indian organised crime is no longer confined to the streets of Mumbai, Delhi or Kolkata. Over the past decade, syndicates once known for local extortion and contract killings have built a sprawling international footprint. Intelligence reports now place Indian criminal networks in more than 30 countries, from Canada and the United States to the United Arab Emirates and Thailand.

The shift has been gradual but deliberate. As domestic law enforcement tightened surveillance and crackdowns in the 2010s, gang leaders began relocating their operations abroad. Today, these networks function as transnational enterprises, often with legal front businesses masking their illicit revenue streams.

From street crime to high-tech fraud

The nature of their business has changed dramatically. Traditional activities like land grabbing and illegal liquor sales have given way to sophisticated cyber fraud, drug trafficking and money laundering. Criminal cells in places like Dubai and Singapore now coordinate online investment scams that target victims across Europe and North America.

Law enforcement officials say encrypted messaging apps have made communication nearly invisible. Gang leaders based in countries with weak extradition treaties issue instructions to operatives in India and elsewhere without ever meeting them face to face. This has made dismantling entire networks far harder than arresting individual gang members.

Key hubs and routes

Three regions have emerged as critical nodes for Indian organised crime abroad. The Gulf countries, particularly the UAE, serve as financial hubs where illicit cash is laundered through real estate and gold trading. Southeast Asia, especially Thailand and Malaysia, has become a base for drug trafficking and human smuggling. Canada and the US are now significant destinations for cyber fraud proceeds and narcotics.

The drug trade has seen a notable shift. Indian gangs are no longer just domestic suppliers. They are now linking up with Latin American cartels to move cocaine into Europe and Asia, using India's coastline and ports as transit points. The Narcotics Control Bureau has flagged a rise in synthetic drug production in Gujarat and Maharashtra meant for export.

Money laundering gets sophisticated

Dirty money from these operations flows through a maze of shell companies, cryptocurrency exchanges and trade-based laundering. Investigators have traced funds from call centre scams in Delhi to bank accounts in Hong Kong and real estate purchases in London. The use of hawala, an informal money transfer system, remains widespread but is now complemented by digital currencies.

One emerging pattern involves gangsters buying small businesses in countries with lax due diligence requirements. A pizza shop in Toronto or a grocery store in Sydney can serve as a front for channelling illegal funds into the legitimate economy. Authorities admit they are often months behind such schemes.

Challenges for law enforcement

Indian police agencies have traditionally operated within state boundaries, making cross-border coordination difficult even domestically. Internationally, they rely on Interpol red notices and mutual legal assistance treaties, which can take years to yield results. Many countries where these gangs operate have limited intelligence-sharing agreements with India.

Another hurdle is the use of encrypted digital evidence. Servers hosting fraudulent websites may be located in jurisdictions that do not respond to Indian requests. Even when evidence is obtained, courts in host countries sometimes deem it inadmissible due to procedural differences.

What happens next depends on how quickly international cooperation can catch up. Several countries have begun dedicated task forces focused on Indian organised crime, but the networks keep adapting. The next wave may involve deeper integration with global drug cartels and state-sponsored cyber actors — a shift that would make today's challenges look modest.
