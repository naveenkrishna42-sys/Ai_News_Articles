What Gaza “before and after” imagery shows

When media outlets publish photo series comparing Gaza before the war with the same locations after months of bombardment, the visual record is stark: streets that once held homes, shops, and daily routines appear fragmented or erased. These “before-and-after” comparisons, often supported by satellite imagery, are among the few ways to assess broad changes over time when access to affected areas is restricted.

However, the images do not tell a single story by themselves. Analysts, journalists, and researchers must translate pixels into categories such as “damaged,” “destroyed,” or “partially affected.” Those labels can shift depending on resolution, the criteria used, and whether the assessment covers entire districts or specific urban footprints.

How analysts estimate destruction—and why numbers differ

Multiple reports cited in the original feed reference high damage levels, including an analysis claiming that almost 75% of buildings in Gaza have been damaged or destroyed since October 7, 2023. In practice, that kind of figure is not a direct measurement of rubble alone; it is derived from image interpretation across a defined period, geography, and classification system.

For decision-makers relying on these estimates, the key is to understand what the metric actually represents. “Building affected” can include homes with roof loss but intact walls, structures partially hit, and sites rendered unsafe by surrounding damage. Likewise, the term “Gaza” may be treated differently by different groups—some use administrative boundaries, others use mapped urban areas, and some omit areas with limited imagery quality.

In other words, the visual evidence is powerful, but it is still an inference. Treat headline percentages as a starting point for planning, not as a final accounting.

Practical benchmarks for interpreting “before-and-after” photos

If you are using such imagery for humanitarian, rebuilding, or policy work, you can apply a few consistent checks. Look for metadata and methodology: what satellite source was used, what resolution is available, and what time stamps define “before” and “after.” Then compare the same neighbourhood across multiple sources to reduce the risk that a single analyst’s classification dominates the narrative.

Finally, connect the imagery to on-the-ground priorities: where are schools, healthcare facilities, and water infrastructure located relative to the highest destruction zones? Photos can quantify loss, but the operational value comes from mapping impact to needs.

What “erased landmarks” means for everyday life

City-scale destruction is not only about collapsed buildings; it disrupts the infrastructure that makes a city function. A neighbourhood can appear as a mass of rubble while still hiding key details: how water lines ran beneath streets, where power distribution nodes were located, and which blocks were market corridors. When those systems fail, recovery becomes slower even in areas where structures are not fully destroyed.

That is why photo essays often focus on landmarks of everyday life—markets, residential blocks, and public spaces—rather than only iconic sites. The disappearance of familiar places signals a breakdown of social and economic continuity, not just physical damage.

  • Housing loss: High “destroyed” or “damaged” rates translate into displacement and long-term reconstruction costs.
  • Service interruption: Even partially damaged buildings can be unsafe, leaving gaps in schooling and healthcare.
  • Infrastructure knock-on effects: Damage to roads and utilities can make some areas effectively uninhabitable.
  • Uncertainty in classification: Photo and satellite assessments can diverge based on definitions and resolution limits.

Buyer’s-eye lens: how to use photo evidence responsibly

Borrowing a commercial buyer’s mindset can make the evidence more usable. Before acting on a headline number, check the “spec sheet” of the assessment: the observation dates, coverage area, and classification approach. In procurement terms, you wouldn’t accept a component listing without tolerances—similarly, you should not treat a destruction percentage as a universal truth without method transparency.

For agencies planning relief logistics, shelter procurement, or reconstruction contracts, this means demanding maps that break down damage categories at a granular level—block or district rather than only city-wide. It also means pairing imagery with independent verification channels: field reports where possible, remote-sensing overlays, and consistent referencing to reduce blind spots.

Why this matters beyond the images

Photos that show a city transformed by war are more than documentation; they are inputs into decisions that affect lives for years. The scale of damage—whether expressed as “almost 75%” or other closely related estimates—should drive planning for shelter, debris management, and safe restoration of essential services.

At the same time, evidence must be handled with care. Different assessments can produce different percentages, and “damage” is not identical to “uninhabitable” or “fully destroyed.” The most responsible use of before-and-after imagery is to pair it with transparent methods and targeted follow-through: maps for where help is needed, not just visuals that express loss.
