RBI's MDR Push: A Turning Point for UPI Economics

The Reserve Bank of India has backed the introduction of Merchant Discount Rate (MDR) on large-value Unified Payments Interface (UPI) transactions, stating it could help expand acceptance. This marks a significant shift from the zero-MDR regime that fuelled UPI's explosive growth but left banks and payment aggregators bearing the cost of processing high-ticket payments. The central bank's stance, reported by The Economic Times, signals a pragmatic approach to sustaining UPI's infrastructure as transaction values climb.

Why MDR on Large-Value UPI Matters

UPI currently processes over 10 billion transactions a month, but a vast majority are low-value person-to-merchant (P2M) payments. Banks have long argued that the zero-MDR model makes high-value merchant transactions unviable, as the cost of processing, fraud monitoring, and settlement is disproportionately high. MDR on large-value transactions would allow acquirers and issuers to earn a fee, incentivising them to invest in robust infrastructure and merchant onboarding, especially in sectors like electronics, travel, and healthcare where ticket sizes are substantial.

What Counts as 'Large-Value'?

While the RBI has not specified a threshold, industry experts suggest transactions above ₹2,000 to ₹5,000 could be considered large-value. The final framework may exempt small merchants or essential categories to protect financial inclusion. A tiered MDR structure, similar to cards, is likely: lower rates for debit and higher for credit, with caps to prevent abuse.

Merchant Economics: Cost vs. Benefit

For merchants, MDR means an additional cost per transaction, typically 0.5% to 1.5% for UPI, depending on the category. However, the benefits include better success rates, faster settlements, and reduced dependency on cash. Large merchants already pay MDR on card transactions; UPI MDR would level the playing field. Small merchants may get exemptions or subsidised rates to avoid discouraging digital adoption.

Consumer Impact: No Direct Fee, But Watch for Pricing

Consumers are unlikely to pay MDR directly, as it is a merchant fee. However, some merchants might surcharge large UPI payments or offer discounts for other payment modes. The RBI is expected to mandate no surcharging to protect users. Overall, consumers could see improved acceptance for high-value purchases, as more merchants adopt UPI for big-ticket items.

Pros and Cons of MDR on Large-Value UPI

  • Pros: Sustainable economics for banks; better infrastructure and success rates; expanded acceptance in high-value categories; level playing field with cards.
  • Cons: Additional cost for merchants; risk of surcharging if not regulated; potential resistance from small businesses; complexity in implementation.

Conclusion: A Balanced Evolution

The RBI's support for MDR on large-value UPI reflects a maturing payments ecosystem. By targeting only high-value transactions, the central bank aims to preserve UPI's affordability for everyday payments while ensuring its long-term viability. Merchants should prepare for a tiered pricing model, and consumers can expect wider acceptance without bearing direct costs. The final framework will be crucial in balancing innovation with inclusion.
