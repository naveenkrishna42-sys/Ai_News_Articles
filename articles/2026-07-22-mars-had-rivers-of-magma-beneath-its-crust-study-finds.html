Mars Had Rivers of Magma Beneath Its Crust, Study Finds

📅 2026-07-22 📂 Science Original source ↗
Key points
  • Study published in Nature Astronomy suggests magma rivers existed under Mars' crust.
  • The discovery provides new clues about the planet's geological history and volcanic activity.
  • Findings may help understand Mars' potential to have supported life in the past.

A Fiery Past Hidden Beneath the Surface

Mars was not always the cold, dusty desert we see today. New research suggests that, deep beneath its crust, rivers of magma once bubbled and flowed. The findings, published in the journal Nature Astronomy, are reshaping how scientists understand the Red Planet's geological history.

For years, evidence pointed to volcanic activity on Mars—massive shield volcanoes like Olympus Mons are proof. But this new study goes deeper, literally. It indicates that molten rock moved under the planet's surface in a way similar to Earth's mantle plumes.

What the Study Reveals

Researchers analysed data from multiple Mars missions, looking at gravitational anomalies and surface features. They found patterns consistent with ancient magma channels. These channels, they argue, were part of a system that transported molten rock from the planet's interior to its crust.

The study's authors believe these magma rivers were active billions of years ago, during a period when Mars was geologically more dynamic. This period likely coincided with the planet's now-extinct magnetic field, which may have protected a thicker atmosphere and liquid water on the surface.

Implications for Past Life

The discovery is significant for the search for life on Mars. Magma activity can create hydrothermal systems—hot, mineral-rich water environments that are prime candidates for microbial life. On Earth, such systems teem with life, even in extreme conditions.

While the study does not prove life existed, it strengthens the case that Mars once had the right ingredients. Subsurface heat and water, driven by magma, could have created habitable niches. The findings give scientists new targets to explore, especially where ancient magma channels meet the surface.

A Planet of Surprises

Mars continues to defy simple narratives. The idea that it had a molten, churning interior for a significant part of its history challenges older models. Those models saw Mars as a planet that cooled quickly and became geologically dead.

The Nature Astronomy study adds to a growing body of evidence that Mars had a more complex and active past. Each new piece of data forces a rethink of how rocky planets evolve, and what conditions are necessary for life to emerge.

Scientists are now keen to refine their models. The next step is to look for surface signatures of these ancient magma rivers—perhaps in the form of mineral deposits or altered rock layers. Future missions, including sample return efforts, may be able to confirm the findings directly.

For now, the image of Mars as a static world is fading. Beneath its rust-coloured surface, rivers of fire once ran, shaping the planet we see today.
