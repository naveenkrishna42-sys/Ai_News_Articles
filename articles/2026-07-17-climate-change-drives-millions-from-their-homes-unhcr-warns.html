Climate change drives millions from their homes, UNHCR warns

📅 2026-07-17 📂 Environment Original source ↗
Key points
  • Climate change and disasters are forcing millions of people to leave their homes worldwide.
  • UNHCR emphasizes that displacement is often internal but can cross borders, requiring legal protections.
  • The agency calls for urgent action to address the root causes and support affected communities.

The UN Refugee Agency (UNHCR) has issued a stark warning: climate change and disasters are uprooting millions of people across the globe. In a recent report, the agency highlights how extreme weather events, rising sea levels, and environmental degradation are driving displacement at alarming rates.

This is not a distant threat. It is happening now, from the flood plains of Bangladesh to the drought-stricken regions of sub-Saharan Africa. UNHCR notes that many of those forced to move are among the world's most vulnerable, often lacking the resources to adapt or recover.

The scale of the crisis

According to the agency, climate-related disasters have displaced an average of over 20 million people annually in recent years. This number is expected to rise as global temperatures continue to climb. The report stresses that displacement is not just a future possibility but a current reality.

Most of this movement is internal. People leave their homes for safer areas within their own countries. However, UNHCR warns that cross-border displacement is also increasing, creating new challenges for international law and refugee protection frameworks.

Small island nations face an existential threat. Entire communities may need to relocate as sea levels encroach on their land. Without urgent action, the agency cautions, these displacements could become permanent, leading to what some term 'climate refugees'.

Who is most at risk?

The report identifies specific groups as particularly vulnerable. These include communities in low-lying coastal zones, arid regions, and areas dependent on agriculture. Women, children, the elderly, and indigenous populations often bear the brunt of these disasters.

Conflict and fragility compound the problem. In countries already struggling with instability, climate shocks can trigger further displacement, straining limited resources and escalating tensions. UNHCR points out that the intersection of climate change and conflict creates a vicious cycle.

For example, in the Sahel region of Africa, desertification and resource scarcity have fueled competition between herders and farmers, leading to violence and displacement. The agency calls for integrated responses that address both environmental and security concerns.

What needs to be done

UNHCR is urging governments to take immediate steps. First, nations must invest in disaster risk reduction and climate adaptation. Early warning systems, resilient infrastructure, and sustainable land management can help communities stay in place.

Second, legal frameworks need updating. The 1951 Refugee Convention does not explicitly cover climate-related displacement. UNHCR advocates for stronger protections, including temporary visas, humanitarian status, and resettlement programs for those forced to cross borders.

Third, international cooperation is critical. Climate change knows no borders. Wealthy nations, which have historically contributed most to emissions, have a responsibility to support affected countries. The agency calls for increased funding for adaptation and loss-and-damage mechanisms.

Finally, the report stresses the importance of data collection. Better tracking of displacement patterns can inform policy and help target aid effectively. Without robust data, responses risk being reactive rather than proactive.

The UNHCR's message is clear: climate change is a displacement crisis. The world must act now to prevent it from becoming a catastrophe.

What to watch: The upcoming COP29 climate summit will be a key test. Nations are expected to discuss a new climate finance goal, which could include provisions for displacement. The outcome will shape how the international community responds to this growing challenge.
