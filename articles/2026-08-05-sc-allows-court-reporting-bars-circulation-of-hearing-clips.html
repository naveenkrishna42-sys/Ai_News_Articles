SC allows court reporting, bars circulation of hearing clips

📅 2026-08-05 📂 India Original source ↗
Key points
  • Supreme Court permits media to report court proceedings
  • Circulation of audio-video clips of hearings barred
  • Order aims to balance transparency with judicial decorum

Court's clarification on media reporting

The Supreme Court on Wednesday clarified that news organisations are free to report court proceedings, but they cannot circulate audio-video clips of hearings. The order, issued by a bench, seeks to strike a balance between the public's right to know and the need to maintain the dignity of judicial proceedings.

The clarification comes amid concerns that a blanket ban on reporting would stifle transparency. The court made it clear that its earlier directive was not intended to gag the press, but to regulate the use of recorded material.

What the order says

According to the order, media houses can continue to provide textual reports, including live blogs and written summaries, of court hearings. However, the 'utilisation' of audio-video recordings of proceedings—whether for broadcast, streaming, or sharing on social media—is not permitted.

The distinction is crucial. While reporting remains unrestricted, the clips themselves are considered part of the court's record and cannot be treated as freely available content. The bench emphasised that this restriction applies to all forms of circulation, including on digital platforms.

Why the restriction?

The court's decision appears rooted in concerns about selective editing and misrepresentation. A short clip, taken out of context, could distort the arguments or observations made during a hearing. The bench noted that full proceedings are already available in the form of official transcripts and judgments.

This is not the first time the court has grappled with the issue. Earlier instances of leaked audio or video from sensitive hearings have prompted the judiciary to revisit its guidelines. The current order aims to preempt such incidents while allowing the media to perform its watchdog role.

Reactions and implications

Legal experts have welcomed the clarification, noting that it removes ambiguity for journalists. "The court has drawn a clear line—report freely, but don't repurpose the raw material," said a senior advocate familiar with the matter. Media bodies, which had sought clarity, are expected to comply with the new guidelines.

The order does not specify penalties for violations, but it is likely to be treated as a binding directive. Repeat offenders could face contempt proceedings, though the court has not explicitly said so.

What to watch

The Supreme Court is expected to formalise these guidelines into a comprehensive framework in the coming months. Until then, newsrooms will have to adapt their practices, ensuring that their reporting remains robust while respecting the new restrictions on audio-video clips.
