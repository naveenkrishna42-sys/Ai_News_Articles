RBI Governor on UPI MDR: 'Someone Has to Pay the Cost'

📅 2026-08-05 📂 Business Original source ↗
Key points
  • RBI governor responds to MDR proposal for UPI transactions above Rs 2,000, saying costs must be paid by someone.
  • India's new taxation bill paves way for merchant fees on digital payments, reversing zero-MDR regime.
  • Debate heats up over who should bear UPI costs—merchants, customers, or the government—as RBI says it has funds to run the platform.

RBI Governor Breaks Silence on UPI Charges

The Reserve Bank of India (RBI) governor has waded into the heated debate over levying merchant discount rate (MDR) on UPI transactions above Rs 2,000, with a stark reminder: someone has to foot the bill. His comments come as the Centre's new taxation bill proposes to reintroduce fees on digital payments, a move that could reshape India's cashless ecosystem.

Speaking on the proposal, the governor said, "Someone has to pay the cost." The remark underscores the financial reality of running UPI, which has grown into the world's largest real-time payment system, processing billions of transactions monthly at near-zero cost to users.

What the New Taxation Bill Says

The government's latest taxation bill, currently before Parliament, includes provisions that could allow charging MDR on UPI transactions above Rs 2,000. If passed, this would mark a significant departure from the current zero-MDR regime, introduced to boost digital adoption after demonetisation.

Merchants, especially small shopkeepers and street vendors, are wary. For them, UPI has become the default mode of payment, and any fee could eat into already thin margins. Consumer groups, too, have voiced concerns that costs might be passed on to customers, undermining the "free" appeal of UPI.

Who Bears the Cost?

The RBI governor's statement puts the spotlight on a question that has no easy answer. Banks and payment processors argue that they incur costs for every transaction—infrastructure, security, and settlement—and these cannot be absorbed indefinitely. The government, meanwhile, has subsidised the ecosystem so far, but the bill's provisions suggest a shift in approach.

Interestingly, the RBI itself has clarified that it has sufficient funds to run the UPI platform without charging merchants or customers. This raises the question: is the push for MDR driven by fiscal necessity or a broader policy rethink?

Industry Reactions and Implications

The proposal has split opinion. Large fintech players, including PhonePe and Google Pay, have welcomed the move as a step towards sustainability. They argue that a nominal fee could improve service quality and reduce fraud. Smaller players, however, fear it could kill the UPI revolution, which has been a global success story.

For the common user, the impact may be minimal if the threshold of Rs 2,000 is retained. But merchants—from kirana stores to e-commerce platforms—could see their transaction costs rise, potentially leading to higher prices for goods and services.

What Happens Next

The bill is yet to be passed, and the RBI has not issued a formal directive on MDR. The governor's comments, however, signal that discussions are underway. If the proposal moves forward, the central bank will likely set a cap on charges to protect smaller merchants.

For now, the message is clear: free isn't truly free. As the debate unfolds, all eyes will be on Parliament and the RBI's next move.
