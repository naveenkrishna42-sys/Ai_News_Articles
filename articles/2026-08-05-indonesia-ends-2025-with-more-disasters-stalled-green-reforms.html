Indonesia ends 2025 with more disasters, stalled green reforms

📅 2026-08-05 📂 Environment Original source ↗
Key points
  • Indonesia recorded an increase in natural disasters in 2025, from floods to landslides.
  • Environmental reforms have seen little progress despite growing climate risks.
  • Experts urge stronger action as the country heads into 2026.
  • Government pledges remain unmet, with enforcement and funding challenges.
  • Observers call for integrated disaster risk reduction and environmental policy.

A year of mounting disasters

Indonesia enters the final days of 2025 with a stark record: natural disasters have risen sharply across the archipelago. From flash floods in Sumatra to landslides in Java, communities have borne the brunt of extreme weather events that scientists link to a warming climate.

The National Disaster Management Authority (BNPB) has not yet released its full-year tally, but regional reports indicate a clear upward trend. In December alone, several provinces declared emergency status after torrential rains triggered mudslides and river overflows.

Local officials say the pattern is becoming routine. "Every rainy season now brings new emergencies," a district disaster officer in West Java told reporters. "We are always responding, never preparing."

Reforms stuck in limbo

Meanwhile, environmental reform has stalled. Key legislation on forest protection and peatland restoration remains mired in parliamentary debate. The government's own climate targets, set under the Paris Agreement, appear increasingly out of reach.

Activists point to weak enforcement of existing laws. Illegal logging and land clearing for palm oil continue in protected areas, often with impunity. A 2024 audit by the Supreme Audit Agency found that forest rehabilitation funds were underutilised by nearly 40 percent.

"The gap between policy and practice is widening," said a Jakarta-based environmental analyst. "We have the rules, but not the will to implement them."

Economic and human toll

The human cost has been significant. In 2025, at least 300 people died in disaster-related incidents, according to preliminary figures from BNPB. Thousands more were displaced, many for weeks or months.

Economic losses have also climbed. The agriculture sector, a mainstay for millions, suffered repeated crop failures due to floods and droughts. The government has allocated emergency relief funds, but reconstruction remains slow and uneven.

Infrastructure damage has been particularly severe in rural areas, where roads and bridges are often the first to wash away. Villagers in East Nusa Tenggara told reporters they waited over a month for aid after a landslide cut off their community.

Root causes unaddressed

Environmental experts argue that disasters are not purely natural. Deforestation, poor land-use planning, and inadequate drainage systems amplify the impact of heavy rains. Indonesia loses over 100,000 hectares of primary forest each year, one of the highest rates in the tropics.

Peatland degradation is another critical issue. Drained and burned peat releases vast amounts of carbon, fuelling both climate change and hazardous haze. The government's peat restoration agency was disbanded in 2023, and its successor has yet to show tangible results.

"We are paying the price for decades of ecological neglect," said a University of Indonesia researcher. "Each flood, each landslide, is a bill coming due."

What happens next

As 2026 approaches, pressure is mounting on the government to act. The Ministry of Environment and Forestry has promised a revised disaster mitigation strategy, but details remain scarce.

International donors, including the Green Climate Fund, have offered support, but disbursement has been slow. Civil society groups are pushing for community-based early warning systems and stricter land-use regulations.

The coming year will test whether Indonesia can translate rhetoric into resilience. With the rainy season far from over, the next few months may bring more tragedy — or a turning point.
