NASA to Chase August 12 Total Solar Eclipse with Balloons and High-Altitude Jet

📅 2026-08-12 📂 Science Original source ↗
Key points
  • NASA will deploy balloons and a high-altitude jet to chase the total solar eclipse
  • Italian scientists are testing a new solar instrument during the eclipse
  • Montana State students will help with NASA's eclipse science experiments

When the Moon slides perfectly in front of the Sun on August 12, plunging parts of North America into an eerie twilight, NASA will not just be watching from the ground. The space agency will be chasing the celestial spectacle with a fleet of balloons and a high-altitude jet, all in a bid to peel back the Sun's most stubborn secrets.

The total solar eclipse, which will trace a path across the continent, offers scientists a rare window to study the Sun's corona—its outer atmosphere—that is normally washed out by the Sun's blinding light. But this year, NASA is going further. By carrying instruments into the stratosphere and launching balloons into the path of totality, researchers hope to capture data that ground-based telescopes simply cannot reach.

A Jet That Chases Shadows

At the heart of the mission is a high-altitude jet, modified to carry a suite of scientific instruments. Flying at an altitude of over 50,000 feet, the aircraft will intercept the eclipse's shadow as it streaks across the Earth at more than 1,500 miles per hour. This will give scientists a longer view of the corona than any ground observer could hope for, extending precious minutes of totality into nearly seven minutes of observation.

The jet's vantage point above the weather also means clearer skies, free from the clouds and atmospheric turbulence that often plague ground-based observations. NASA has used similar aircraft for past eclipses, but this year's mission includes a new twist: a coordinated network of balloons.

Balloons: A Fleet of Floating Observatories

Dozens of high-altitude balloons, launched from multiple sites along the eclipse path, will rise to the edge of space, tethered to instruments that measure the Sun's magnetic field and the solar wind. These balloons, each about the size of a small house when fully inflated, will drift through the stratosphere, beaming data back to scientists on the ground.

Among the participants are students from Montana State University, who have been preparing their payloads for months. Their experiments will focus on how the Sun's corona heats up to millions of degrees, a mystery that has puzzled physicists for decades. The students' work is part of a NASA program that gives universities a chance to contribute to real space science, and for many of them, it is the thrill of a lifetime.

Italian Instrument Tests a New Theory

Across the Atlantic, Italian scientists are also readying a new solar instrument for a high-stakes test during the eclipse. The device, designed to capture light from the corona in unprecedented detail, could help answer why the Sun's outer atmosphere is so much hotter than its surface. If the test succeeds, the instrument could be used on future space missions, offering a new tool for solar research.

The Italian team has been working against the clock, integrating the instrument with a platform that will be flown on a NASA aircraft or balloon. Their goal is to measure the polarisation of light in the corona, which can reveal the strength and direction of magnetic fields. It is a delicate experiment, and the eclipse provides a perfect natural laboratory.

Why This Eclipse Matters

Total solar eclipses are not just spectacular events for skywatchers; they are also unique scientific opportunities. The corona, which is about a million times fainter than the Sun's disk, is normally hidden by the Sun's glare. During totality, the Moon blocks that glare, revealing the corona in all its glory. But totality lasts only a few minutes at any given location, so scientists have learned to move—literally—to catch as much of it as possible.

This year's eclipse is particularly significant because it coincides with a period of increased solar activity, known as solar maximum. That means more sunspots, more solar flares, and a more dynamic corona. The data collected could help improve space weather forecasts, which are crucial for protecting satellites and power grids on Earth.

For the students in Montana, the Italian researchers, and the NASA engineers, the eclipse is both a scientific milestone and a logistical challenge. Weather forecasts, airspace clearances, and instrument calibrations all have to line up perfectly. But if everything goes according to plan, the rewards could be immense.

As the August 12 eclipse approaches, teams are in final preparations, running simulations and double-checking their equipment. The sky will do its part; it is up to the scientists to make the most of it.

The next total solar eclipse visible over North America won't occur until 2044, making this month's event a once-in-a-generation chance. For the researchers involved, the countdown is on.
