Apollo Experts: 80-90% of Sleep Apnoea Cases in India Go Undiagnosed

📅 2026-07-17 📂 Health Original source ↗
Key points
  • Apollo Hospitals experts estimate 80-90% of sleep apnoea cases in India remain undiagnosed.
  • Untreated sleep apnoea is linked to hypertension, heart disease, diabetes and workplace accidents.
  • Doctors recommend screening for people who snore loudly, wake up gasping or feel excessively sleepy during the day.

Millions of Indians may be sleeping poorly without knowing why. Doctors at Apollo Hospitals have warned that 80 to 90 per cent of sleep apnoea cases in the country go undiagnosed.

Sleep apnoea is a disorder where breathing repeatedly stops and starts during sleep. It is often dismissed as simple snoring, but experts say the health consequences can be severe.

The silent toll on health

Untreated sleep apnoea does not just leave people tired. It puts serious strain on the body. The condition is linked to high blood pressure, heart disease, stroke and Type 2 diabetes.

“Patients come to us for fatigue or hypertension, and we find they have severe sleep apnoea,” said a senior Apollo pulmonologist. “They’ve been living with it for years, unaware that their breathing stops hundreds of times every night.”

Why so many cases go unnoticed

Most people do not know they stop breathing in their sleep. Bed partners may notice loud snoring or gasping sounds, but many sleep alone. Others assume snoring is normal.

Doctors also rarely ask about sleep quality during routine check-ups. “There is low awareness among both patients and general physicians,” the expert added. “We need to make sleep health a standard part of medical screening.”

Beyond heart and brain: a hidden driver of road accidents

The impact of undiagnosed sleep apnoea goes beyond chronic disease. Daytime sleepiness caused by poor sleep is a major factor in road accidents and workplace errors.

Studies have shown that people with untreated sleep apnoea are up to five times more likely to be involved in a crash. Truck drivers, factory workers and night-shift employees are particularly at risk.

“A drowsy driver can be as dangerous as a drunk driver,” the Apollo expert said. “This is a public safety issue that we are not addressing.”

The weight connection

Obesity is a leading risk factor for sleep apnoea. Excess fat around the neck can block the airway during sleep. With rising obesity rates in Indian cities, experts expect the number of cases to climb.

However, not all patients are overweight. People with a narrow airway, large tonsils or a family history of the condition are also vulnerable.

What can be done

The gold standard for diagnosis is a sleep study, also called polysomnography. It can be done at a hospital or, in some cases, at home with a portable monitor.

Treatment usually involves a CPAP machine — a device that delivers a steady stream of air through a mask to keep the airway open. Lifestyle changes such as weight loss and avoiding alcohol before bed also help.

Apollo experts are now calling for routine screening of high-risk groups, including people with hypertension, diabetes and those who snore heavily. Early detection, they say, can prevent complications and improve quality of life.

What to watch: Health authorities may soon include sleep apnoea screening in national non-communicable disease programmes. For now, anyone who wakes up tired despite a full night's sleep should ask their doctor about a sleep test.
