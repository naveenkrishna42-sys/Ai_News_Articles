Savings Account Rule 2026: New PAN Card Limits Reshape Deposits

  • From 2026, savings account deposits and cash transactions are directly tied to your PAN, with higher thresholds triggering automatic reporting.
  • The ₹10 lakh annual deposit limit for PAN holders could mean extra KYC or tax scrutiny if breached.
  • Businesses and high-value savers must reconcile multiple accounts under one PAN to avoid penalties.

What the 2026 Savings Account Rule Actually Changes

From 1 January 2026, the Income Tax Department will enforce a new framework linking savings account activity to PAN. Under the updated rules, banks must flag accounts where total credits exceed ₹10 lakh in a financial year or cash deposits cross ₹1 lakh per transaction. These thresholds apply per PAN, not per account, forcing customers to aggregate all savings balances. The move targets tax evasion and benami transactions, but it also affects ordinary savers who shuffle money between banks.

Why the ₹10 Lakh Threshold Matters

For salaried individuals, ₹10 lakh in annual credits is rarely breached. However, freelancers, small business owners, and those receiving rent or agricultural income may cross it inadvertently. Once flagged, the bank will request source-of-funds documentation. Non-compliance can lead to a freeze on further credits until clarified. This is not a tax—it is a reporting trigger that can escalate to scrutiny.

Cash Transaction Limit: ₹1 Lakh Per Transaction

The earlier ₹50,000 cash deposit limit for PAN quoting has been revised. Now, any single cash deposit of ₹1 lakh or more requires PAN, and repeated deposits just below the limit will be aggregated. Banks will report such activity in their Annual Information Statement (AIS). For traders and shopkeepers who deposit daily earnings, this means maintaining a clear paper trail.

Real-World Buyer Implications: Who Gets Hit Hardest

High-net-worth individuals using multiple savings accounts for liquidity management will need to consolidate or maintain detailed records. Joint account holders must note that PAN of the primary holder is used for aggregation. NRIs with NRO accounts face similar rules, though repatriation limits differ. The biggest impact is on small businesses that use personal savings accounts for business receipts—a practice that now invites automatic scrutiny.

Practical Benchmarks and Value Justification

Consider a freelancer earning ₹12 lakh annually, deposited across three banks. Under the new rule, the total credits across all accounts will be summed. If the freelancer cannot produce invoices or Form 26AS matching the deposits, the bank may report the excess. The cost of non-compliance—potential 30% tax on unexplained credits plus penalties—far outweighs the effort of maintaining a separate current account.

Pros and Cons of the PAN-Linked Limit Regime

  • Pros: Curb benami accounts, improve tax transparency, reduce cash hoarding, simplify KYC for genuine savers.
  • Cons: Increased paperwork for small businesses, risk of accidental breach for joint accounts, potential delays in credit for non-compliance, double reporting if multiple banks flag same PAN.

How to Stay Compliant Without Hassle

First, link all savings accounts to your PAN if not already done. Second, track credits monthly using a simple spreadsheet or bank statements. Third, for business income, open a current account to segregate personal and commercial transactions. Fourth, if you expect to cross ₹10 lakh, keep invoices and bank statements ready. Fifth, avoid structuring cash deposits to stay under ₹1 lakh—banks are required to report suspicious patterns.

Expert Take: Is This a Tax by Another Name?

No. The rule does not impose a new tax. It merely expands reporting obligations. However, the data will feed into the tax department's risk-scoring system. For honest taxpayers, the impact is minimal. For those evading taxes, the net has tightened. The key is to treat the ₹10 lakh threshold as a trigger for documentation, not a ceiling on savings.
