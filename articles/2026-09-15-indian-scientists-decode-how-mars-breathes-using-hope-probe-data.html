Why Mars Appears to 'Breathe'

Researchers at the Physical Research Laboratory (PRL), Ahmedabad have mapped how the Martian upper atmosphere expands during the day and contracts at night — a rhythmic swelling that scientists describe as the planet 'breathing'. The work draws on remote-sensing data from the Emirates Mars Mission (Hope probe), which has been orbiting Mars since 2021 in a high, elliptical path ideal for tracking global atmospheric behaviour.

Unlike Earth, Mars has a thin carbon-dioxide atmosphere with no protective ozone layer, so solar extreme-ultraviolet radiation directly heats and inflates the upper air. The PRL analysis quantifies this daily and seasonal pulse, offering the most granular Indian-led view yet of Martian thermospheric dynamics.

What the Data Actually Shows

The team tracked temperature and density profiles across altitudes and local times, correlating them with solar activity and dust storms. The core finding: Mars' upper atmosphere responds to solar forcing far faster than older models assumed, with measurable expansion within hours of peak insolation.

Why This Matters for Future Missions

Atmospheric density is the single biggest unknown for any spacecraft attempting aerobraking or precision landing on Mars. A denser-than-expected thermosphere drags orbiters down faster, forcing fuel-hungry corrections. A thinner one delays aerocapture manoeuvres entirely. The PRL maps give mission planners — including ISRO's proposed Mars Orbiter Mission-2 — a live calibration tool rather than a static average.

The findings also sharpen climate models used to interpret ancient river valleys and polar ice layers, connecting present-day atmospheric behaviour to Mars' long-term climatic history.

Key Real-World Highlights

  • First Indian-led global mapping of Martian thermospheric 'breathing' using Hope probe data.
  • Demonstrates growing India-UAE scientific collaboration in planetary science.
  • Direct operational value for aerobraking, landing, and orbiter lifetime estimates.
  • Improves accuracy of Mars climate models by replacing static assumptions with dynamic profiles.
  • Positions PRL as a key node in international Mars atmospheric research.

Buyer and Researcher Context

For space agencies and private Mars ventures, atmospheric models are a procurement-grade input. Missions budget tens of crores for drag compensation fuel; better density maps translate directly into lower launch mass and longer orbital life. Universities running planetary science programmes can use PRL's published datasets to validate their own climate simulations without building new instruments.

The study's open-data approach — leveraging a partner nation's spacecraft — also shows how mid-budget space programmes can punch above their weight in high-impact science.
