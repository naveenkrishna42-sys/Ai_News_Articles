RBI rules force banks to overhaul model risk testing frameworks

📅 2026-07-23 📂 Banking Original source ↗
Key points
  • RBI mandates independent model validation and continuous testing for banks
  • New rules cover credit, market, and operational risk models including AI/ML systems
  • Banks face higher compliance costs and need to hire specialised testing staff

The Reserve Bank of India's latest regulatory push has turned model risk management into a testing problem for banks across the country. Lenders now face a new set of requirements that demand rigorous, independent validation of the mathematical models they use for everything from credit scoring to stress testing.

The central bank's directive, reported earlier this week, focuses on ensuring that banks do not rely on flawed or outdated models that could misstate risk. The shift is significant because it transforms what was largely a documentation and compliance exercise into a continuous testing and monitoring obligation.

What the new rules demand

Under the updated framework, banks must establish a separate model risk management function that reports directly to the board or a board-level committee. This function will be responsible for validating all models before they go into production and for conducting periodic reviews thereafter.

The rules apply to a wide range of models, including those used for credit risk, market risk, operational risk, and stress testing. Even models that use artificial intelligence and machine learning are now squarely in the regulator's sights. Banks must document the assumptions, limitations, and data inputs behind each model, and they must test how those models perform under adverse scenarios.

One of the key changes is that validation can no longer be done by the same team that built the model. The RBI has insisted on independence, meaning banks will need to hire or train separate validation teams. For smaller lenders, this could mean a steep increase in costs.

Why this matters for the banking sector

Indian banks have been using increasingly complex models in recent years, especially as digital lending and AI-based credit scoring have grown. The RBI's concern is that some of these models may not have been adequately tested for accuracy or fairness. A flawed model could lead to mispriced loans, underestimated capital requirements, or even systemic risk.

The new rules also require banks to maintain a model inventory and to report regularly to the RBI on the performance and validation status of each model. Non-compliance could result in higher capital charges or restrictions on certain businesses.

Industry estimates suggest that the largest Indian banks will need to spend several crore rupees each to set up the required infrastructure. Hiring experienced quantitative analysts and model validators, who are in short supply in India, will add to the pressure.

Impact on smaller lenders and fintech partnerships

For smaller banks and cooperative lenders, the challenge is even greater. They often lack the in-house expertise to build and validate sophisticated models. Many have relied on third-party vendors or fintech partners for their credit scoring and risk management tools. The new rules do not explicitly ban such arrangements, but they make the bank ultimately responsible for the model's performance.

This could slow down partnerships between banks and fintech firms, as lenders will now demand far more transparency into how models work. Fintech companies that provide AI-based underwriting will have to open up their algorithms for independent testing.

What banks need to do now

Banking compliance officers are already working on gap analyses. The first step is to map every model currently in use and classify it by risk level. High-risk models, such as those used for capital adequacy calculations, will need to be validated within a shorter timeline.

Recruitment of model risk specialists has picked up, with several large banks advertising for roles such as model validation manager and quantitative risk analyst. Some banks are also looking at software tools that can automate parts of the testing process.

The RBI has given banks a transition period, but the deadlines are tight. Institutions that drag their feet could face regulatory action.

In the coming months, the industry will watch closely for any clarification from the RBI on how the rules apply to models built by third parties, and whether there will be any leniency for smaller lenders. For now, the message is clear: model risk is no longer a back-office compliance issue. It is a front-line testing problem that demands immediate attention.
