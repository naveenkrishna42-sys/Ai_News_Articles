From Bitcoin to blockchain: Key cryptocurrency terms and what they mean

📅 2026-07-18 📂 Crypto Original source ↗
Key points
  • Bitcoin introduced the concept of a decentralised digital currency in 2009.
  • Blockchain is the underlying technology that records transactions across a network of computers.
  • Mining involves solving complex puzzles to validate transactions and earn new coins.
  • Wallets store private keys needed to access and manage cryptocurrency holdings.
  • Altcoins like Ethereum and Ripple offer different features beyond simple payments.

Understanding Bitcoin and its origins

Bitcoin burst onto the scene in 2009 as the world's first decentralised digital currency. Created by an unknown person or group using the pseudonym Satoshi Nakamoto, it promised a way to send money without banks or governments in the middle.

At its core, Bitcoin is a peer-to-peer system. Users can transfer value directly to one another, and every transaction is recorded on a public digital ledger called a blockchain. This ledger is maintained by a network of computers, not a central authority.

Today, Bitcoin remains the most valuable and widely recognised cryptocurrency. Its total market capitalisation has at times crossed several trillion dollars, though prices remain notoriously volatile.

What is a blockchain?

A blockchain is a chain of blocks, each containing a list of transactions. Once a block is added to the chain, its data cannot be altered without changing all subsequent blocks—which would require controlling more than half the network's computing power.

This design makes blockchains highly secure and transparent. Anyone can view the entire history of transactions, but no single entity can tamper with the record. This is why blockchain is often described as a 'trustless' system—you don't need to trust a bank; you trust the math and the network.

Beyond cryptocurrencies, blockchains are being explored for supply chain tracking, digital identity, and even voting systems. But the most common use remains as the backbone of digital currencies.

Mining and how transactions are validated

Mining is the process by which new Bitcoin transactions are confirmed and added to the blockchain. Miners use powerful computers to solve complex mathematical puzzles. The first miner to solve the puzzle gets to add the next block and is rewarded with newly created bitcoins.

This system is called proof-of-work. It consumes enormous amounts of electricity—some estimates put Bitcoin's annual energy use comparable to that of entire countries. Critics argue this is environmentally unsustainable, while supporters say it's a necessary cost for security.

As more miners join the network, the puzzles get harder. This ensures that new blocks are added roughly every ten minutes, regardless of how much computing power is available.

Crypto wallets: your digital vault

To use Bitcoin or any cryptocurrency, you need a wallet. A wallet doesn't store coins—they live on the blockchain. Instead, it stores the private keys that prove you own the coins and allow you to send them to others.

Wallets come in two main types: hot and cold. Hot wallets are connected to the internet, making them convenient for everyday transactions but more vulnerable to hacking. Cold wallets, like hardware devices or paper printouts, are offline and far more secure.

Losing your private keys means losing access to your funds permanently. There's no 'forgot password' button in crypto, which is why backup phrases—often 12 or 24 words—are crucial.

Altcoins and the broader ecosystem

Thousands of cryptocurrencies now exist beyond Bitcoin. These are often called altcoins. Ethereum, the second-largest, introduced smart contracts—self-executing agreements that run on its blockchain. This opened the door to decentralised finance, or DeFi, and non-fungible tokens, or NFTs.

Ripple focuses on fast, low-cost cross-border payments. Litecoin, created in 2011, was designed to process transactions faster than Bitcoin. Each altcoin aims to solve a different problem or serve a specific niche, but all share the foundational ideas of decentralisation and cryptographic security.

Investors should be cautious. The crypto market is lightly regulated, scams are common, and prices can swing wildly in hours. Many experts advise only risking money you can afford to lose.

As regulatory frameworks evolve worldwide, the coming months will likely bring clearer rules for exchanges, taxation, and consumer protection. How governments choose to handle this technology will shape its future for years to come.
