Introduction

The Bangalore Mirror’s latest investigation, titled "Fake it till you make it: Quacks exposed," lifts the lid on a clandestine network of medical impostors operating throughout Bengaluru. These unlicensed practitioners masquerade as consultants in cardiology, orthopaedics, and fertility, leveraging cheap consultation fees and slick online marketing to attract vulnerable patients. The exposé underscores a growing threat to public health, where profit‑driven deception outpaces regulatory response.

Scale of the Problem in Bengaluru

Over the past year, Bangalore Mirror reporters have documented more than thirty clandestine clinics in prime residential and commercial districts. The operations range from small flats equipped with basic examination rooms to storefront “wellness centres” that display fabricated Indian Medical Council (IMC) registration numbers. The common thread is a business model built on undercutting legitimate specialists while offering minimal medical expertise.

How Fake Credentials Bypass Patients

Investigators found that impostors obtain forged MBBS or PG diplomas from unregulated institutes and then purchase fake IMC enrollment certificates. Many display registration numbers that appear legitimate but are not linked to any active license. Online listings omit verification checks, and some practitioners even use legitimate hospital affiliations as a veneer, falsely claiming partnership or consultancy rights.

Real-World Impact on Buyers

Patients who seek treatment from these quacks often receive incorrect diagnoses, receive inappropriate medication, or suffer surgical complications due to lack of proper training. Financial exposure is significant; while legitimate specialists may charge ₹3,000–₹5,000 for a consultation, fake clinics advertise fees as low as ₹1,500, creating an illusion of value. The long‑term cost includes repeat visits, corrective procedures, and emotional distress.

Regulatory Gaps and Enforcement Challenges

The Medical Council of India’s verification portal remains underutilised, and many practitioners exploit the lag between registration and real‑time checks. State health departments face staffing shortages, limiting routine inspections of non‑hospital based clinics. Moreover, the distinction between “alternative medicine” practitioners and allopathic doctors creates loopholes that impostors readily exploit.

Buyer Protection Strategies

Patients can safeguard themselves by confirming a doctor’s NMC registration number on the official portal, requesting proof of hospital affiliation, and asking for recent patient testimonials. Seeking a second opinion before any invasive procedure reduces risk, while reporting suspicious clinics to the State Health Department helps authorities act swiftly. Consumers should also avoid clinics that pressure cash‑only payments or guarantee unrealistic outcomes.

Conclusion

The Bangalore Mirror exposé reveals a disturbing trend where ambition and profit override ethical medical practice. While regulatory bodies are beginning to tighten verification, the burden of due diligence rests heavily on the patient. Greater awareness, stricter enforcement, and transparent credentialing are essential to protect public health and restore trust in Bengaluru’s medical ecosystem.

Key Insights

  • Unqualified doctors use forged diplomas and fake registration numbers to appear legitimate.
  • Low‑cost consultations attract patients but often result in misdiagnosis and higher long‑term expenses.
  • Regulatory bodies face staffing and technology gaps, allowing scams to proliferate.
  • Verification of NMC registration, hospital ties, and patient reviews are effective protective measures.
  • Public reporting and stricter inspections are critical to curb the rise of medical impostors.
