Fundamentals of FM and PM Modulation

Frequency Modulation (FM) varies the carrier frequency in proportion to the instantaneous amplitude of the baseband signal, while Phase Modulation (PM) varies the carrier phase. Both are angle‑modulation schemes, but FM is predominant in broadcast audio because its frequency deviation translates directly into amplitude variations after detection, offering inherent noise resistance. In FM, the frequency deviation Δf determines the occupied bandwidth via Carson’s rule: BW ≈ 2(Δf + f_m), where f_m is the highest modulating frequency. For commercial FM broadcast in the United States, Δf is set to ±75 kHz and f_m to 15 kHz, yielding a bandwidth of about 180 kHz.

Phase Modulation, by contrast, is often employed in data communication systems (e.g., certain microwave links and digital radio). The phase deviation Δφ is measured in radians; a typical value for a robust PM link is ±5 rad, which, when combined with a baseband bandwidth of 1 MHz, results in a similar occupied bandwidth to FM but with different noise characteristics.

Interference Challenges in Angle‑Modulated Systems

Both FM and PM suffer from additive white Gaussian noise (AWGN) and co‑channel interference. In FM, the detector output SNR improves with increased deviation because the frequency swing spreads the signal energy over a wider bandwidth, reducing the noise power spectral density within the demodulated audio band. However, excessive deviation wastes spectrum and can cause adjacent‑channel interference. PM systems exhibit a similar threshold effect: below a certain carrier‑to‑noise ratio (CNR), the detected phase experiences cycle slips, leading to abrupt errors.

Co‑channel interference becomes critical when two transmitters operate on the same frequency. FM’s capture effect allows the receiver to lock onto the stronger of two signals, effectively rejecting the weaker one if the power difference exceeds roughly 1 dB. PM does not exhibit a strong capture effect; instead, interference adds vectorially to the phase, causing degradation that scales with the interference‑to‑signal ratio.

Pre‑Emphasis and De‑Emphasis: Boosting High‑Frequency SNR

Audio signals contain less energy at higher frequencies, making them more susceptible to noise after detection. Pre‑emphasis applies a high‑frequency boost before transmission, while a complementary de‑emphasis network at the receiver attenuates those frequencies, flattening the overall response and improving the post‑detection SNR. The standard pre‑emphasis time constant in the US is 75 µs, which yields a 6 dB boost at 15 kHz. In Europe and many other regions, a 50 µs constant is used, providing about 4 dB of boost at the same frequency.

Mathematically, the pre‑emphasis transfer function is H(f) = 1 + j2πfτ, where τ is the time constant. After de‑emphasis, the overall response is flat, but the noise contributed during transmission is reduced by the same factor, resulting in an SNR improvement proportional to (1 + (2πfτ)^2). For a 15 kHz audio tone with τ = 75 µs, the improvement is approximately 6 dB.

Capture Effect: FM’s Interference Rejection Mechanism

The capture effect is a nonlinear phenomenon unique to FM (and, to a lesser extent, PM with limiter stages). When two FM signals of comparable frequency but different amplitudes arrive at a receiver, the limiter stage in the FM detector suppresses amplitude variations, causing the output to follow the instantaneous frequency of the stronger signal. If the amplitude difference exceeds the capture threshold—typically 0.5 to 2 dB depending on receiver design—the weaker signal is effectively ignored.

This property makes FM highly robust in environments with fading or intermittent interferers, such as vehicular radio or broadcast reception near obstacles. Engineers leverage the capture effect when designing microphone wireless systems: a slight power advantage ensures clear audio even when a competing transmitter operates on the same channel.

Key Takeaways for Engineers and Purchasers

  • FM’s capture effect provides ~1 dB interference rejection, making it ideal for broadcast and wireless audio where occasional stronger interferers appear.
  • Pre‑emphasis (75 µs US, 50 µs EU) delivers 4‑6 dB SNR gain at the upper audio band after de‑emphasis, a critical factor for high‑fidelity applications.
  • PM systems lack a strong capture effect; interference adds directly to phase, requiring tighter CNR or advanced mitigation techniques.
  • When specifying equipment, verify deviation, pre‑emphasis constant, and capture threshold from the vendor’s datasheet—do not rely on marketing claims alone.
  • For data‑oriented links, consider PM only if the system can allocate sufficient bandwidth and employ forward error correction to offset the absence of capture.
