Background: FIR Registered in Delhi Over Social Media Posts

Delhi Police have registered an FIR against Nishu Azad, a student protester, and her father following a complaint alleging that social media posts published by them insulted Hindu deities. The complaint, filed by advocate Vishal Srivastava at the Connaught Place police station, was lodged shortly after activist Swatantra Bhardwaj was arrested in connection with a separate protest at Jantar Mantar. The case has drawn attention to the intersection of online expression, religious sentiment, and criminal liability under Indian law.

According to multiple reports from The Times of India, Hindustan Times, ANI News, and India TV News, the complainant stated that the posts had "outraged my feelings" and caused offence to religious sentiments. The phrase echoes the wording historically associated with Section 295A of the Indian Penal Code, which criminalises deliberate acts intended to outrage religious feelings.

Who Filed the Complaint and Against Whom

The complaint was filed by advocate Vishal Srivastava, who approached the Connaught Place police station seeking action against Nishu Azad and her father. Nishu Azad has been associated with protest activity at Jantar Mantar, the designated demonstration site in central Delhi. While her exact role in the Bhardwaj-linked protest has not been publicly detailed, the FIR connects her online posts to the broader episode that led to the activist's arrest.

Both Nishu Azad and her father have been named in the complaint. Police sources confirmed that an FIR has been registered, though details on whether either has been arrested, summoned, or questioned were not immediately available in the reporting.

Legal Provisions Invoked in the Case

The FIR has been registered under Section 295A of the Indian Penal Code (now part of the Bharatiya Nyaya Sanhita framework), which deals with deliberate acts intended to outrage religious feelings of any class by insulting its religion or religious beliefs. Section 34, dealing with acts done by several persons in furtherance of common intention, has also been invoked given that both Nishu Azad and her father are named.

Section 295A carries a punishment of up to three years of imprisonment, or a fine, or both. The provision has been the subject of extensive judicial scrutiny, with the Supreme Court of India ruling in multiple judgments that the section requires deliberate intent to insult religion, not mere accidental or good-faith criticism.

Connection to the Jantar Mantar Protest and Bhardwaj's Arrest

The complaint is temporally and contextually linked to the arrest of activist Swatantra Bhardwaj. Reports indicate that Bhardwaj was taken into custody in connection with a protest at Jantar Mantar, a longstanding venue for public demonstrations in New Delhi. The exact nature of the protest, its organisers, and the specific charges against Bhardwaj remain partially unclear in the available reporting.

The complainant, Srivastava, moved against Nishu Azad and her father shortly after this arrest, citing social media posts that allegedly mocked Hindu gods. The sequence has raised questions about whether the FIR is part of a coordinated response to the protest activity or an independent legal action triggered by specific online content.

Key Real-World Highlights

  • The FIR invokes Section 295A IPC, which requires proof of deliberate intent to outrage religious feelings.
  • Complainant advocate Vishal Srivastava used the phrase "outraged my feelings" in his complaint, mirroring statutory language.
  • Both Nishu Azad and her father are named, with Section 34 applied for common intention.
  • No immediate information on arrests or summons has been reported as of the latest updates.

The case highlights recurring concerns about the use of Section 295A in cases involving social media posts and protest participants. Legal observers note that while the provision remains valid, courts have repeatedly cautioned against its misuse to silence criticism or dissent. The coming days are likely to see further statements from the accused, possible bail applications, and clarity on whether the posts in question meet the threshold of "deliberate" insult required under the statute.
