Climate Change Effects: NASA Details Rising Seas and Extreme Weather

📅 2026-07-18 📂 Environment Original source ↗
Key points
  • Global average temperature has risen over 1°C since the late 19th century, driven by greenhouse gas emissions.
  • Ice sheets in Greenland and Antarctica are losing mass at an accelerating rate, contributing to sea level rise.
  • Extreme weather events like heatwaves, floods, and hurricanes have become more frequent and intense.

The Earth is warming at a pace that scientists describe as unprecedented in human history. According to NASA, the planet's average temperature has climbed more than 1°C since the late 19th century, with most of that warming occurring in the past four decades. The primary driver: a sharp increase in carbon dioxide and other greenhouse gases from burning fossil fuels, deforestation, and industrial activity.

The consequences are no longer theoretical. NASA's data shows that the effects of climate change are being felt on every continent and in every ocean. From melting ice sheets to shifting wildlife populations, the signs are both clear and accelerating.

Rising Seas and Melting Ice

One of the most visible impacts is sea level rise. NASA satellites have recorded an average rise of about 3.3 millimetres per year over the past three decades. This is driven by two factors: the expansion of seawater as it warms, and the addition of freshwater from melting glaciers and ice sheets.

The Greenland and Antarctic ice sheets are losing mass at an alarming rate. Greenland alone is losing an average of 279 billion tonnes of ice per year. In Antarctica, the loss is roughly 148 billion tonnes annually. This meltwater flows into the oceans, pushing sea levels higher and threatening coastal communities from Mumbai to Miami.

More Intense and Frequent Extreme Weather

Climate change does not just raise temperatures—it supercharges the weather. Warmer air holds more moisture, which leads to heavier rainfall and more severe flooding. The Indian subcontinent has seen this firsthand, with monsoon seasons becoming more erratic and intense downpours causing landslides and urban flooding.

At the same time, heatwaves are becoming longer and more lethal. NASA notes that record-breaking high temperatures are now occurring far more often than record lows. Droughts are also intensifying in many regions, reducing water availability for agriculture and drinking, and increasing the risk of wildfires.

Hurricanes and cyclones are gaining strength. Warmer ocean waters provide more energy, making storms more powerful. The frequency of Category 4 and 5 storms has increased in several ocean basins since the 1980s.

Ecosystems Under Pressure

Shifting climate patterns are disrupting ecosystems worldwide. Many species are moving toward the poles or to higher elevations to find suitable conditions. Coral reefs, often called the rainforests of the sea, are experiencing mass bleaching events as ocean temperatures rise. The Great Barrier Reef has lost more than half of its coral cover in recent decades.

On land, forests are under stress. Warmer, drier conditions have made large areas of forest in North America, Australia, and Siberia more vulnerable to pests and fires. The Amazon rainforest, a critical carbon sink, is showing signs of degradation.

Agriculture is also feeling the heat. Changing rainfall patterns and extreme weather are affecting crop yields. In India, where a large portion of the population depends on rain-fed farming, unpredictable monsoons pose a direct threat to food security.

What Comes Next

NASA's data makes one thing clear: the planet will continue to warm as long as greenhouse gas emissions rise. Scientists project further sea level rise, more extreme weather, and additional disruptions to ecosystems and human societies. The pace and severity of these changes depend on decisions made today—whether to cut emissions, adapt infrastructure, and protect natural buffers like mangroves and forests.

The window for meaningful action is narrowing, but the data also offers a path forward. Monitoring from space and ground stations allows scientists to track changes with precision, giving policymakers the information needed to respond. What happens next will be shaped by how seriously the world takes the warnings.
