Bombay High Court Sends Thane Restaurant Back to the Regulator

The Bombay High Court has declined to interfere directly in a licence suspension dispute involving a Thane restaurant, directing the establishment to first approach the Food Safety Commissioner under the Food Safety and Standards Act, 2006. The order reinforces a settled principle of administrative law: statutory remedies must be exhausted before courts step in. For food businesses across the Mumbai Metropolitan Region, the ruling is a practical signal about how suspension and closure orders will be litigated.

The restaurant had challenged the suspension of its licence, reportedly issued by the local food safety authority following inspection findings. Rather than adjudicate the merits, the court pointed the petitioner to the appellate mechanism built into the FSS Act, which gives the Commissioner both the power and the technical mandate to review such orders.

Why the Appellate Route Matters for Restaurants

Under the FSS Act and its rules, a food business operator whose licence is suspended or cancelled can appeal to the Food Safety Commissioner within a prescribed period, typically 15 days from receipt of the order. The appellate authority can confirm, modify, or set aside the suspension after hearing the operator.

Skipping this step is expensive. High Court writ petitions involve admission hearings, pleadings, and adjournments that can stretch for months, while the kitchen stays shut. The appellate route is faster, cheaper, and builds a documented record that strengthens any later judicial challenge.

What Triggers Suspension — and What It Costs

Suspensions usually follow serious hygiene failures, pest infestation, unsafe storage temperatures, expired stock, or repeat grading shortfalls. Authorities may also act where a sample fails lab testing under the Food Safety and Standards (Laboratory and Sampling Analysis) Regulations.

The commercial damage is immediate. A mid-size Thane or Mumbai restaurant turning over ₹15–40 lakh a month can lose most of that revenue during a closure, besides staff wages, rent, and spoiled inventory. Rebuilding customer trust after a public suspension takes far longer than the suspension itself.

Real-World Highlights

  • Exhausting the statutory appeal is now effectively mandatory before seeking writ relief.
  • Appeals before the Commissioner are decided on records — inspection reports, sampling results, and hygiene audits matter.
  • Courts routinely remand matters where the appellate remedy is available and unexhausted.
  • Prompt legal response within the 15-day window preserves the strongest possible case.
  • FSSAI licence fees are modest relative to the revenue risk of a shutdown.

Practical Compliance Playbook for F&B Operators

Restaurants should treat inspections as auditable events. Maintain daily temperature logs, pest-control records, staff medical fitness certificates, and supplier invoices with FSSAI licence numbers. A documented food safety management system based on Schedule 4 hygiene requirements is the single best defence.

When an order arrives, note the date of receipt, engage counsel immediately, and file the appeal with the Commissioner within the window. Parallel corrective action — cleaning, staff retraining, third-party audits — demonstrates good faith and often shortens the suspension.

Pros and Cons of the Appellate Route

  • Pros: Faster than writ petitions, lower cost, technically informed decision-maker, preserves judicial options.
  • Cons: Short 15-day filing window, limited scope for new evidence, no automatic stay in every case.

The Bombay High Court's direction is a reminder that India's food safety framework has a functioning internal review layer. Restaurants that use it properly — with clean records and quick legal action — stand the best chance of reopening quickly and protecting their brand.
