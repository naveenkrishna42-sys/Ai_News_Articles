Total Solar Eclipse to Sweep Spain, Iceland and Greenland in August

📅 2026-08-03 📂 Science Original source ↗
Key points
  • Total solar eclipse in August 2026 will be visible from Spain, Iceland and Greenland.
  • First total solar eclipse in Europe since 1999, with partial phases across the UK.
  • NASA and other agencies issue eye-safety warnings and viewing guidance.
  • Best viewing conditions expected in northern Spain and Iceland, weather permitting.

On a mid-August morning, the Moon will slide perfectly between the Sun and Earth, casting a shadow that races across the North Atlantic. The path of totality will cross northern Spain, Iceland, and Greenland, offering skywatchers a rare celestial spectacle. The event marks the first total solar eclipse to touch Europe in over two decades, with the previous one occurring in August 1999.

Astronomers and space agencies, including NASA, are already gearing up for the event. The agency has announced coverage plans for the eclipse, which will be visible in its full glory only along a narrow corridor. Outside this path, a partial eclipse will be seen across much of Europe, including the British Isles, where observers will witness the Moon taking a bite out of the Sun.

A Narrow Path, A Wide Audience

The eclipse's path of totality begins at sunrise in the North Atlantic, sweeps over Greenland's southern tip, then crosses Iceland's western coast. From there, it moves southeast toward Spain. In Spain, the shadow will traverse the northern regions, including cities like Bilbao and Zaragoza, before exiting the continent near the Mediterranean coast.

For most of Europe, the eclipse will appear as a partial one. In the UK, for instance, observers in the West Country could see up to 90% of the Sun obscured. This has prompted local astronomy groups to organise viewing events, with telescopes and solar filters set up in public parks.

Why This Eclipse Matters

This is the first total solar eclipse visible from continental Europe since 1999. That earlier event, which crossed France, Germany, Austria, and the Balkans, drew massive crowds and sparked widespread public interest. This year's eclipse, though confined to a smaller land area, is expected to attract thousands of tourists to Iceland and Spain.

For scientists, the eclipse offers a valuable opportunity to study the Sun's corona—the outer atmosphere that is normally hidden by the Sun's bright light. Total eclipses also allow researchers to test instruments and models that could be used in future space missions.

Protecting Your Eyes

As with any solar event, safety is paramount. Looking directly at the Sun, even during a total eclipse, can cause permanent eye damage. Experts stress that only certified eclipse glasses or handheld solar viewers should be used. Regular sunglasses, camera lenses, or binoculars are not safe substitutes.

During totality, when the Sun is completely covered, it is safe to view with the naked eye. But the moment the Sun begins to reappear, viewers must put their glasses back on. This warning has been repeated by NASA, the European Space Agency, and national health bodies.

Where to Watch

In Spain, the best viewing spots are in the north, where the eclipse will occur in mid-morning. Cities like Zaragoza and Huesca are expected to see over two minutes of totality. In Iceland, the capital Reykjavik will experience a partial eclipse, with totality visible only from the western fjords. Greenland offers the longest viewing time, but remote locations and unpredictable weather make it a challenge for travellers.

Meteorologists note that August weather in these regions can be cloudy, especially in Iceland and Greenland. Those planning to travel should have backup plans and be prepared to move to clearer skies.

As the date approaches, more details on viewing events and live streams will emerge. NASA has announced it will provide a live broadcast, allowing people worldwide to follow the eclipse as it unfolds. For those in the path of totality, the experience promises to be unforgettable—a few minutes of eerie darkness in the middle of a summer morning.
