Dighton Trial: Postpartum Psychosis Warning Signs Experts Say Were Missed

📅 2026-08-11 📂 Health Original source ↗
Key points
  • Trial of Lindsay Clancy, accused of killing her three children, has reignited debate over postpartum psychosis.
  • Experts say media and courts often conflate postpartum depression with psychosis, leading to missed diagnoses.
  • Psychiatrists call for better screening and awareness of rare but severe postpartum mental health conditions.

A Trial That Has Shaken the Nation

The trial of Lindsay Clancy, the Massachusetts mother accused of killing her three children, has drawn intense scrutiny. Many are calling it one of the saddest murder trials in recent US history. The case has thrust postpartum psychosis into the national conversation, with experts questioning whether warning signs were overlooked.

Clancy, a former nurse, is accused of strangling her three young children in their Dighton home. The defence argues she was suffering from severe postpartum psychosis, a rare but serious mental health condition. Prosecutors, however, maintain that her actions were deliberate and calculated.

Mental Health Terms Being Confused in Court and Media

Psychiatrists following the case say the public discourse has been muddied by a fundamental misunderstanding of mental health terms. Postpartum depression, postpartum anxiety, and postpartum psychosis are often used interchangeably, even in courtrooms and news coverage. This confusion, experts argue, can have real consequences for how such cases are judged.

Dr. Sarah Whitmore, a forensic psychiatrist, explains that postpartum psychosis is distinct from depression. It involves hallucinations, delusions, and a break from reality, typically emerging within the first two weeks after childbirth. "Depression is about mood; psychosis is about losing touch with what is real," she says. "Conflating them does a disservice to both the accused and the broader understanding of maternal mental health."

Why Postpartum Psychosis Keeps Getting Missed

Despite its severity, postpartum psychosis remains underdiagnosed. One reason is that its early symptoms—insomnia, agitation, unusual speech—can be mistaken for the exhaustion that many new mothers experience. Another is the stigma around admitting to disturbing thoughts, which can prevent women from seeking help.

Dr. Meera Krishnan, a maternal mental health specialist, notes that screening tools are often inadequate. "Most obstetric check-ups focus on physical recovery," she says. "There is no routine, standardised screening for psychosis in the way there is for depression." The result is that women who need urgent psychiatric care may fall through the cracks.

Experts Divided Over Clancy's Symptoms

The Clancy case has split experts. Some believe her documented history of anxiety and obsessive thoughts, along with reports of sleeplessness, pointed clearly to postpartum psychosis. Others argue that her behaviour in the months before the killings suggested a more calculated mindset, and that using psychosis as a defence may be a stretch.

Court records show Clancy had been seeing a psychiatrist and was prescribed medication. Yet no one apparently recognised the severity of her condition. This has led to difficult questions: Could the tragedy have been prevented with better mental health care? And how many other mothers are silently suffering without adequate support?

The Need for Better Maternal Mental Health Care

Psychiatrists are now calling for a systemic overhaul. They want routine screening for all postpartum women, not just those who report feeling sad. They also want better training for healthcare providers to recognise the red flags of psychosis, such as rapid mood swings, paranoia, and thoughts of harming the baby.

Family members, too, have a role to play. "Families often dismiss warning signs as 'baby blues'," says Dr. Whitmore. "But if a new mother is talking about things that don't make sense, or seems unreachable, that is an emergency." She advises families to trust their instincts and seek help immediately if something feels wrong.

What Happens Next

The trial is expected to continue for several more weeks, with closing arguments likely to centre on the psychiatric evidence. Whatever the verdict, the case has already changed the conversation around maternal mental health. The hope among advocates is that it will lead to tangible reforms—better screening, more resources, and a public that understands the difference between depression and psychosis.
