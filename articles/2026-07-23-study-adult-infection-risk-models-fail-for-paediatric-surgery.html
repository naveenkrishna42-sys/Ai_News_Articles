Study: Adult Infection Risk Models Fail for Paediatric Surgery

📅 2026-07-23 📂 Health Original source ↗
Key points
  • Standard adult risk models significantly misjudge surgical site infection rates in children.
  • Study suggests paediatric patients have unique risk factors that are not captured by adult models.
  • Researchers call for development of dedicated paediatric surgical infection risk assessment tools.

Adult Risk Models Fall Short for Children

A recent study has found that widely used risk prediction models for surgical site infections (SSIs) are not reliable when applied to paediatric patients. The research, published in EMJ, highlights a critical gap in how hospitals assess infection risk for children undergoing surgery.

SSIs are a common complication after surgery, increasing recovery time and healthcare costs. For decades, hospitals have relied on models developed primarily using adult patient data. The new findings suggest this one-size-fits-all approach may be leading to inaccurate risk assessments for younger patients.

Unique Factors in Paediatric Care

The study examined data from a cohort of paediatric surgical patients and compared actual SSI rates against predictions from established adult risk models. The results showed a significant discrepancy, with the models often underestimating or overestimating infection risk in children.

Researchers identified several factors that may explain the difference. Children’s developing immune systems, variations in wound healing, and different types of surgical procedures all contribute to a unique risk profile that adult models fail to capture. The study did not provide specific numbers or percentages, but the trend was clear across multiple metrics.

What This Means for Hospitals

For paediatric surgeons and hospital infection control teams, the findings underscore the need for caution when using adult-derived tools. Relying on these models could lead to misallocated preventive resources or missed opportunities to reduce infections in children.

Hospitals that currently use a single risk model for all age groups may need to reassess their protocols. The study’s authors suggest that developing a dedicated paediatric SSI risk model should be a priority for improving child surgical outcomes.

Call for New Research

The research team is now calling for further studies to build a robust, paediatric-specific risk assessment framework. They argue that such a tool would help clinicians better target preventive measures, such as antibiotic prophylaxis and wound care, to the children who need them most.

Experts not involved in the study have echoed this sentiment. Dr. Ananya Sharma, a paediatric infectious disease specialist (not quoted in the study), said that the findings align with growing awareness that children are not simply small adults in medical contexts.

The next step will involve collecting larger, multi-centre datasets to identify the key variables that predict SSI risk in children. Until such a model is validated, clinicians are advised to apply adult risk models with extreme caution in paediatric settings.
