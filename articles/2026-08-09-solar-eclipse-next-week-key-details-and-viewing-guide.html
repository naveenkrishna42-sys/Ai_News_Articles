What is a solar eclipse?

A solar eclipse happens when the Moon passes between the Sun and Earth, casting a shadow on our planet. This alignment can partially or fully block the Sun's light, depending on the observer's position. During a total eclipse, the sky darkens dramatically, and the Sun's corona becomes visible—a sight that draws crowds and inspires awe.

For those who have never witnessed one, the experience is often described as surreal. Daytime turns to twilight, temperatures drop, and animals may behave unusually. But the spectacle comes with a serious caution: looking directly at the Sun, even during an eclipse, can cause permanent eye damage.

Safety first: Protecting your eyes

Experts stress that regular sunglasses are not enough to view an eclipse safely. Specialised eclipse glasses with solar filters are essential, or viewers can use indirect methods like pinhole projectors. Without proper protection, the Sun's intense radiation can burn the retina, leading to vision loss.

Officials have not yet released a detailed advisory for this specific event, but standard guidelines apply. Viewers should ensure their eclipse glasses meet international safety standards, such as ISO 12312-2. Counterfeit glasses have been a concern in past eclipses, so buyers should verify the authenticity of their equipment.

The path of totality—where the eclipse will be fully visible—has not been fully outlined in initial reports. However, based on the source's location in Buffalo, New York, and its coverage of the Toronto region, observers in the northeastern United States and southern Ontario may have a prime vantage point. Partial phases of the eclipse are typically visible over a much wider area, so many people across the continent could catch a glimpse.

Clear skies will be a major factor. Cloud cover can ruin the view, so forecast updates closer to the date will be crucial. For those in the path, arriving early to a location with an unobstructed horizon is advisable.

What to expect during the event

As the Moon slowly covers the Sun, shadows may sharpen and light will dim. In areas of totality, the corona—a halo of plasma—will become visible for a few precious minutes. Observers often report a sense of collective wonder as the event unfolds.

Photographers should use solar filters on their cameras and lenses to avoid damaging equipment. Smartphone users can also capture images, but they should employ a solar filter or use a pinhole projection method to protect both the device and their eyes.

For those who miss this eclipse, the next major solar event may not occur for years, making this a must-see for astronomy fans. But even partial views can be memorable, provided safety measures are followed.
