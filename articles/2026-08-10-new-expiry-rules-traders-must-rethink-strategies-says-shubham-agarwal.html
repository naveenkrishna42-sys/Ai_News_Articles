New Expiry Rules: Traders must rethink strategies, says Shubham Agarwal

📅 2026-08-10 📂 Business Original source ↗
Key points
  • New expiry rules and Closing Auction Session (CAS) change trading dynamics
  • Shubham Agarwal advises traders to rethink strategies amid thin volumes
  • SEBI confident CAS will improve as participation rises despite initial hiccups

The market's new blueprint is here, and it is forcing traders to tear up their old playbooks. With the introduction of revised expiry rules and the mandatory Closing Auction Session (CAS), the final minutes of trading no longer behave the way they used to. Shubham Agarwal, a well-known market strategist, says the shift is fundamental — not cosmetic.

For decades, traders relied on the last half-hour to square off positions, chase momentum, or catch a final spike. That window has now narrowed and changed character. The CAS, which SEBI has rolled out across equity and F&O segments, aggregates orders in a single call auction after regular trading closes. The result? A price discovery mechanism that is more deliberate, but also more volatile in its early days.

Why the closing auction matters

The Closing Auction Session is not a tweak; it is a structural change. It replaces the continuous matching of orders in the final minutes with a batch auction that determines the official closing price. This means traders cannot react in real time to the last tick. They must place orders blind, hoping the auction price lands in their favour.

Agarwal points out that this alters the risk-reward calculus for intraday traders. "The closing price is now the result of a single event, not a stream of trades," he explains. "That changes how you plan exits." For those who used to hold till 3:25 pm and dump at 3:29 pm, the new system demands a decision earlier — or a willingness to accept the auction outcome.

Thin volumes at debut

The early days of CAS have been rocky. Reports from the first sessions show thin volumes, with many F&O traders sitting out. The Economic Times noted that the closing auction debut hit F&O activity, as participants adjusted to the unfamiliar rhythm. Livemint echoed this, pointing to subdued participation in the initial sessions.

This is not surprising. Any new mechanism faces a learning curve. But SEBI is confident. The regulator believes that as participation rises, the auction will deepen and become more efficient. "CAS is here to stay," a SEBI official indicated, adding that early teething problems are expected to fade.

Impact on expiry day strategies

Expiry day, once a carnival of last-minute buying and selling, is now a quieter affair. The new rules, which also include changes to weekly expiries, mean that traders cannot rely on the final burst to rescue a losing position. Agarwal advises a rethink: "Your stop-losses and targets need to be set before the auction. You cannot manage them after the fact."

For option sellers, the CAS can be a blessing. The reduced noise in the closing minutes may lower the chance of a sudden adverse move. For scalpers, it is a curse — their playground has shrunk. The key, Agarwal says, is to adapt. He suggests traders simulate auction behaviour using pre-close indications and adjust their position sizes accordingly.

The BusinessLine report added that the market is likely to adapt over time. Historical data from other markets that adopted closing auctions — such as those in Europe — shows that volumes normalise after an initial dip. The Indian market, with its retail-heavy F&O base, may take a few more weeks to settle.

What traders should watch

As the CAS matures, expect the auction price to become a more reliable reference for the next day's open. Institutional participation, especially from mutual funds and foreign investors, will likely increase, adding depth. Agarwal recommends tracking the auction volume as a percentage of total daily volume — a rising trend would signal growing comfort.

Also, keep an eye on SEBI's follow-up circulars. The regulator has promised to review the mechanism after three months. If thin volumes persist, tweaks to the order matching rules or the timing of the auction could be on the cards. Until then, traders must treat the closing auction as the new normal — and plan their exits with it in mind.

The next few weeks will be telling. If volumes pick up, as SEBI expects, the CAS will become a seamless part of the trading day. If not, the regulator may need to recalibrate. Either way, the old habits of the 3:25 pm scramble are gone for good.
