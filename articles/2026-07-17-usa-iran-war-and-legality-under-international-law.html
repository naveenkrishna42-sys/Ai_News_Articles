USA-Iran War and Legality Under International Law

📅 2026-07-17 📂 Wars & Conflicts Original source ↗
Key points
  • The US-Iran war raises critical questions about the legality of force under the UN Charter.
  • The US has cited self-defence under Article 51, but critics argue it was a preemptive strike.
  • Iran's response, including missile attacks, also faces scrutiny under international humanitarian law.
  • The UN Security Council has been unable to act due to veto powers, leaving legal questions unresolved.
  • The International Court of Justice may eventually weigh in, but no case has been filed yet.

Legal Framework for the Use of Force

The armed conflict between the United States and Iran has triggered a fierce debate among international legal scholars. At the heart of the discussion is whether either side's actions violate the United Nations Charter, the foundational treaty governing the use of force between states.

Under the Charter, Article 2(4) prohibits the threat or use of force against the territorial integrity or political independence of any state. There are only two exceptions: self-defence under Article 51, or authorisation from the UN Security Council under Chapter VII. Neither side has sought or received Security Council approval.

US Justification: Self-Defence or Preemption?

The United States has argued that its military strikes against Iranian targets were an exercise of self-defence. Washington points to Article 51, which allows a state to use force if it has been subjected to an armed attack. US officials claim Iran was planning imminent attacks against American personnel and interests in the region.

Critics counter that the US action was preemptive, not defensive. They note that the threshold for an 'armed attack' is high, and that the US has not provided concrete evidence of an imminent threat. The Bush-era doctrine of preemptive self-defence remains controversial, and many legal experts argue it stretches Article 51 beyond its intended meaning.

Iran's Response Under Scrutiny

Iran's retaliatory missile strikes against US bases in Iraq also face legal questions. While Tehran frames its actions as self-defence against US aggression, international law requires that any defensive response must be necessary and proportionate. Reports of civilian casualties or damage to civilian infrastructure could constitute violations of international humanitarian law.

The principle of distinction requires parties to a conflict to target only military objectives. If Iranian missiles struck civilian areas, even unintentionally, that could amount to a war crime. Both sides have accused each other of such violations, but independent verification remains limited.

The UN Security Council's Paralysis

The UN Security Council, tasked with maintaining international peace and security, has been unable to pass a resolution on the conflict. The United States, as a permanent member, can veto any action against its own military operations. Russia and China, meanwhile, have opposed US strikes but are unlikely to authorise force against Iran.

This deadlock leaves the legal questions in limbo. Without a binding Security Council resolution, each state is left to interpret the Charter on its own. The result is a fragmented legal landscape where power often trumps principle.

Potential Role of the International Court of Justice

The International Court of Justice (ICJ) could theoretically resolve the dispute, but neither the US nor Iran has accepted the Court's compulsory jurisdiction in matters of national security. A case would require both parties to agree to ICJ arbitration, which appears unlikely given the current hostility.

Even if a case were filed, the ICJ's rulings are binding only if states consent to enforcement. In practice, the Court's ability to stop ongoing hostilities is limited. Legal experts say the conflict will likely be judged by history, not by the Hague.

What to watch: Whether either side files a complaint at the ICJ or seeks a UN General Assembly resolution condemning the other's use of force. The diplomatic fallout could shape future norms on preemptive strikes.
