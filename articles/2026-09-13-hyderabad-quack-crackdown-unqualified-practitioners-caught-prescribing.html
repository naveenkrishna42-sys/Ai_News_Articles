Hyderabad's Quackery Problem: Unqualified Practitioners Exposed

In a sweeping crackdown on medical malpractice, Telangana health authorities have uncovered a network of unqualified practitioners operating in Hyderabad, illegally treating patients and prescribing allopathic medicines. The raids, conducted across several localities, revealed that individuals without valid medical degrees or registration were running clinics, dispensing prescription drugs, and endangering public health.

This development raises urgent questions about regulatory oversight and the ease with which fake doctors operate in the city. For residents, the implications are severe: misdiagnosis, harmful drug interactions, and delayed proper treatment can lead to life-threatening complications.

What the Investigation Found

According to official sources, the crackdown followed complaints from legitimate medical practitioners and public tip-offs. Teams from the Directorate of Medical Education and the Drugs Control Administration raided clinics in areas including Malakpet, Kukatpally, and LB Nagar. Several practitioners were found treating patients for chronic conditions like diabetes and hypertension without any formal training.

Evidence seized included fake degree certificates, forged registration documents from the Telangana State Medical Council, and unlabelled allopathic drugs. Some offenders had been operating for years, building a loyal patient base through low consultation fees and word-of-mouth referrals.

Real-World Risks for Patients

Unqualified practitioners often lack the diagnostic skills to differentiate between minor ailments and serious diseases. A common cold may be treated with antibiotics, or a cardiac event misdiagnosed as gastritis. The consequences range from antibiotic resistance to fatal delays in emergency care.

Prescribing allopathic medicines without training is particularly dangerous. These drugs have specific dosages, contraindications, and side effects. A quack may prescribe steroids for a simple rash, leading to skin atrophy or systemic complications. For children and the elderly, the risks multiply.

Legal and Regulatory Gaps

Despite existing laws, enforcement remains weak. The Telangana State Medical Council has the authority to prosecute unregistered practitioners, but cases often drag on for years. Meanwhile, clinics reopen under new names, and offenders move to different neighbourhoods.

Health department officials acknowledge that public ignorance is a key enabler. Many patients, especially from lower-income groups, prefer these practitioners due to lower fees and longer consultation hours. However, the long-term cost—both health and financial—far outweighs the short-term savings.

What Buyers (Patients) Should Check Before Choosing a Doctor

  • Verify registration: Cross-check the doctor's registration number on the Telangana State Medical Council website.
  • Check qualifications: Look for MBBS, MD, or equivalent degrees from recognised institutions. Be wary of unverifiable foreign degrees.
  • Inspect the clinic: Legitimate clinics display registration certificates, biomedical waste disposal protocols, and emergency contact numbers.
  • Ask for a prescription: Qualified doctors provide printed prescriptions with their registration number and clinic details.
  • Be sceptical of miracle cures: Promises of instant relief or permanent cures for chronic diseases are red flags.

Pros and Cons of the Crackdown

  • Pros: Increased public awareness; dismantling of illegal networks; stronger deterrent for future offenders; improved trust in legitimate healthcare.
  • Cons: Temporary disruption in access for patients who relied on these clinics; risk of offenders relocating to rural areas; slow judicial process may dilute impact.

Pricing and Value Justification

Qualified private practitioners in Hyderabad charge ₹300–₹800 per consultation, while government hospitals offer free or subsidised care. Quacks typically charge ₹50–₹150, undercutting legitimate doctors. However, the true cost of quackery includes unnecessary tests, harmful medications, and complications requiring hospitalisation—often running into thousands of rupees. Investing in a verified doctor is not an expense but a safeguard against catastrophic health and financial outcomes.

Moving Forward: Enforcement and Education

The Telangana health department plans to conduct regular audits and establish a rapid-response team for complaints. Public awareness campaigns will emphasise the dangers of unqualified practitioners. Citizens can report suspicious clinics via the health department helpline.

For now, the message is clear: your health is too precious to gamble on unverified practitioners. Always prioritise credentials over convenience.
