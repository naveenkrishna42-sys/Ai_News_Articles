Oxygen levels in Earth's waters dropping at alarming rate, scientists warn

📅 2026-07-21 📂 Science Original source ↗
Key points
  • Oxygen levels in Earth's water bodies are declining rapidly, scientists report.
  • The phenomenon, driven by climate change and pollution, could destabilize the planet.
  • Marine life and human livelihoods face severe risks if the trend continues.

Warning from researchers

Scientists have issued a stark warning: oxygen is vanishing from Earth's oceans, lakes, and rivers at a pace that could destabilise the planet. New research published in Oceanographic Magazine highlights a crisis that has been building for decades but is now accelerating.

Deoxygenation of water bodies is not a new phenomenon, but the rate of change has alarmed experts. The study, covered by outlets including Phys.org and Gizmodo, points to a future where large swathes of marine ecosystems become uninhabitable.

What is driving the loss?

Two main factors are behind the decline. Warmer water holds less dissolved oxygen, and rising global temperatures are heating up oceans and freshwater systems. At the same time, nutrient runoff from agriculture and sewage feeds algal blooms. When these blooms die and decompose, they consume vast amounts of oxygen, creating dead zones.

These dead zones are expanding. Coastal areas and inland water bodies are particularly affected, with some regions already reporting hypoxic conditions that kill fish and other aquatic life.

Global consequences

The implications go beyond marine biology. Fisheries that millions depend on for food and income are at risk. Tourism and coastal economies could take a hit as oxygen-starved waters become lifeless.

Researchers warn that the entire planet's stability is at stake. Oceans regulate the climate, absorb carbon dioxide, and support a vast web of life. If oxygen levels continue to drop, these functions could be severely impaired. The report describes this as a threat comparable to ocean acidification and warming.

A systemic crisis

Deoxygenation is not limited to the seas. Freshwater bodies such as lakes, rivers, and reservoirs are also losing oxygen. This affects drinking water quality and freshwater biodiversity. In many regions, the problem is compounded by drought and over-extraction of water.

The study's authors call for urgent action to cut greenhouse gas emissions and reduce nutrient pollution. Without such measures, they say, the trend will worsen.

What can be done

Addressing the crisis requires tackling its root causes. Transitioning to clean energy can slow global warming. Better farming practices can limit the flow of fertilisers into waterways. Wastewater treatment improvements can also help.

Some restoration efforts have shown promise. In certain areas, reducing nutrient inputs has allowed oxygen levels to recover partially. But these successes are limited in scale. The researchers stress that global coordination is essential.

Governments and international bodies have begun to take note. The issue is expected to feature prominently in upcoming climate and biodiversity summits.

The window for action is narrowing. Scientists warn that if emissions and pollution continue unchecked, the damage could become irreversible. The next decade will be critical in determining whether the world's waters can be saved from this silent but devastating change.
