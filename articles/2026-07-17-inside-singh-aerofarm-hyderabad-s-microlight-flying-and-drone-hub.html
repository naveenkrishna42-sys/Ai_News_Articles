Inside Singh Aerofarm: Hyderabad's Microlight Flying and Drone Hub

📅 2026-07-17 📂 Hyderabad Original source ↗
Key points
  • Singh Aerofarm, a 10-acre farm near Hyderabad, is used for microlight flying and drone testing.
  • The farm's owner, a flying enthusiast, established the facility to pursue his passion while maintaining agricultural operations.
  • The site has attracted drone startups and hobbyists for testing and training due to its open space and relaxed regulations.
  • The farm highlights a growing trend of using rural land for aviation activities in India.

On the outskirts of Hyderabad, a 10-acre farm is rewriting the rules of aviation. Singh Aerofarm, a working agricultural plot, has become an unlikely hub for microlight flying and drone testing. The farm's owner, a flying enthusiast, transformed part of his land into a private airstrip. What started as a personal passion project now draws pilots, hobbyists, and startups from across the region.

The farm sits about 40 kilometres from Hyderabad's main city centre. It is surrounded by fields and sparse habitation, making it ideal for low-altitude flight operations. The owner, who flies microlight aircraft himself, cleared a stretch of land to create a makeshift runway. Today, the strip hosts regular flights on weekends, with enthusiasts bringing their own aircraft or renting time on the farm's microlight.

The Drone Connection

Singh Aerofarm has also become a testing ground for drone companies. Startups working on agricultural drones, delivery quadcopters, and surveillance unmanned aerial vehicles (UAVs) use the site to run trials. The open space offers a safe environment for flight testing without the restrictions of urban airspace.

Several drone firms from Hyderabad's tech corridor have visited the farm. They test payload capacity, battery endurance, and navigation systems. The farm's location allows them to simulate rural delivery scenarios, which are crucial for India's emerging drone logistics sector. The owner charges a nominal fee for use of the strip and provides basic ground support.

Regulatory Flexibility

Microlight flying in India requires clearance from the Directorate General of Civil Aviation (DGCA). Singh Aerofarm operates under a private airstrip exemption, which allows limited recreational flying. The farm does not offer commercial flight services, but it hosts flying clubs and private pilots who meet safety standards.

For drone testing, the farm benefits from India's updated drone rules. The 2021 Drone Rules permit operation of drones below 500 kg in green zones without explicit permission. Singh Aerofarm falls under such a zone, as it is away from airports and military installations. This legal clarity has encouraged more companies to use the site for research and development.

Agricultural Roots

Despite the aviation activity, Singh Aerofarm remains a functioning farm. The owner grows seasonal crops on most of the land, and the airstrip is a narrow strip of packed earth that cuts through the fields. During harvest season, flying operations are paused to allow farm equipment to move freely.

The dual use of the land reflects a pragmatic approach. The owner told local media that farming alone could not sustain the property, so he found a supplementary use. The flying and drone activities generate enough revenue to cover maintenance costs, and they bring a community of like-minded people to the farm. "It is a way to keep the land alive," he said.

For Hyderabad's aviation enthusiasts, Singh Aerofarm fills a gap. The city's main airport, Rajiv Gandhi International Airport, is a commercial hub with no facilities for microlight flying. Private airstrips are rare in the region. The farm provides an accessible option for those who want to fly without bureaucratic hurdles.

Drone startups see the farm as a low-cost alternative to expensive testing facilities. "We can run 10 flight tests in a day here for what we would spend on a single day at a certified test centre," said a founder of a local drone company who has used the site. The farm's informal setup keeps costs low, but it also means there are no hangars, refuelling stations, or emergency services on site. Pilots and companies bring their own equipment and take responsibility for safety.

The farm has not sought formal accreditation from aviation authorities, but it follows basic safety protocols. All pilots must show valid licences, and drones are limited to below 400 feet altitude. The owner maintains a log of flights and coordinates with local air traffic control when microlights are in the air. So far, there have been no accidents or incidents reported.

Singh Aerofarm's model could inspire similar setups across India. As drone use expands in agriculture, logistics, and surveillance, the demand for testing sites will grow. Farms with large open spaces and cooperative owners could become critical infrastructure for the drone ecosystem. For now, this 10-acre plot in Hyderabad remains a quiet but active hub for those who look to the skies.

What comes next for Singh Aerofarm depends on regulatory clarity and community interest. The owner has hinted at expanding the runway and adding basic amenities. But any major expansion would require formal approvals from the DGCA and local authorities. For now, the farm continues its dual life — growing crops by day, launching drones and microlights by weekend.
