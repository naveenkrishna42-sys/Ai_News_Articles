Mission Defeat Hepatitis Expands Screening Drive Across India

📅 2026-08-04 📂 Health Original source ↗
Key points
  • Mission Defeat Hepatitis expands its screening drive to more districts and states.
  • Gastroenterologist lists yellow eyes, dark urine as key symptoms needing urgent attention.
  • Myths about spread via touching or sharing food are debunked on World Hepatitis Day.
  • Cipla partners with INASL to boost awareness and early detection efforts.

Wider Net for Screening

Mission Defeat Hepatitis, the national programme aimed at eliminating viral hepatitis, is widening its screening coverage. The expansion comes as health authorities push for early detection to curb rising liver-related complications. More districts are being brought under the ambit, with a focus on rural and semi-urban areas where access to diagnostic facilities remains thin.

The move coincides with a series of awareness drives timed around World Hepatitis Day. Public health messaging is now zeroing in on symptoms that often get ignored until the disease has progressed. According to a leading gastroenterologist, yellowing of the eyes and dark urine are among the first visible signs that warrant immediate medical consultation.

Symptoms That Should Not Be Ignored

Jaundice-like symptoms, including yellow eyes and dark-coloured urine, are classic indicators of liver distress. The doctor emphasised that these signs, along with unexplained fatigue and abdominal discomfort, should prompt people to get tested without delay. Early diagnosis can significantly improve treatment outcomes and prevent irreversible liver damage.

Yet, many patients wait until the condition becomes severe. Lack of awareness, stigma, and misinformation often delay visits to healthcare providers. Public health experts argue that routine screening, especially among high-risk groups, remains the most effective tool to change this trajectory.

Myths vs Facts on Transmission

World Hepatitis Day 2026 also saw efforts to dispel common misconceptions. Many people still believe hepatitis can spread through casual contact such as touching or sharing food. Medical experts clarify that the primary routes of transmission are blood, unprotected sex, and from mother to child during birth. Sharing meals or everyday physical contact poses no risk.

This distinction matters because fear of contagion often fuels discrimination against those living with hepatitis. Correcting these myths is not just about health—it's about dignity. Awareness campaigns are now using social media and community outreach to reach younger demographics who are most active online but often least informed about viral hepatitis.

Industry Joins the Fight

Private sector participation is also ramping up. Cipla has announced a partnership with the Indian National Association for the Study of Liver (INASL) to support hepatitis control efforts. The collaboration will focus on training healthcare workers, improving diagnostic infrastructure, and spreading awareness among vulnerable populations.

Care Health Insurance, meanwhile, is urging early screening as a preventive measure against serious liver diseases. The insurer points out that undetected hepatitis can progress to cirrhosis or liver cancer, leading to high treatment costs and reduced quality of life. Preventive care, they argue, is both a health and financial imperative.

What Next

The expanded screening drive is expected to release district-level data in the coming months. Health officials will use these figures to identify hotspots and allocate resources more effectively. For now, the message from all stakeholders is consistent: get tested, get treated, and stop the myths.
