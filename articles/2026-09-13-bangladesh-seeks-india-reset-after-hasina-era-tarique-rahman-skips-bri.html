Bangladesh Seeks India Reset After Hasina Era, Tarique Rahman Skips BRICS

Key points
  • Dhaka labels the Sheikh Hasina-era relationship with India 'uncomfortable' and wants ties rebuilt on mutual interests.
  • Bangladesh PM Tarique Rahman did not attend the Delhi BRICS Summit; Dhaka says the invite was for the BIMSTEC Chair, not BRICS.
  • The reset carries real commercial weight: trade, transit, water-sharing and border management are all back on the negotiating table.

Dhaka Signals a Strategic Reset

Bangladesh has formally signalled its intent to “reset” relations with India, describing the Sheikh Hasina era as an “uncomfortable” period that skewed the relationship toward one side. The position, articulated by the interim government in Dhaka, marks the sharpest public reframing of bilateral ties in over a decade.

The trigger was diplomatic choreography around the BRICS Summit in Delhi. Bangladesh Prime Minister Tarique Rahman did not attend. Dhaka clarified that the invitation extended to him was in his capacity as BIMSTEC Chair, not as a BRICS participant. Delhi, for its part, did not issue a separate BRICS invite. Both facts are now central to how each capital reads the other’s intent.

What Actually Changed Between Delhi and Dhaka

For fifteen years, India’s Bangladesh policy ran through one channel: Sheikh Hasina’s Awami League. Connectivity, transit and power projects moved fast, but so did allegations of asymmetric benefit. Dhaka now argues that a relationship anchored to a single party is inherently unstable.

The interim dispensation’s phrasing — “mutual interests” — is deliberate. It signals that Bangladesh wants issue-by-issue bargaining rather than blanket alignment. That is a normalisation of ties, not a rupture.

Buyer and Business Implications

For Indian exporters, the reset cuts both ways. A cooler political temperature could slow preferential access to Bangladeshi ports and power purchase arrangements that Indian firms have banked on. Simultaneously, a rules-based reset could unlock faster customs clearances and reduce the informal payments that inflate landed costs on the Dhaka route.

Bangladeshi garment buyers sourcing Indian cotton and yarn should watch the trade deficit conversation closely. Any Dhaka push for reciprocal tariff relief would lower input costs for its factories but squeeze Indian mills’ margins. Commodity traders in Kolkata and Guwahati are already pricing in a wider spread on border trade.

Pros, Cons and Real-World Highlights

  • Pro: A mutual-interest framework reduces the risk of ties collapsing whenever Bangladesh’s domestic politics shift.
  • Pro: Issue-linked negotiation could finally move Teesta water-sharing, long stalled by single-channel diplomacy.
  • Con: Indian infrastructure and power exporters face uncertainty on existing and pipeline contracts.
  • Con: The BRICS snub, however procedural, gives hardliners in both capitals ammunition.
  • Watch: Visa issuance speed and border management will be the earliest measurable tests of the reset.

The Road Ahead

Delhi has so far avoided public commentary on Dhaka’s “uncomfortable” characterisation. That restraint is itself a signal — India appears willing to let the rhetoric settle before responding on substance. The next concrete markers will be a BIMSTEC-side meeting, progress on visa backlogs, and whether Dhaka renews transit commitments on schedule.

For businesses on both sides of the border, the practical question is simple: does the reset translate into predictable paperwork and stable pricing, or into a prolonged renegotiation? Early indicators suggest a slow, transactional thaw rather than a clean break.
