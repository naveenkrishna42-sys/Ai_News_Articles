AI and Ramanomics may unlock secrets of living cells

📅 2026-08-08 📂 Technology Original source ↗
Key points
  • Parmita Mishra's startup Precigenetics uses AI and chip tech to study living cells.
  • The approach, dubbed 'Ramanomics', could overcome a major hurdle in cell biology.
  • This innovation may accelerate drug discovery and reduce reliance on invasive methods.

BENGALURU: A new approach combining artificial intelligence with chip-based technology could remove a long-standing barrier in biology — the difficulty of studying living cells without altering them. The method, dubbed 'Ramanomics', is being developed by Parmita Mishra through her startup Precigenetics, and has caught the attention of researchers and industry watchers alike.

Traditional techniques for examining cells often involve fixing, staining, or destroying them, which can distort the very processes scientists hope to observe. Ramanomics aims to sidestep this by using label-free imaging — a way to read the chemical fingerprint of a cell without injecting dyes or tags. The challenge, however, has been the sheer complexity of the data generated, which is where AI steps in.

The chip-and-AI combo

Precigenetics is building a platform that marries microfluidic chips with machine learning algorithms. Cells are guided through tiny channels on a chip, where Raman spectroscopy captures their molecular signatures in real time. The AI then sifts through vast datasets to identify patterns that would be nearly impossible for humans to parse manually.

This pairing is not entirely new in principle, but Mishra's application to living-cell analysis is drawing attention for its potential commercial viability. The technology could allow researchers to monitor how cells respond to drugs, toxins, or genetic changes without ever disturbing them — a leap forward from current methods that often rely on endpoint measurements.

Why 'Ramanomics' matters

The term 'Ramanomics' reflects a fusion of Raman spectroscopy and 'omics' — the biological disciplines that study entire systems, such as genomics or proteomics. In this case, the focus is on the complete molecular profile of a cell as it lives and divides. This could offer a dynamic view of cellular behaviour, rather than a static snapshot.

For drug discovery, the implications are significant. Pharmaceutical companies spend years and billions of rupees on candidate molecules that fail late in the pipeline because of unforeseen effects on cells. A tool that can observe those effects earlier and more accurately could cut costs and speed up development.

Early-stage promise, unanswered questions

Mishra's work is still in its early stages, and details about the platform's sensitivity, throughput, and cost remain scarce. The startup has not yet published peer-reviewed results, and questions linger over how easily the technology can scale from lab prototypes to industrial use.

Experts note that Raman spectroscopy has been around for decades, but its application to live-cell analysis has been hampered by weak signals and slow acquisition times. Whether AI can overcome these physical limitations is still an open question. Some researchers are cautiously optimistic, pointing out that similar hurdles have been tackled in other fields like imaging and genomics.

Precigenetics has not disclosed its funding or partnerships, and it remains unclear when the first commercial product might hit the market. The company's website offers little beyond a general description of its mission, suggesting the team is keeping details under wraps until more data is available.

What to watch next

Over the coming months, the scientific community will be watching for peer-reviewed validation and pilot studies from independent labs. If Precigenetics can demonstrate reproducible results, Ramanomics could become a standard tool in biomedical research. Until then, the promise remains just that — a promise waiting for proof.
