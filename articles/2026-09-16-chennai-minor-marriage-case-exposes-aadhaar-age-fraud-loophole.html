A Chennai Arrest That Raises National Questions

Chennai police arrested a man for marrying a minor, while relatives allegedly attempted to flee with the girl using a forged Aadhaar card. The case, first reported by The New Indian Express, is not a one-off crime story. It is a live demonstration of how India's most trusted identity document can be weaponised to override the legal age of marriage and evade child protection law.

The Prohibition of Child Marriage Act, 2006, sets the minimum legal age for marriage at 18 for women and 21 for men. When a family produces a fake Aadhaar showing an adult age, the entire enforcement chain — registration, police verification, and even hospital records — is compromised at the entry point.

How the Alleged Fraud Worked

According to police accounts, the minor's family obtained an Aadhaar card listing a fabricated date of birth. This gave the marriage a veneer of legality. When authorities intervened, relatives allegedly tried to leave with the girl, deepening suspicions of coordinated concealment.

The Aadhaar ecosystem links biometrics to a declared date of birth, but the declaration itself is often supported by self-attested documents. Unlike a passport or a birth certificate issued at a municipal level, Aadhaar enrolment does not automatically cross-validate age against a statutory birth register. This is the structural weakness that fraudsters exploit.

Why This Matters Beyond Chennai

Child marriage in India has declined, but it has not disappeared. NFHS-5 data showed that nearly one in four women aged 20–24 were married before 18. The persistence of the practice is closely tied to documentation loopholes rather than ignorance of the law alone.

Aadhaar is now mandatory for school admissions, bank accounts, SIM cards, and marriage registration in many states. Once a fake date of birth enters that ecosystem, it becomes self-reinforcing. The minor can appear adult in every subsequent transaction, making retrospective detection extremely difficult.

Real-World Buyer and Citizen Implications

  • Institutional risk: Schools, hospitals, and marriage registrars relying solely on Aadhaar for age proof inherit the fraud.
  • Verification cost: Genuine citizens face longer verification queues when authorities tighten checks after each scandal.
  • Legal exposure: Knowingly facilitating a marriage with forged documents can attract forgery charges under Sections 463–471 IPC, independent of child marriage provisions.
  • Data trust deficit: Every exposed forgery erodes confidence in Aadhaar as a single source of truth.

Pros and Cons of the Current Aadhaar Verification Model

  • Pro: Aadhaar provides a near-universal, low-cost identity backbone covering over 130 crore residents.
  • Pro: Biometric locking and authentication reduce duplicate enrolments significantly.
  • Con: Date of birth is largely self-declared, with no mandatory link to a birth certificate.
  • Con: Local enrolment operators face pressure and lack forensic document training.
  • Con: Penalties for fraudulent enrolment are rarely pursued to conviction, weakening deterrence.

The Fix: Verification, Not Just Legislation

Tamil Nadu has one of India's stronger child protection machinery, yet this case surfaced in Chennai. The lesson is that laws alone cannot close the gap. What is needed is mandatory cross-validation of Aadhaar dates of birth against municipal birth registers for anyone below 21, periodic biometric re-verification for high-risk documents, and criminal liability for enrolment agents who process forged proofs.

For families, institutions, and buyers of identity-dependent services, the takeaway is blunt: Aadhaar is a verification tool, not a birth certificate. Treating it as the final word on age is precisely the assumption that this Chennai case has now shattered.
