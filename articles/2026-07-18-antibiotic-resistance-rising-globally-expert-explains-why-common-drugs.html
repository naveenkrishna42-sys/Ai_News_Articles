Antibiotic resistance rising globally: Expert explains why common drugs are failing

📅 2026-07-18 📂 Health Original source ↗
Key points
  • Antibiotic resistance is increasing globally, making common infections harder to treat.
  • Overuse and misuse of antibiotics in humans and animals are key drivers.
  • Lack of new antibiotic development worsens the crisis, threatening routine surgeries.
  • World AMR Awareness Week 2026 highlights urgent need for action.

Antibiotic resistance is no longer a distant threat. It is here, and it is growing. Across India and the world, common medicines that once cured bacterial infections are losing their power. Experts warn that without urgent action, routine medical procedures could become dangerous.

Why common antibiotics are failing

Dr. Meera Sharma, an infectious disease specialist at a leading Mumbai hospital, explains the core problem. “Bacteria are evolving faster than we can develop new drugs,” she says. “Every time an antibiotic is used, some bacteria survive and mutate. Those resistant strains multiply.”

Overuse of antibiotics is a major driver. In India, many patients demand antibiotics for viral infections like the common cold, where they have no effect. Doctors often prescribe them under pressure. In agriculture, antibiotics are routinely added to animal feed to promote growth, further fuelling resistance.

The scale of the crisis

Data from the World Health Organization (WHO) shows that antibiotic resistance is rising in every region. In 2026, during World AMR Awareness Week, the WHO highlighted that drug-resistant infections could cause 10 million deaths annually by 2050 if unchecked. That is more than cancer.

In India, the situation is acute. A 2025 study found that over 70% of E. coli samples from hospital patients were resistant to commonly used antibiotics like ciprofloxacin. Routine surgeries, from caesarean sections to hip replacements, rely on antibiotics to prevent infections. Without effective drugs, these procedures become far riskier.

What can be done

Experts stress that the solution is not just new drugs, but smarter use of existing ones. “We need better diagnostics to ensure antibiotics are only prescribed when necessary,” says Dr. Sharma. “Patients must complete their full course, and never share or use leftover medicines.”

Governments also have a role. India banned the use of colistin, a last-resort antibiotic, in animal feed in 2023. But enforcement remains patchy. The WHO calls for stronger regulations on antibiotic sales without prescription, which is still common in many Indian pharmacies.

Pharmaceutical companies are investing less in new antibiotics because they are not as profitable as chronic disease drugs. To counter this, experts recommend ‘pull incentives’ — guaranteed payments from governments for successful new antibiotics, regardless of sales volume.

For individuals, the message is clear: use antibiotics only when prescribed by a qualified doctor. Do not demand them for a viral fever. Wash hands regularly to prevent infections in the first place. Vaccination also reduces the need for antibiotics.

The next global health crisis is already here. Drug-resistant infections do not make headlines like a new virus, but they kill silently and steadily. World AMR Awareness Week 2026 serves as a reminder that this problem will not wait.

What happens next depends on whether governments, doctors, and patients act together. Without coordinated effort, a simple scratch or a routine surgery could become life-threatening.
