Govt tightens ISRO exit rules as mass resignation trend sparks concern

📅 2026-07-18 📂 Top Stories Original source ↗
Key points
  • Government orders ISRO to stop routine acceptance of resignations from mission-critical staff
  • Gaganyaan team members face additional restrictions on leaving the organisation
  • ISRO's vacancy rate has climbed to a 25-year high, with nearly 30% of posts vacant
  • Over 100 scientists have quit or taken early retirement in recent months

The Centre has directed the Indian Space Research Organisation (ISRO) to stop routinely accepting resignations from scientists, responding to a surge in exits that has left nearly three in ten posts vacant — a 25-year high for the agency.

The new directive, issued by the Department of Space, specifically bars automatic clearance of resignations from staff attached to the Gaganyaan human spaceflight mission. Officials familiar with the order said the move is intended to stem the loss of experienced personnel at a critical juncture for India's space programme.

Why ISRO is making it harder to quit

Sources within the department confirmed that the instruction is not a blanket ban on resignations, but it tightens the process significantly. Managers of individual projects must now provide written justification before any resignation is forwarded for approval.

For scientists on the Gaganyaan team, the rules are even stricter. The government has directed that such resignations require explicit sign-off from the Secretary of the Department of Space — a senior bureaucrat — rather than being processed at the directorate level as was common earlier.

One official, speaking on condition of anonymity, said the order came after internal reviews showed that several key scientists had left without a proper handover of responsibilities. 'The concern is not just about numbers. It is about losing people with specialised knowledge that takes years to build,' the official said.

Vacancy rate at a 25-year high

Data accessed from the Department of Space shows that as of March 2026, the overall vacancy rate across ISRO centres stood at 28.7%. This is the highest since at least 2001. The agency employs roughly 16,000 scientists and engineers, but over 4,500 positions remain unfilled.

The situation is particularly acute in the human spaceflight programme, where attrition has been concentrated. Over 100 ISRO scientists have either resigned or taken voluntary retirement in the past 12 months, according to records tabled in Parliament earlier this year.

Industry observers point to a combination of factors: growing competition from private space startups offering higher salaries, a perception of slow career progression within ISRO, and the emotional toll of working on high-stakes missions with limited flexibility.

What the new rules mean for scientists

The order does not alter the contractual obligations of ISRO employees, but it changes how quickly — and easily — a resignation can be processed. Scientists who wish to leave must now demonstrate that they have completed critical project milestones or that their departure will not jeopardise mission timelines.

In practice, this could mean longer notice periods for individuals working on Gaganyaan, which aims to send Indian astronauts into orbit by 2028. Some scientists may also face restrictions on moving directly to private companies, though the government has not announced any non-compete clauses.

Critics within the scientific community argue that the policy could backfire. 'If people feel trapped, they will leave anyway, but they will do so quietly — and possibly without proper transitions,' said a former ISRO official who spoke to TIVRA News on background.

What happens next

The Department of Space is expected to review the impact of the new exit rules within six months. Separately, ISRO has launched a recruitment drive aimed at filling 1,200 scientific and technical positions, with special incentives for candidates with experience in human spaceflight systems. Whether the tightened rules will slow the exodus — or accelerate it — remains to be seen.
