RRI scientists predict petal-like corona structures for 2026 eclipse

📅 2026-08-13 📂 Science Original source ↗
Key points
  • RRI-led team predicts 'petal-like' structures in the Sun's corona during the 2026 total solar eclipse.
  • The prediction will be tested against observations from ESA, NASA, and ground-based telescopes.
  • The eclipse provides a rare opportunity to study the Sun's faint outer atmosphere.
  • Indian researchers aim to validate their models of coronal dynamics and space weather forecasting.

A rare celestial alignment

On August 12, 2026, a total solar eclipse will sweep across parts of Europe, plunging daytime skies into twilight for a few fleeting minutes. For scientists, this is more than a spectacle—it is a window into the Sun's hidden outer atmosphere, the corona, which is normally drowned out by the Sun's brilliant glare.

A team led by researchers from the Raman Research Institute (RRI) in Bengaluru has now made a bold prediction: the corona during this eclipse will display petal-like structures, shaped by the Sun's magnetic field and the complex dance of plasma in its outer layers.

What the prediction means

The corona is a million-degree halo of ionised gas that extends millions of kilometres into space. Despite decades of study, its exact structure remains difficult to model because it is so faint and dynamic. The RRI team's prediction, based on advanced magnetohydrodynamic simulations, suggests that during the eclipse, the corona will appear as a series of radially aligned petals, or streamers, that twist and bend according to the Sun's magnetic field lines.

These structures are not just visually striking; they hold clues to how the solar wind is accelerated and how space weather events originate. If the prediction holds true, it would validate the team's model, which could improve our ability to forecast solar storms that affect satellites, power grids, and communication systems on Earth.

Testing the model

To test this prediction, a global array of instruments will be trained on the Sun during the eclipse. The European Space Agency is using its Proba-3 mission to mimic the eclipse artificially in space, creating sustained coronagraphic observations. Meanwhile, NASA plans to chase the eclipse with balloons and a high-altitude jet, capturing images from the stratosphere to avoid atmospheric distortion.

Ground-based telescopes across Europe and India will also contribute, snapping high-resolution images of the corona's fine structure. The RRI team will compare these observations with their simulations, hoping to see the predicted petals in the data.

Why the corona matters

The corona is the source of the solar wind, a stream of charged particles that constantly flows outwards from the Sun. When intense solar activity ejects large clouds of plasma, they can travel towards Earth and cause geomagnetic storms. Being able to predict the corona's structure is therefore not just an academic exercise—it is a practical tool for protecting our technological infrastructure.

Indian scientists have been increasingly active in solar physics, and this project is a testament to that growing expertise. The RRI team's work, supported by the Indian Space Research Organisation and other institutions, positions India as a key player in international solar research.

What to watch next

As the eclipse passes, the world will be watching the sky—and the data streams. The RRI team's prediction will be put to the test, and the results could reshape our understanding of the Sun's corona. If the petals appear as expected, it will be a milestone for Indian space science; if not, the discrepancy will open new questions for the next solar cycle.
