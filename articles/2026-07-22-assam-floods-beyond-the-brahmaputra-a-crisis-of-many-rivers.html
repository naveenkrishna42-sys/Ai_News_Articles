Assam Floods: Beyond the Brahmaputra, a Crisis of Many Rivers

📅 2026-07-22 📂 Top Stories Original source ↗
Key points
  • Assam flood toll reaches 31, with 21 deaths in a single day.
  • Over 5.6 lakh people affected across 16 districts.
  • Army deployed for rescue operations, saving 571 people.
  • Multiple rivers, including the Brahmaputra, are breaching danger marks.
  • Deforestation and climate change are exacerbating the crisis.

The Rising Toll of Assam's Deluge

Assam is drowning. The state's annual flood misery has taken a deadly turn, with 21 people losing their lives in just one day. This brings the total flood-related death toll to 31 since the monsoon season intensified.

Over 5.6 lakh people are now affected across 16 districts. The army has been deployed in full force, rescuing 571 people stranded in marooned villages. The situation is dire, and officials are scrambling to reach those cut off by rising waters.

Why the Brahmaputra Isn't the Whole Story

The immediate assumption is that the Brahmaputra River is the sole culprit. But that is only part of the picture. The Brahmaputra, swelling with meltwater and relentless rain, has breached its danger mark in several places. However, it is the network of its tributaries—the Jia Bharali, the Kopili, the Beki—that is causing the most severe damage.

These rivers, fed by deforestation in the hills and choked by silt, spill over their banks with little warning. Many of them have changed course over the years, flooding areas that were once considered safe. The result is a crisis that is both predictable and, year after year, devastating.

Human Cost and Rescue Efforts

The human cost is staggering. Entire villages are underwater. People are living on embankments and makeshift rafts. The army's rescue operation, which saved 571 people, is a lifeline, but it is a temporary fix. The vast scale of the disaster means many remain isolated.

Roads have been washed away, and rail links snapped. The National Disaster Response Force (NDRF) is working alongside the army, but the sheer volume of water makes access difficult. In many areas, boats are the only mode of transport.

Deeper Causes: Deforestation and Climate Change

Experts point to deeper causes. Deforestation in the upper catchment areas, both in Assam and in neighbouring Arunachal Pradesh, has reduced the land's ability to absorb rainfall. The result is faster runoff and more severe flooding downstream.

Climate change is another factor. The monsoon is becoming more erratic, with intense rainfall events concentrated over shorter periods. The Brahmaputra basin is particularly vulnerable to these shifts. The rivers cannot handle the sudden deluge, and they overflow their banks with deadly force.

Encroachment on floodplains has also made the situation worse. As people build homes and farms on land that was always meant to be a river's escape route, the damage from annual floods increases. It is a cycle of destruction and rebuilding that seems impossible to break.

What happens next depends on the weather. The monsoon is forecast to continue, and rivers are expected to remain above danger levels for days. The immediate priority is rescue and relief, but the long-term questions about how to manage Assam's water crisis will persist long after the waters recede.
