Why Ayushmann Khurrana’s call for small-budget films matters to buyers

Ayushmann Khurrana’s pitch for more “small-budget” movies such as Hanuman Ansh and Obsession is not only a creative preference—it’s a commercial strategy. At events like FICCI FRAMES, the conversation increasingly shifts from star power alone to production efficiency, theatrical viability, and distribution confidence. For producers, distributors, and commercial partners, smaller budgets can make pipeline planning more predictable and reduce downside when audience taste shifts.

In practical terms, a small-budget film allows buyers—especially digital platforms, satellite channels, and theatrical distributors—to evaluate projects on clearer metrics: cost-to-revenue ratios, content differentiation, and marketing reach. When costs are contained, even modest performance can translate into acceptable unit economics, especially when ancillary rights (OTT, music, dubbing, and syndication) are planned early.

The business model: lower risk, faster iteration, smarter packaging

Small-budget films typically win by focusing on script clarity and production discipline. The buyer’s lens is straightforward: if the film’s creative premise is tightly executed, it can travel across audiences without requiring an oversized marketing burn. That often means smaller shooting schedules, fewer expensive sets, and a deliberate approach to VFX and post-production.

Khurrana’s emphasis on being “budget-friendly” aligns with how rights marketplaces behave today. Platforms prefer projects that can be promoted with a strong hook—whether it’s a thriller premise, a character-driven arc, or a culturally resonant concept—without relying entirely on heavy theatrical spends. From a commercial buying perspective, the film becomes easier to underwrite.

Still, “small budget” doesn’t automatically mean “small ambition.” Buyers look for a professional baseline: pacing that holds attention, sound and grading that meet broadcast/OTT standards, and a narrative strong enough to survive algorithmic discovery. Titles like Hanuman Ansh and Obsession are frequently cited in this context because they demonstrate that budget control can coexist with audience pull.

What benchmarks a commercial buyer should ask for

If you’re assessing a small-budget film as a rights or distribution buyer, demand evidence in the form of unit economics and execution readiness. Ask for a budget split (production vs post vs marketing), a realistic release plan, and an understanding of audience targeting. These details determine whether the project can scale or stalls after initial traction.

Marketing also needs measurable assumptions: trailer engagement expectations, press and influencer strategy, and whether festival/industry events are being used for sales momentum. In many cases, buyers see higher confidence when the marketing plan is built around content-led positioning rather than pure celebrity reach.

FICCI FRAMES-style visibility: distribution confidence and partnerships

Industry platforms like FICCI FRAMES function as deal-making accelerators. When talent such as Khurrana publicly backs smaller films, it can improve sentiment among distributors and brand partners—especially when paired with clear creative identity. Buyers interpret such statements as a market signal: demand exists for content that is easier to finance and can be packaged effectively across windows.

For commercial buyers, visibility at these events can help shortlist projects faster. It also encourages co-financing models where brands and rights holders share upside tied to performance indicators. When rights are structured early, the film’s cashflow becomes less dependent on a single outcome.

Rights strategy: how “small budget” changes negotiations

Smaller budgets often shift terms toward multi-window plans. Instead of betting heavily on one theatrical window, buyers may negotiate staggered OTT, satellite, and music rights, backed by performance triggers. The practical implication: buyers can justify payments more confidently when the film’s cost profile is transparent.

However, buyers should still pressure-test post-release discoverability. A film can be financially lean and still underperform if the marketing fails or if the storyline lacks a clear “why now” hook for mainstream audiences.

Key real-world highlights for producers and rights buyers

  • Lower underwriting risk: Budget discipline can improve ROI odds when collections or streaming traction are only moderate.
  • Better experimentation: Smaller scale enables genre and narrative variety without jeopardizing the entire slate.
  • Clearer packaging: Rights buyers can structure deals across windows when costs are predictable.
  • Marketing must be smarter, not bigger: Content-led promotion usually outperforms star-only push in cost-controlled campaigns.
  • Execution quality remains non-negotiable: Audio, sound mix, and grading quality must meet theatrical/OTT expectations.

Pros & Cons for the commercial buyer

  • Pros: Faster decision cycles, more projects to choose from, and a stronger ability to tailor deals around performance indicators.
  • Cons: Higher reliance on strong writing and audience targeting; weak marketing execution can disproportionately impact returns.
  • Trade-off: You may negotiate with more flexibility, but you must verify production readiness to avoid quality compromises.

What to do next if you’re shopping for small-budget films

If you’re a distributor, OTT buyer, or brand partner, treat “small-budget” as a framework, not a shortcut. Request detailed budget heads, a post-production timeline, and a distribution plan that matches audience targeting. Then assess whether the film’s premise is strong enough to travel across theatrical and digital discovery channels.

Khurrana’s advocacy for more such films is likely to keep shaping buyer preferences: projects that can be financed efficiently, executed professionally, and marketed with measurable clarity will earn attention faster. The winning formula isn’t just affordability—it’s disciplined production plus a story that holds attention long enough for marketing to convert into audiences.
