Jupiter's Moon Europa Holds More Liquid Water Than Earth, New Data Shows

📅 2026-07-27 📂 Science Original source ↗
Key points
  • Europa's subsurface ocean is estimated to contain more than twice the volume of all Earth's oceans combined.
  • New analysis of old spacecraft data and improved models confirm a global, salty ocean beneath Europa's ice shell.
  • The finding complicates the search for life due to limited fluid exchange between the deep ocean and the icy surface.

The Ocean Beyond Earth

Jupiter's moon Europa has long been a prime candidate in the search for extraterrestrial life. Now, fresh analysis confirms what scientists suspected for decades: this icy world likely holds more liquid water than Earth itself.

New data, drawn from re-examined spacecraft observations and advanced computer models, indicates that Europa's subsurface ocean is a global feature. Its volume is estimated to be more than double the combined volume of all the oceans on our planet.

A Hidden, Salty Sea

The evidence points to a salty ocean, not just a slushy layer, trapped beneath a frozen crust that could be tens of kilometres thick. The water is kept liquid by the immense tidal forces exerted by Jupiter's gravity, which generate heat within the moon's interior.

This internal heat, combined with the presence of salts and likely hydrothermal vents on the seafloor, creates an environment that could, in theory, support microbial life. The ocean is in direct contact with a rocky mantle, a key ingredient for the chemical reactions that sustain life on Earth.

New Challenges for Finding Life

However, the latest findings also introduce a significant complication. The research shows that there is very limited direct fluid exchange between the deep subsurface ocean and the shallower environment just beneath the ice.

This separation means that any potential signs of life—organic molecules or chemical byproducts—from the deep ocean may never reach the surface. For future missions, which plan to drill or sample the ice, this poses a serious problem. They might find a sterile surface even if a thriving ecosystem exists kilometres below.

A Growing Family of Ocean Worlds

Europa is not alone. At least six moons in our solar system are now thought to hide similar oceans. The list includes Saturn's Enceladus and Titan, and Jupiter's Ganymede and Callisto. As old data is re-analysed and new missions like NASA's Europa Clipper prepare to launch, scientists expect more surprises.

The discovery that such worlds are common has shifted the focus of astrobiology. The question is no longer whether liquid water exists elsewhere, but whether life has managed to take hold in these dark, isolated seas.

What comes next is the hard part. The Europa Clipper mission, set to arrive in the early 2030s, will perform multiple flybys to map the ice shell and study the ocean's composition. It may not find life, but it will tell scientists exactly where to look—and how deep they will have to drill.
