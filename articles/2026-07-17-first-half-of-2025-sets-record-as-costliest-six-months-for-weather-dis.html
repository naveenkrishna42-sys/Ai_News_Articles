First Half of 2025 Sets Record as Costliest Six Months for Weather Disasters

📅 2026-07-17 📂 Environment Original source ↗
Key points
  • First half of 2025 recorded the highest economic losses from weather disasters in history.
  • Data from Environment America shows damages exceeding earlier records.
  • Extreme events like floods, storms, and wildfires drove the costs.

The first six months of 2025 have become the most expensive period for weather disasters since records began, according to a new analysis by Environment America. The damages from floods, storms, wildfires and other extreme events surpassed all previous half-year tallies.

The group's report, based on federal data, puts the total economic loss at well over $100 billion. That figure eclipses the previous record set in the first half of 2021. Officials have not yet released a final country-by-country breakdown, but the United States accounted for a significant share.

What Drove the Record Costs

Several major disasters struck within weeks of each other. A series of powerful winter storms in January caused widespread power outages and property damage across the Midwest and Northeast. February brought severe flooding to parts of California, where atmospheric rivers overwhelmed levees and flooded farmland.

Spring saw an unusually active tornado season. A single outbreak in late April across the southern Plains caused billions in damage. Wildfires in Canada and the western U.S. also contributed, with smoke and destruction extending into early summer.

Environment America noted that the frequency of billion-dollar events has been rising. The first half of 2025 saw at least 12 such disasters, compared to an average of six in previous decades.

Climate Change and Insurance Pressures

Scientists have long warned that a warming planet increases the intensity and cost of extreme weather. Warmer air holds more moisture, leading to heavier rainfall. Hotter, drier conditions fuel wildfires. The report does not directly attribute any single event to climate change, but it cites broader trends consistent with climate models.

The record losses are already affecting insurance markets. Several major insurers have raised premiums or pulled out of high-risk states like Florida and California. Homeowners in flood-prone areas face rising costs and limited coverage options.

Global Picture and Comparisons

While the U.S. saw the highest absolute losses, other regions also suffered. Cyclones in the Bay of Bengal caused heavy damage in Bangladesh and India. Flooding in Brazil and parts of Europe added to the tally. The report estimates that global economic losses from weather disasters in the first half of 2025 exceeded $200 billion.

That figure includes insured and uninsured losses. Many developing nations lack the financial buffers to recover quickly. The gap between rich and poor countries' ability to rebuild remains stark.

Infrastructure and Preparedness Gaps

The report also highlights how outdated infrastructure worsens the damage. In many areas, drainage systems, levees and power grids were not designed for the current intensity of storms. Upgrades are expensive and slow, leaving communities exposed.

Emergency managers have struggled to keep up. Federal disaster funds have been depleted faster than in prior years. Some states have had to request additional aid, raising questions about long-term resilience planning.

What to watch: The second half of 2025 has already begun with hurricane season in the Atlantic and monsoon rains in South Asia. If the pace of costly disasters continues, the full-year total could break records as well. Insurers and governments are watching closely.
