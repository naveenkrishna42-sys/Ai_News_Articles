Five healthier habits doctors recommend over intermittent fasting

📅 2026-08-04 📂 Lifestyle Original source ↗
Key points
  • Experts advise balanced nutrition over restrictive eating windows
  • Consistent sleep and stress management are crucial for metabolic health
  • Strength training and mindful eating support sustainable weight control
  • Hydration and regular meals help regulate appetite and energy

Why doctors are rethinking intermittent fasting

Intermittent fasting has become one of the most popular diet trends in India, with celebrities and influencers touting its benefits for weight loss and longevity. But many doctors are now urging caution, pointing out that the approach may not be suitable for everyone—and that there are simpler, more sustainable habits that deliver better long-term results.

While fasting can help some people reduce calorie intake, it often leads to overeating during eating windows, nutrient deficiencies, and disrupted sleep. Physicians are increasingly recommending a different set of priorities that focus on overall health rather than just the clock.

1. Eat a balanced plate at every meal

Instead of skipping meals, doctors suggest filling your plate with a mix of proteins, healthy fats, complex carbohydrates, and fibre. This combination keeps blood sugar stable and prevents the energy crashes that often follow high-carb meals.

A simple rule: half the plate with vegetables, a quarter with lean protein, and a quarter with whole grains. This approach ensures you get essential vitamins and minerals without feeling deprived.

2. Prioritise consistent sleep

Sleep is often overlooked in weight management conversations, but it plays a critical role in regulating hunger hormones. Poor sleep increases ghrelin, which stimulates appetite, and decreases leptin, which signals fullness.

Doctors recommend aiming for seven to nine hours of quality sleep each night. Keeping a fixed sleep schedule—even on weekends—helps your body maintain its natural rhythms, making it easier to make healthy food choices during the day.

3. Move your body, especially with strength training

Cardio is great, but strength training is essential for preserving muscle mass, which naturally declines with age. More muscle means a higher resting metabolic rate, helping you burn more calories even at rest.

You don't need a gym membership—bodyweight exercises like squats, lunges, and push-ups are effective. Aim for at least two sessions per week, and gradually increase intensity as you get stronger.

4. Practice mindful eating

Mindful eating means paying full attention to your food—savouring each bite, chewing slowly, and stopping when you're satisfied, not stuffed. This practice helps you recognise true hunger and fullness cues, reducing the likelihood of overeating.

In our fast-paced lives, meals are often rushed or eaten in front of screens. Setting aside even ten minutes to eat without distractions can make a significant difference in how much you consume and how well you digest.

5. Stay hydrated throughout the day

Thirst is often mistaken for hunger, leading to unnecessary snacking. Drinking water regularly—especially before meals—can help control portion sizes and improve digestion.

Doctors suggest starting your day with a glass of water, keeping a bottle handy at your desk, and choosing water over sugary drinks. Herbal teas and infused water with lemon or cucumber are good alternatives if plain water feels boring.

Why these habits beat fasting

Unlike restrictive eating windows, these habits are flexible and can be adapted to any lifestyle. They don't require willpower to skip meals; instead, they build a foundation of consistent, healthy choices that are easier to maintain over the long term.

Intermittent fasting may work for some, but it often leads to a cycle of restriction and bingeing, especially in social settings. The five habits above address the root causes of weight gain—poor sleep, stress, lack of movement, and mindless eating—without the side effects of extreme dieting.

As always, it's wise to consult a healthcare professional before making significant changes to your diet or exercise routine, particularly if you have underlying health conditions.

In the coming months, researchers are likely to publish more data on the long-term effects of intermittent fasting. For now, doctors agree that slow and steady wins the race—and that building sustainable habits is the surest path to lasting health.
