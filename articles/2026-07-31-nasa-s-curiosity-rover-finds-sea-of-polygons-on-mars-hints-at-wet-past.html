NASA's Curiosity rover finds 'sea of polygons' on Mars, hints at wet past

📅 2026-07-31 📂 Science Original source ↗
Key points
  • Curiosity rover spotted a large area covered in polygon or honeycomb-like patterns.
  • The formations may have been created by repeated wet-dry cycles, hinting at past habitable conditions.
  • Scientists say the discovery could help understand whether Mars once supported microbial life.

NASA's Curiosity rover has stumbled upon a striking geological feature on Mars – a vast expanse of polygon-shaped patterns, resembling a honeycomb, spread across the surface. The discovery, announced this week, is offering fresh insights into the Red Planet's ancient environment and its potential to have supported life.

A landscape like no other

The rover, which has been exploring Gale Crater since 2012, captured images of what scientists are calling a 'sea of polygons'. These repeating, hexagonal or pentagonal shapes stretch across the terrain, forming a pattern never seen before in such scale on Mars.

According to NASA, the formations are likely the result of repeated cycles of wetting and drying in the planet's distant past. As water evaporated and the ground contracted, cracks formed in a regular, geometric pattern – a process that has been observed in some desert environments on Earth.

Clues to a wetter Mars

The presence of these polygons suggests that Mars was not always the cold, arid desert we see today. Instead, it may have experienced periodic floods or groundwater upwellings, followed by long dry spells. This kind of cyclic climate could have created conditions favourable for microbial life, if it ever existed.

Scientists are particularly excited because similar patterns on Earth are often associated with environments where life thrives. The discovery adds to a growing body of evidence that Gale Crater once hosted a lake, and possibly a dynamic, habitable environment.

What the polygons tell us

Each polygon measures anywhere from a few centimetres to a couple of metres across. Their uniform shape points to a consistent process – likely the expansion and contraction of wet soil as it dried out. Over time, these cracks filled with sediment, preserving the pattern for billions of years.

Curiosity's onboard instruments are now analysing the chemical composition of the rocks within the polygons. Early data suggests the presence of minerals that form in water, such as sulphates and clays, which further support the theory of a wetter past.

While Curiosity has found other signs of ancient water before – including pebbles worn smooth by streams and mudstone that once held organic molecules – this is the first time such an extensive network of polygons has been documented.

Implications for future missions

The discovery comes as NASA prepares for its next-generation rover, which is designed to search for signs of ancient life directly. Understanding where and how these polygon fields formed will help mission planners choose the most promising landing sites.

It also strengthens the case for sending a sample-return mission to Mars, as rocks from these formations could hold preserved biosignatures – chemical clues that life once existed. Scientists say the polygons could act as a natural archive of the planet's climate history, much like ice cores on Earth.

The findings were published in a peer-reviewed journal, though NASA officials have not yet released full details. The agency has said that further analysis is underway.

As Curiosity continues its slow climb up Mount Sharp, the team hopes to find more such patterns at higher elevations, which could reveal how long these wet-dry cycles persisted. Each new discovery brings us closer to answering the biggest question of all: was Mars ever truly alive?
