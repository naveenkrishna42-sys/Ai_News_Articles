Why Sleeping With Lights On, Even Dim, Can Disrupt Your Sleep

📅 2026-08-09 📂 Health Original source ↗
Key points
  • Even dim light during sleep can suppress melatonin and disrupt circadian rhythms.
  • Gynaecologist Dr Mitali Rathod highlights potential hormonal effects of sleeping with lights on.
  • Wellness experts now focus on indoor lighting, including LEDs that mimic natural sunlight.
  • Simple changes like dimming screens and using warm bulbs can improve sleep quality.

For years, the focus on sleep hygiene has centred on screens, caffeine and late-night scrolling. Now, a quieter culprit is stepping into the spotlight: the humble light bulb. Whether it's a night lamp in the hallway, a glowing charger, or the faint blue light from a television on standby, indoor lighting is increasingly being linked to how well — or how poorly — we sleep.

The conversation is gaining traction across health desks and wellness circles. Reports from The Indian Express and Newsweek have highlighted how indoor lighting is becoming a new focus in the wellness conversation, with experts pointing to its role in regulating our body's internal clock. The message is clear: even dim light, when present through the night, can interfere with the quality of your rest.

The Hormonal Link

One of the more striking angles comes from gynaecologist Dr Mitali Rathod, who recently spoke about how sleeping with lights on may affect hormones. While the full details of her remarks are still emerging, the underlying science is well established. Light exposure at night suppresses melatonin, the hormone responsible for signalling to your body that it's time to sleep.

When melatonin levels drop, sleep becomes lighter and more fragmented. This isn't just about feeling tired the next day. Over time, disrupted melatonin can affect other hormonal systems, including those tied to stress and reproduction. Dr Rathod's comments have added a clinical voice to what many sleep researchers have been saying for years.

Why Even Dim Light Matters

You might think a small night light is harmless. But research suggests otherwise. The human body evolved in a world where darkness meant night. Artificial light, no matter how faint, signals to the brain that it's still daytime. This can delay the onset of deep sleep and reduce the time spent in restorative stages.

Even light from a street lamp filtering through curtains, or the glow of a phone charger, can be enough to alter your sleep architecture. The key is not just brightness but wavelength. Blue light, common in LEDs and screens, is particularly potent at suppressing melatonin.

The Rise of 'Human-Centric' Lighting

This growing awareness has sparked a shift in how we design lighting for our homes and workplaces. The London Evening Standard recently explored how LEDs that mimic sunshine could be key to wellbeing. These bulbs are designed to change colour temperature throughout the day — brighter and cooler in the morning, warmer and dimmer in the evening.

The idea is to work with our circadian rhythm rather than against it. In India, where power cuts and late-night work schedules often blur the line between day and night, such lighting could have particular relevance. However, experts caution that no bulb can replace the simple habit of switching off lights before bed.

Practical Steps for Better Sleep

If you're someone who needs a light on to fall asleep, you're not alone. But small adjustments can make a difference. Consider using a dim red or amber bulb instead of white or blue light. Red light has a longer wavelength and is less disruptive to melatonin production.

Another option is to set a timer on your lights so they switch off after you've dozed off. If you must keep a light on for safety or for a child, place it low to the ground and keep it as dim as possible. The goal is not to eliminate all light, but to reduce its impact on your body's natural rhythms.

Also, be mindful of screens. Even a few minutes of scrolling in bed can delay sleep onset. Try to keep phones away from the bed or switch them to grayscale mode at night.

What to Watch

As the wellness conversation around indoor lighting grows, expect more research and product innovation in the coming months. Indian manufacturers are already exploring affordable 'circadian-friendly' bulbs for the domestic market. But the simplest and most cost-effective solution remains a switch — literally. Turning off the lights before you sleep could be one of the easiest ways to protect your sleep, and your hormones, in the long run.
