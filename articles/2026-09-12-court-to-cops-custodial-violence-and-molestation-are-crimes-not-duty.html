Court to Cops: Custodial Violence and Molestation Are Crimes, Not Duty

✍️ By TIVRA News Editorial Team 📅 2026-09-12 📂 Crime & Law Original source ↗
Key points
  • A court refused pre-arrest bail to police officers accused of custodial violence and molestation, holding that such acts fall outside the lawful discharge of duty.
  • The ruling confirms that 'duty' is a legal shield for bona fide acts, not a blanket immunity for assault, intimidation or sexual misconduct in custody.
  • Officers accused of custodial offences face arrest, departmental action and loss of the protection that Section 197 CrPC or BNSS equivalent would otherwise offer.

Court Refuses Protection to Officers Accused of Custodial Misconduct

A court has denied relief to police officers accused of custodial violence and molestation, drawing a hard line between the lawful performance of duty and criminal conduct carried out under the colour of uniform. The bench recorded that assault, intimidation and molestation inside a lock-up or during interrogation are crimes in their own right, and cannot be dressed up as part of official responsibility.

The order arose from a plea for anticipatory bail by officers facing allegations of beating and sexually inappropriate conduct against a person in their custody. Rejecting the plea, the court held that custodial settings create a power imbalance that the law treats with heightened suspicion, not reflexive protection.

Why the 'Official Duty' Defence Failed

Police officers in India frequently seek shelter under statutory provisions that require prior sanction before prosecution for acts done in the discharge of official duty. The logic is straightforward: officers must be able to enforce the law without fearing personal prosecution for every lawful arrest or use of reasonable force.

The court's reasoning narrows that shield considerably. A duty shield covers acts that have a reasonable connection to lawful duty. Beating a handcuffed suspect, molesting a woman in custody, or extracting a confession through violence has no such connection. These are offences under the Bharatiya Nyaya Sanhita, and the protection evaporates the moment the act turns criminal.

What This Means for Anticipatory Bail

Anticipatory bail is discretionary. Courts weigh the gravity of the offence, the risk of evidence tampering, the possibility of witness intimidation, and the applicant's ability to influence the investigation. For serving police officers, the last factor cuts sharply against them, because they retain access to colleagues, records and the complainant's whereabouts.

In this matter, the court found that granting pre-arrest bail would send a message that custodial abuse carries no immediate consequence. The denial is therefore both a factual finding and a deterrent signal.

Real-World Impact on Complainants and Officers

For a person in custody, the practical barrier has always been fear: who will believe a detainee against a uniformed officer? This ruling reinforces that courts will not treat the uniform itself as evidence of good faith. Complainants still need medical records, prompt FIR registration and, ideally, immediate legal representation.

For officers, the message is equally direct. Lawful force in a genuine arrest remains protected. Excess, humiliation or sexual misconduct does not. Departments that fail to act internally now face stronger judicial scrutiny, and individual officers risk both criminal prosecution and service penalties running in parallel.

Pros and Cons of the Ruling

  • Pro: Strengthens deterrence against custodial abuse, where conviction rates have historically been low.
  • Pro: Clarifies that the duty shield is not blanket immunity for criminal conduct.
  • Pro: Encourages departments to record interrogations and maintain lock-up logs.
  • Con: Officers may adopt defensive policing, avoiding legitimate physical intervention in volatile situations.
  • Con: Fear of false complaints can slow urgent arrests if departmental safeguards are weak.
  • Con: Without fast-track inquiries, complainants may still face long delays before justice.

The Bottom Line

The court's refusal of relief is a reminder that the phrase 'in the course of duty' describes a lawful function, not a licence. Custodial violence and molestation remain crimes, and officers accused of them must answer like any other accused person.

For victims, the route to remedy runs through immediate medical examination, a written complaint, and prompt escalation to the magistrate if the local police stall. For police leadership, the lesson is institutional: training on lawful restraint, mandatory CCTV in lock-ups, and swift departmental action protect both the public and the force's own credibility.
