Pakistan's Pre-FATF Bounty Hike: Symbolic or Substantive?

Pakistan has announced an increase in the bounty on Masood Azhar, the chief of Jaish-e-Mohammed (JeM) and a key architect of the 2008 Mumbai attacks, along with 18 other individuals listed in its Red Book. The move comes just days before the Financial Action Task Force (FATF) regional review, raising immediate questions about its intent: is this a genuine step toward counterterrorism or a tactical manoeuvre to placate global watchdogs?

The Red Book, Pakistan's internal list of most-wanted terrorists, now carries higher reward amounts for Azhar and his associates. While Pakistani authorities claim this reflects their commitment to eradicating terror, sceptics point to a pattern of symbolic actions timed with international scrutiny. For India, which has consistently demanded action against cross-border terror, the hike is unlikely to erase decades of inaction.

Masood Azhar and the 2008 Mumbai Attacks: A Case of Delayed Justice

Masood Azhar's role in the 2008 Mumbai attacks, which killed 166 people including 18 security personnel, is well-documented. The attacks, carried out by 10 LeT terrorists, were planned and executed with support from Pakistan-based handlers. Azhar's JeM, though not directly involved in the Mumbai strikes, has been responsible for numerous other attacks in India, including the 2001 Parliament attack and the 2019 Pulwama bombing.

The 19 individuals in the Red Book linked to the Mumbai attacks include senior LeT operatives such as Hafiz Saeed, Zaki-ur-Rehman Lakhvi, and Sajid Mir. Many of them have been designated as global terrorists by the UN, yet they continue to operate freely in Pakistan. The bounty hike, therefore, is a reminder of the unfinished business of justice.

Why the Timing Matters: FATF Review and International Pressure

The FATF, which placed Pakistan on its grey list in 2018 for failing to curb terror financing, removed it in 2022 after Pakistan implemented some reforms. However, the upcoming regional review will assess Pakistan's progress on action against UN-designated terrorists. By hiking bounties just before the review, Pakistan appears to be signalling compliance without taking irreversible steps like arresting or extraditing the wanted men.

Experts note that Pakistan has repeatedly used such tactics to buy time. In 2019, ahead of an FATF deadline, Pakistan froze accounts of some terror groups but later allowed them to operate under new names. The bounty hike follows a similar playbook: visible enough to claim action, but hollow in enforcement.

Buyer's Guide: What the Bounty Hike Means for Stakeholders

For investors and businesses eyeing Pakistan, the country's continued tolerance of terror proxies poses significant reputational and financial risks. FATF grey-listing had cost Pakistan an estimated $38 billion in lost GDP, according to government figures. A return to the grey list would further deter foreign investment, raise borrowing costs, and complicate banking transactions.

For India, the bounty hike is a test of Pakistan's sincerity. New Delhi has long maintained that talks and terror cannot go together. The onus is on Pakistan to demonstrate action beyond paper notifications. For global observers, the key metric will be whether the bounty leads to actual arrests or remains a cosmetic exercise.

Pros and Cons of Pakistan's Bounty Hike

  • Pros:
    • Signals intent to act against terror financing, potentially easing FATF pressure.
    • Raises public awareness about wanted terrorists.
    • Could lead to intelligence sharing if enforced.
  • Cons:
    • Timing suggests it's a cosmetic move to satisfy FATF, not a genuine shift.
    • No mechanism to ensure arrests; bounties have been declared before without effect.
    • Undermines India's faith in Pakistan's commitments, hurting bilateral ties.

Expert Take: Enforcement Gap Remains Critical

Security analysts point out that Pakistan's bounty system has historically been ineffective. Hafiz Saeed, with a USD 10 million US bounty, lives openly in Lahore. The new hike, therefore, is unlikely to change ground realities unless Pakistan demonstrates political will to dismantle terror infrastructure. For India, the focus must remain on diplomatic pressure and global isolation of terror sponsors.

As the FATF review unfolds, the world will watch whether Pakistan's actions match its words. The bounty hike is a small step, but without arrests, it remains a footnote in the long struggle for justice.
