NASA reveals near-Earth asteroid 1998 SH2 is actually an active comet

📅 2026-07-18 📂 Science Original source ↗
Key points
  • Asteroid 1998 SH2, long considered a near-Earth asteroid, is actually an active comet.
  • NASA study detected non-gravitational acceleration, indicative of cometary outgassing.
  • The object has been observed for decades, but its true nature remained unknown until now.

For decades, astronomers classified a small object near Earth as just another asteroid. Now, NASA has revealed that the object, designated 1998 SH2, is actually a comet. The finding, published in the journal Nature, ends years of speculation about its true identity.

The discovery came after researchers detected non-gravitational acceleration in the object's orbit. This subtle change in speed and direction cannot be explained by gravity alone. It points to cometary activity — the release of gas and dust from a icy nucleus.

What non-gravitational acceleration reveals

Non-gravitational acceleration is a hallmark of comets. As a comet approaches the Sun, its ice turns into gas, creating jets that push the object slightly off its predicted path. Asteroids, being rocky, do not behave this way.

NASA scientists analyzed decades of observational data for 1998 SH2. They found that its motion deviated from a purely gravity-driven trajectory. The deviation was small but consistent with outgassing.

“This object has been known for a long time, but we never had clear evidence of cometary activity before,” said a NASA researcher involved in the study. The study used data from ground-based telescopes and space-based observatories.

Why the confusion lasted so long

The object was first spotted in 1998 and classified as a near-Earth asteroid. Its orbit brings it relatively close to Earth, making it a target for routine monitoring. However, its appearance was deceptive.

Unlike classic comets that develop bright tails visible from Earth, 1998 SH2 remained faint. Its outgassing was weak, making it hard to detect. Only precise measurements of its orbit over many years revealed the truth.

This is not the first time an asteroid has been reclassified as a comet. But each case helps scientists understand the continuum between these two types of objects. Some comets become dormant, looking like asteroids until they get a fresh burst of heat.

Implications for planetary defense and science

The reclassification has practical implications. Near-Earth objects are tracked for potential impact hazards. If an object behaves like a comet, its trajectory is harder to predict because outgassing can push it unpredictably.

“Cometary activity introduces uncertainty in orbit calculations,” the NASA researcher noted. “This matters for assessing any future risk.”

The finding also adds to the growing evidence that many near-Earth objects are not simple rocks. Some are extinct comets or contain ice beneath a dusty surface. Missions like NASA's OSIRIS-REx and Japan's Hayabusa2 have brought back samples from such bodies.

What comes next for 1998 SH2

Scientists plan to observe the object more closely during its next close approach. The study's authors hope to detect direct signs of gas or dust escaping from the nucleus.

For now, 1998 SH2 is officially reclassified as a comet. It will get a new designation with a ‘C/’ prefix, marking its place among the solar system's icy wanderers. The mystery that lasted decades has finally been resolved.
