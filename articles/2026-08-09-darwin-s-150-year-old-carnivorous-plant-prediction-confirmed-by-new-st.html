Darwin's 150-year-old carnivorous plant prediction confirmed by new study

📅 2026-08-09 📂 Science Original source ↗
Key points
  • Study provides multidisciplinary evidence for carnivory in Saxifraga
  • Confirms Charles Darwin's 1875 prediction about two plant species
  • Alpine flower uses sticky leaves to trap and digest insects

A 150-year-old botanical mystery solved

In 1875, Charles Darwin made a bold suggestion that two plant species might be carnivorous. For over a century, botanists debated whether the alpine flower Saxifraga truly belonged in this predatory category. Now, a new multidisciplinary study has confirmed what Darwin suspected.

Researchers have identified clear evidence of carnivory in Saxifraga, a flowering plant found in rocky, mountainous regions. The findings, published in the journal Nature, settle a question that has lingered since the Victorian era.

Sticky leaves that trap and digest

The plant's leaves are covered in glandular hairs that secrete a sticky substance. Small insects that land on the surface become trapped, much like on a flypaper trap. Once ensnared, the plant releases digestive enzymes that break down the prey and absorb the nutrients.

This mechanism is subtle, unlike the dramatic snap of a Venus flytrap or the pitfall traps of pitcher plants. Saxifraga's approach is quieter, but equally effective for survival in nutrient-poor alpine soils.

Evidence across multiple disciplines

The study's strength lies in its multidisciplinary approach. Researchers combined field observations, anatomical studies, and biochemical analysis to build a compelling case. They documented the presence of digestive enzymes, observed nutrient uptake from captured insects, and traced the flow of nitrogen from prey into plant tissue.

This convergence of evidence leaves little room for doubt. Saxifraga is not merely a plant that happens to catch insects—it actively benefits from them.

Why Darwin's prediction matters

Darwin's original suggestion was based on careful observation of the plant's sticky morphology. But without modern tools, he could not prove the digestive process. His intuition, however, was remarkably prescient.

The confirmation adds Saxifraga to a growing list of carnivorous plants, which now includes over 600 species worldwide. Most are found in habitats where nitrogen is scarce, making insect capture an evolutionary advantage.

Scientists note that carnivory in plants may be more common than previously assumed. Many species with sticky surfaces could be engaging in this behaviour, unnoticed until now.

A quiet predator in plain sight

The alpine flower has been growing in plain sight for millennia, its carnivorous nature hidden in plain view. Gardeners and hikers may have unknowingly brushed past this killer, mistaking it for an ordinary wildflower.

The study serves as a reminder that nature's complexity often exceeds human assumptions. What appears passive may be actively hunting.

Researchers are now planning to examine other sticky-leaved plants for similar adaptations. The discovery could open new avenues in understanding plant evolution and nutrient acquisition strategies.

Darwin's legacy as a keen observer of natural phenomena continues to yield insights long after his time. His 1875 suggestion, once considered speculative, now stands as a testament to scientific foresight.

The next steps will involve studying Saxifraga's ecological role and whether climate change affects its insect-trapping efficiency. As alpine habitats shift, this plant's survival strategy may face new challenges.
