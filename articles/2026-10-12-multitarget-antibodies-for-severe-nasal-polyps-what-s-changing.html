Multitarget Antibodies for Severe Nasal Polyps: What’s Changing

  • Evidence gaps remain around long-term outcomes, patient selection, and durability of response versus standard biologics and surgery.

Why severe nasal polyps are hard to treat

Severe nasal polyps are more than a “blocked nose.” They reflect chronic inflammation that can persist despite intranasal steroids and saline care, and in many patients, oral steroids are needed intermittently—often with unacceptable side effects. When polyps recur quickly, quality of life declines through persistent congestion, smell loss, facial pressure, and disrupted sleep.

Clinicians typically escalate therapy step-by-step: topical corticosteroids, short courses of oral steroids, targeted management of underlying conditions such as asthma, and—when disease is refractory—endoscopic sinus surgery plus ongoing medical treatment. In the last few years, biologics have changed the outlook for selected patients, but access, cost, and variable response rates still leave a subset of people with ongoing symptoms.

That clinical reality is the backdrop to the EMJ reporting on multitarget antibodies for severe nasal polyps, which represent a strategy to hit inflammation from more than one angle rather than relying on a single molecular pathway.

Multitarget antibodies: the “two-pronged” inflammation strategy

Single-target biologics focus on one cytokine or receptor axis known to drive type 2 inflammation. Multitarget antibodies, by contrast, are designed to neutralise more than one relevant signal—potentially addressing the fact that nasal polyp disease is not uniform. Some patients show stronger involvement of specific inflammatory mediators, while others have a mixed pattern.

For buyers of health outcomes—patients and their families—the key question is not just whether a drug “works,” but whether it reduces the cycle of flares, repeat steroid bursts, and revision surgeries. Multitarget approaches are expected to be most valuable if they (a) deliver more consistent symptom improvement across inflammatory profiles and (b) extend the time to relapse.

However, the most important practical point is that multitarget does not automatically mean “better for everyone.” Response depends on underlying disease biology, comorbid asthma or aspirin-exacerbated respiratory disease, baseline symptom burden, and prior treatment history.

What to look for in real-world effectiveness

When clinicians evaluate new options for severe nasal polyps, they rely on measures that reflect daily life: nasal congestion severity, nasal endoscopy scores, need for systemic steroids, and—critically—smell function recovery. A therapy that improves smell and reduces steroid reliance often changes long-term burden more than modest average changes in a single composite score.

From a patient’s perspective, “durability” matters as much as initial response. The practical benchmarks to track during follow-up include: time until symptoms return, frequency of rescue oral steroid courses, and whether repeat procedures can be avoided or delayed.

Safety monitoring also shapes real-world value. Even when adverse events are not frequent, the pattern of monitoring—blood tests, infection surveillance, and review of comorbid respiratory conditions—determines how feasible a treatment is for day-to-day life.

  • Potential benefit: More complete blockade of inflammatory pathways may improve consistency of response in mixed disease.
  • Potential benefit: Reduced need for oral steroids can lower cumulative side effects.
  • Trade-off: Long-term durability and safety profile may require more post-market observation than early trial signals.
  • Adherence factor: Administration schedule (and clinic access) can determine whether the treatment fits a patient’s routine.
  • Selection risk: Patients without relevant inflammatory drivers may see less benefit and should be guided accordingly.

How this changes clinical decision-making in India

In many Indian settings, severe nasal polyps are managed through a combination of ENT care and respiratory medicine, especially when asthma is present. The adoption of multitarget antibodies would likely add another decision layer: identifying who is most likely to benefit and ensuring consistent follow-up to measure response objectively.

Clinicians may also use biomarkers or clinical phenotyping to refine eligibility, such as evidence of type 2 inflammation, endoscopic severity, and history of recurrent disease despite maximal inhaled and topical therapies. For patients, the value is in receiving a treatment plan that is measurable rather than vague—clear endpoints, scheduled assessments, and a contingency plan if response is incomplete.

Because EMJ’s report focuses on exploration rather than a single approved pathway, Indian buyers—patients and payers—should treat this as an “evidence-evolution” moment. The strongest buying signals will come from published outcome data on symptom scores, smell measures, surgery avoidance, and relapse timing.

Questions patients should ask before starting (or switching) an antibody therapy

1) What is the goal for me: symptom relief, smell improvement, reducing steroid bursts, or surgery prevention?

2) How will response be measured—endoscopy score, patient-reported congestion, smell tests, or all of these?

3) What is the expected time to see change, and what would count as an inadequate response?

4) What safety monitoring is needed, and how often will follow-ups happen?

5) If I don’t respond well, what is the next step—adjusting background therapy, considering surgery, or switching biologic strategy?

What the data should include next

To make multitarget antibodies a dependable option, future reporting should clearly separate early response from long-term control. Buyers of healthcare value should look for evidence that the treatment maintains effect over time, reduces relapses, and meaningfully improves quality-of-life outcomes.

Below is a checklist of data types that matter for decision-making without locking the reader into unverified claims. It also helps patients compare emerging options once more evidence becomes available.

Key takeaways for patients and clinicians

Multitarget antibodies represent a promising direction because severe nasal polyps often reflect more than one inflammatory driver. The practical advantage should be judged by steroid reduction, durability of symptom control, and improved smell—outcomes that map directly to daily functioning.

For now, EMJ’s coverage is best read as a signal to watch emerging evidence carefully. The strongest decisions will come when studies clarify who benefits most, how long benefits last, and what monitoring and safety look like in routine care.
