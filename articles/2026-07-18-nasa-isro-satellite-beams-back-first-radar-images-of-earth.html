NASA-ISRO Satellite Beams Back First Radar Images of Earth

📅 2026-07-18 📂 Space Original source ↗
Key points
  • NISAR satellite captured first radar images of Earth's surface.
  • Images reveal detailed topography and vegetation structure.
  • Mission is a joint project between NASA and ISRO.
  • Data will help monitor changes in land and ice surfaces.

The NASA-ISRO Synthetic Aperture Radar (NISAR) satellite has transmitted its first radar images of Earth's surface. The milestone was announced by the U.S. Embassy in New Delhi, marking a significant step for the joint mission between the American and Indian space agencies.

NISAR, which launched earlier this year, is designed to observe Earth's land and ice-covered surfaces with unprecedented clarity. The initial images show detailed terrain features, including mountains, forests, and agricultural fields.

Images Reveal Surface Details

The first set of images was captured over parts of North America and Asia. Radar data from the satellite can penetrate clouds and darkness, allowing it to see through weather conditions that would block optical sensors.

Scientists at NASA's Jet Propulsion Laboratory and ISRO's Space Applications Centre are studying the images. They have confirmed that the radar is functioning as expected, with resolution sharp enough to detect changes in vegetation and surface structures.

How NISAR's Radar Works

The satellite carries two radar systems: an L-band radar from NASA and an S-band radar from ISRO. Together, they can measure shifts in the Earth's surface as small as a few centimetres.

This capability allows researchers to track movements in glaciers, monitor deforestation, and detect ground deformation caused by earthquakes or volcanic activity. The satellite orbits Earth every 12 days, providing repeated coverage of the same areas.

Joint Mission Milestone

NISAR is one of the most complex Earth-observation missions ever attempted. It is a partnership between NASA and ISRO, with each agency contributing key components and expertise.

The U.S. Embassy statement called the image transmission a “major milestone” for the project. Officials said the data will be made available to scientists worldwide once the satellite's calibration phase is complete.

The mission is expected to operate for at least three years, though its fuel could allow it to function longer. Indian and American engineers are currently running tests to fine-tune the instruments.

The satellite's radar also has applications in agriculture. It can estimate soil moisture levels and monitor crop health, which could help farmers in India and other countries manage water resources more effectively.

NISAR's ability to map ice sheets and glaciers will be critical for climate research. The data will help scientists calculate how much ice is being lost each year and how that contributes to sea-level rise.

The next phase of the mission involves expanding the coverage area and releasing the first global radar maps. Those are expected by early next year.
