BRICS Summit 2026: Why the New Delhi Declaration Matters to Indian Buyers and Businesses

The 18th BRICS Summit in New Delhi closed with the New Delhi Declaration, a consensus document that will shape trade, technology and commodity flows across the bloc for years. For Indian consumers and businesses, the summit's outcomes are not abstract diplomacy — they will influence EV battery prices, semiconductor availability, cross-border payment costs and export access.

Hosting the summit gave India unusual leverage. Prime Minister Narendra Modi used the platform to flag two risks that directly affect Indian industry: the weaponisation of critical minerals and the weaponisation of technology. With Xi Jinping in the hall, that message carried weight.

Key Takeaways From the 18th BRICS Summit

1. The New Delhi Declaration: Consensus With Caveats

The declaration reaffirmed commitments to multilateralism, supply chain resilience and reform of global financial institutions. Consensus among an expanded membership — now spanning major oil producers, manufacturing hubs and resource-rich economies — is itself the achievement. The practical value lies in working groups on payments, minerals and technology transfer that will report before the next summit.

2. Modi's Critical Minerals Warning

Modi warned against the weaponisation of critical minerals in Xi Jinping's presence. India imports the bulk of its lithium, cobalt and rare earth requirements, and domestic EV and electronics manufacturing depends on stable supply. Any export restriction by a dominant producer — China controls a majority of rare earth refining — raises input costs for Indian battery makers and, eventually, vehicle prices.

3. Technology Weaponisation and the Semiconductor Question

The summit flagged export controls and tech denial as systemic risks. For India's semiconductor mission, which is drawing investments in Gujarat and Assam, diversified sourcing and technology partnerships are now a strategic necessity, not a preference. Buyers of electronics should expect policy support for domestic fabrication to remain a priority.

4. India's Diplomatic Sprint: 15 Bilaterals in 3 Days

Modi held 15 bilateral meetings in three days, a pace that signals India's intent to convert BRICS membership into bilateral commercial gains — energy deals, defence cooperation, digital payments linkages and market access. For exporters, these conversations often precede tariff and standards agreements.

Real-World Implications for Buyers and Investors

  • EV and battery buyers: Critical mineral supply security remains the single biggest cost variable. Domestic refining and recycling capacity will determine how quickly battery pack prices fall.
  • Electronics consumers: Semiconductor sourcing diversification supports long-term price stability, but near-term global demand keeps component costs firm.
  • Exporters: Bilateral momentum can translate into easier market access, but tariff and standards negotiations take quarters, not weeks.
  • Investors: Mining, refining, payments infrastructure and fab-linked logistics are the sectors most directly touched by the declaration's working groups.

Pros and Cons of the Summit Outcome

  • Pro: Consensus declaration keeps the bloc cohesive despite internal differences.
  • Pro: India's hosting role strengthens its bridge position between the Global South and Western economies.
  • Con: Declarations lack enforcement mechanisms; delivery depends on national policy.
  • Con: Critical mineral concentration remains unresolved, leaving import-dependent economies exposed.

What to Watch Next

Track the working group outputs on local currency settlement, mineral supply pacts and technology transfer. For Indian buyers, the clearest near-term signals will come from battery pricing, semiconductor investment announcements and any new bilateral trade facilitation. The New Delhi Declaration sets direction; the follow-through decides value.
