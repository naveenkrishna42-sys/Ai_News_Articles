Lung cancer accounts for 6% of Karnataka's cancer cases; tobacco top risk

📅 2026-07-31 📂 Health Original source ↗
Key points
  • Lung cancer constitutes 6% of all cancer cases in Karnataka
  • Tobacco use remains the single biggest risk factor
  • Rising detection among non-smokers and younger individuals
  • Preventive habits and early screening highlighted by pulmonologists

Lung cancer now accounts for 6% of all cancer cases reported in Karnataka, according to recent data. While the numbers may seem modest, oncologists and public health experts point to a worrying trend: the disease is no longer confined to long-term smokers. A growing number of non-tobacco users and younger patients are being diagnosed, raising fresh questions about air pollution, genetic susceptibility, and lifestyle triggers.

Tobacco still the dominant cause

Despite the shifting demographics, tobacco remains the single largest preventable risk factor for lung cancer in the state. Health officials reiterate that smoking—whether cigarettes, beedis, or chewing tobacco—contributes to the majority of cases. The message is stark: quitting tobacco, or never starting, dramatically lowers the risk.

Karnataka's public health machinery has long run anti-tobacco campaigns, but their impact remains uneven. Rural districts, where tobacco use is deeply entrenched, continue to report high incidence rates. Urban centres, meanwhile, are seeing a different pattern emerge—lung cancer in people who have never smoked.

Why non-smokers and the young are at risk

Pulmonologists across India have noted a steady rise in lung cancer among non-smokers, particularly women. Exposure to second-hand smoke, indoor air pollution from cooking fuels, and outdoor particulate matter are suspected contributors. The young are not spared either; doctors report cases in their 30s and 40s, often detected at advanced stages.

Dr Manish Kumar Aggarwal, a pulmonologist, stresses that lung cancer is not a smoker's disease alone. He advocates five preventive habits: avoiding tobacco in any form, ensuring adequate ventilation at home, wearing masks in high-pollution areas, maintaining indoor plants, and undergoing low-dose CT screening for high-risk groups. Early detection, he argues, is the difference between survival and mortality.

Screening and global lessons

Countries like Taiwan and the UK have shown that early screening works. Taiwan introduced low-dose CT for high-risk populations, including non-smokers with family histories, leading to earlier diagnoses and better outcomes. The UK has piloted targeted lung health checks in deprived areas, where smoking rates and lung cancer mortality are highest.

India, and Karnataka specifically, has yet to adopt such population-level screening. Experts say the cost of CT scans and the lack of awareness among primary-care physicians remain hurdles. But the evidence from these countries suggests that a similar approach could catch more cases at treatable stages.

The road ahead

As the world observes World Lung Cancer Day, the focus in Karnataka must shift from treatment to prevention and early detection. Public health campaigns need to address not just tobacco, but also air quality and occupational hazards. For individuals, the takeaway is simple: recognise the symptoms—persistent cough, chest pain, breathlessness—and seek medical advice without delay.

What remains to be seen is whether Karnataka's health system will invest in screening infrastructure and public awareness at the scale required. The state's cancer burden is not just a statistic; it is a call to action.
