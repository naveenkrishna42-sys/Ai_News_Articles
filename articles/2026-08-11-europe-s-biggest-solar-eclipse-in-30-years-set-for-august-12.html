Europe's biggest solar eclipse in 30 years set for August 12

📅 2026-08-11 📂 Science Original source ↗
Key points
  • Total solar eclipse on August 12, 2026, is the first in Europe since 1999.
  • Path of totality crosses northern Spain and Iceland; partial eclipse visible across the continent.
  • Weather and safety precautions are key for millions of expected viewers.

Europe is bracing for its most significant celestial event in nearly three decades as a total solar eclipse is set to sweep across the continent on August 12, 2026. The eclipse, which occurs just a day after this report, marks the first time since 1999 that a total solar eclipse will be visible from European soil.

Astronomers and skywatchers have been tracking the event for months, with live updates and tracking tools now pointing to exact times for locations across the continent. The path of totality—where the moon completely blocks the sun—will cross parts of northern Spain and Iceland, offering prime viewing for those in the right spots.

Where the eclipse will be visible

The total phase of the eclipse will be visible along a narrow corridor stretching from Iceland across the Atlantic and into northern Spain. Major Spanish cities such as A Coruña, Bilbao, and Zaragoza fall within or near the path, while Madrid will experience a deep partial eclipse.

Elsewhere in Europe, the partial eclipse will be visible across most of the continent, with the sun appearing as a crescent from cities like London, Paris, Berlin, and Rome. The degree of coverage will vary, with northern regions seeing a more dramatic effect than southern ones.

For those outside the totality zone, even a 90 percent partial eclipse will not compare to the full spectacle, where day turns to twilight and the corona becomes visible to the naked eye.

Timing and tracking

Eclipse trackers published by multiple outlets, including Al Jazeera and Space, provide location-specific times for the event. In Spain, the eclipse will begin in the late morning and reach maximum coverage around midday, while in Iceland, the timing will shift slightly later.

Viewers are advised to check local timings well in advance, as even a few minutes' difference can mean missing the peak. The entire event, from first contact to last, will last roughly two to three hours depending on location, but totality itself will be brief—under two minutes in most spots.

Weather remains the biggest unknown. Cloud cover could spoil the view for many, and forecasters are closely monitoring conditions across the path. Spain's typically clear summer skies offer better odds, but Iceland's maritime climate is less predictable.

Safety first

Officials and astronomers have stressed that looking directly at the sun, even during a partial eclipse, can cause permanent eye damage. Specialised eclipse glasses or solar filters are essential for any direct viewing.

During the brief moments of totality, when the sun is fully covered, it is safe to view with the naked eye—but only for those within the narrow path. For everyone else, the partial phases require constant protection.

Local authorities in Spain have prepared for an influx of visitors, with some regions declaring the event a major tourism draw. Hotels in the path of totality have been booked for months, and many towns are planning viewing events and festivals around the eclipse.

Why this eclipse matters

The last total solar eclipse visible from Europe was in August 1999, which crossed from the UK through France, Germany, and into Turkey. That event drew massive crowds and remains a vivid memory for many Europeans.

Since then, total eclipses have occurred elsewhere, but none have been so accessible to European populations. The 2026 event is being called the biggest in 30 years because of the sheer number of people who live within driving distance of the path.

After this, the next total solar eclipse in Europe won't occur until 2027, when one will cross parts of Spain again—but that will be a more limited event. For many, this August 12 will be a once-in-a-lifetime chance to witness totality without travelling far from home.

As the clock ticks down to totality, all eyes are on the skies. Whether the weather cooperates or not, the event will unfold as scheduled, and millions across the continent will look up—safely, one hopes—to witness a rare moment of cosmic alignment.

What happens next depends on the clouds, but one thing is certain: the next few hours will determine whether this becomes a cherished memory or a missed opportunity for millions of Europeans.
