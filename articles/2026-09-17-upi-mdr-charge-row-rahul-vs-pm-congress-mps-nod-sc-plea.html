UPI MDR Controversy: Political Slugfest Erupts Over Charges on Digital Payments

A political firestorm has erupted over the government's decision to introduce Merchant Discount Rate (MDR) on Unified Payments Interface (UPI) transactions above ₹2,000. Congress leader Rahul Gandhi on Wednesday accused Prime Minister Narendra Modi of "prostrating before Trump," alleging that the move was dictated by American pressure. The BJP hit back, citing that Congress MPs on the Parliamentary Standing Committee on Finance had themselves recommended the charge. Meanwhile, a plea in the Supreme Court challenges the Centre's decision, adding a legal dimension to the escalating row.

At the heart of the dispute is the future of India's digital payments ecosystem, which has so far been free for merchants and customers alike. The introduction of MDR—a fee paid by merchants to banks for processing digital transactions—marks a significant policy shift. While the government argues it is necessary to sustain the UPI infrastructure, critics contend it will burden small businesses and discourage digital adoption.

What Exactly Is MDR and Why Does It Matter?

MDR is a fee charged by acquiring banks to merchants for accepting digital payments. Until now, UPI transactions were exempt from MDR to promote digital adoption. The new rule proposes a tiered structure: transactions up to ₹2,000 remain free, while those above ₹2,000 will attract MDR, likely ranging from 0.1% to 0.3% of the transaction value. For a ₹5,000 transaction, the merchant might pay ₹5 to ₹15 as MDR.

For small merchants, especially those with thin margins, this could translate into a significant cost. For banks and payment aggregators, however, it provides a revenue stream to maintain and upgrade the UPI system, which processes over 10 billion transactions a month. The government argues that a sustainable model is essential to prevent systemic risks.

Political Slugfest: Who Said What

Rahul Gandhi's remark that the PM "decided to prostrate before Trump" references a perceived influence of former US President Donald Trump, though the connection remains unclear. The BJP countered by pointing out that the Standing Committee on Finance, which includes Congress MPs, had recommended MDR in its report. Sources indicate that five Congress MPs on the panel did not dissent when the recommendation was made.

This political blame game obscures the technical nuances. The committee's recommendation was based on representations from banks and payment stakeholders who argued that the zero-MDR regime was unsustainable. However, the timing and communication of the decision have sparked allegations of a backdoor deal.

Legal Challenge in Supreme Court

A plea filed in the Supreme Court challenges the Centre's decision to levy MDR on merchant UPI transactions over ₹2,000. The petitioner argues that the move violates the principles of digital inclusion and could disproportionately affect small businesses. The court's intervention could delay or alter the implementation, adding uncertainty for stakeholders.

Legal experts note that the case will hinge on whether the government followed due process and whether the MDR framework is arbitrary. The outcome could set a precedent for future digital payment regulations.

Impact on Merchants and Digital Payments Ecosystem

For merchants, the new MDR means higher costs for high-value transactions. Small retailers, already reeling from the pandemic, may pass on the cost to consumers or revert to cash. This could slow the digital payments momentum that India has built over the past decade.

For the broader ecosystem, MDR could attract more private investment in UPI infrastructure, improving reliability and innovation. However, the risk of reduced adoption among price-sensitive users remains.

Pros and Cons of MDR on UPI

  • Pros:
    • Provides revenue for banks to maintain UPI infrastructure.
    • Encourages investment in digital payment security and innovation.
    • Aligns UPI with global payment systems that charge MDR.
  • Cons:
    • Increases cost for merchants, especially small businesses.
    • May deter high-value digital transactions, pushing users back to cash.
    • Political controversy could undermine trust in digital payments.

Buyer Implications: What Merchants Should Know

Merchants must evaluate whether to absorb the MDR or pass it on to customers. For high-ticket items, the cost could be significant. Negotiating with payment aggregators for lower rates might be possible. Additionally, merchants should monitor the Supreme Court case, as a stay could postpone implementation.

Consumers might not see direct charges, but could face higher prices if merchants pass on the cost. It is crucial to stay informed and adapt to the changing digital payments landscape.
