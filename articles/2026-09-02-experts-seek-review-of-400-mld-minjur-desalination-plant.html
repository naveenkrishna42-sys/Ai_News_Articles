Experts Seek Review of 400 MLD Minjur Desalination Plant

📅 2026-09-02 📂 Chennai Original source ↗
Key points
  • Environmental experts call for comprehensive review of proposed 400 mld desalination plant at Minjur
  • Plant raises concerns over brine disposal, energy consumption, and impact on coastal marine ecosystem
  • Alternatives like rainwater harvesting and wastewater recycling offer more sustainable solutions, experts argue

Chennai's Desalination Dilemma

Chennai, India's fourth-largest city, faces an acute water crisis that has worsened over the past decade. With groundwater tables plummeting and traditional reservoirs failing to meet demand, the Tamil Nadu government has proposed a 400 million liters per day (MLD) desalination plant at Minjur. The ambitious project aims to supplement the city's water supply, but experts are urging a thorough review before moving forward.

The existing Minjur desalination plant, operational since 2010, currently produces 100 MLD. The proposed expansion would increase this fivefold, raising fundamental questions about environmental sustainability, cost-effectiveness, and long-term viability in an era of climate uncertainty.

Why Experts Are Sounding the Alarm

A coalition of environmental scientists, water resource engineers, and marine biologists have submitted representations demanding an independent environmental impact assessment. Their primary concerns center on brine disposal—a byproduct of desalination that contains concentrated salts and chemical residues.

"Coastal ecosystems in the Gulf of Mannar are already under stress," noted one marine ecology expert familiar with the submissions. "Disposing of brine from a plant this size requires careful scientific evaluation, not assumptions."

The energy footprint presents another challenge. Desalination plants are notoriously power-hungry, and adding 400 MLD of capacity would significantly increase the city's carbon emissions at a time when India is committed to reducing its environmental impact.

Cost Versus Alternatives: The Critical Debate

The financial implications are substantial. Desalinated water costs anywhere between ₹40-70 per kiloliter, compared to ₹10-20 for conventional treated water. For a city where nearly 40% of residents live in informal settlements, such costs could make the water unaffordable for those who need it most.

Critics argue that the same investment in rainwater harvesting infrastructure, wastewater recycling plants, and lake restoration could yield better results at a fraction of the cost. Chennai receives an average annual rainfall of 1,400 mm—enough to meet the city's needs if captured and stored effectively.

Key Facts: Desalination Debate

  • Proponents argue: Desalination provides a drought-proof water source independent of rainfall patterns and monsoon variability.
  • Critics counter: High operational costs and environmental concerns make it a last resort, not a primary solution.
  • Current demand: Chennai requires approximately 1,500 MLD; current supply falls short by nearly 300 MLD during summer months.
  • Expert consensus: Desalination should complement, not replace, traditional water management strategies.

The Path Forward

The Tamil Nadu Water Supply and Drainage Board has indicated willingness to engage with expert recommendations. A formal review process, if initiated, would involve public consultations, independent scientific assessment, and comparison with alternative water security strategies.

What remains clear is that Chennai cannot afford to sidestep this debate. The city's water future depends on solutions that are both environmentally responsible and economically accessible to all its residents. Whether desalination at this scale serves those goals—or merely provides a superficially convenient answer—demands rigorous scrutiny before public funds are committed.
