Satellite images show SpaceX rocket crash site on Moon

📅 2026-08-07 📂 Science Original source ↗
Key points
  • South Korea's space agency released before-and-after satellite images of the Moon showing a new crater.
  • The crater was caused by the impact of a SpaceX rocket stage that crashed into the lunar surface.
  • Scientists say the impact offers new insights into the Moon's subsurface structure.

Lunar impact captured from orbit

Satellite imagery released by South Korea's space agency has provided a rare before-and-after view of the site where a SpaceX rocket stage crashed into the Moon. The images, shared this week, show a fresh crater on the lunar surface, confirming the impact and giving scientists a new tool to study the Moon's makeup.

The spacecraft, a Falcon 9 upper stage, had been drifting in space for years before its trajectory brought it onto a collision course with the Moon. Unlike typical controlled landings, this was an unplanned crash, making the resulting crater a natural experiment for researchers.

What the images reveal

The South Korean agency's photographs, taken from lunar orbit, highlight a distinct depression that was not present in earlier surveys of the same region. The crater's size and shape, scientists say, can help estimate the density and composition of the upper layer of the lunar regolith.

By comparing the new images with older data, researchers can infer how the Moon's surface responds to a high-speed impact. This information is valuable for planning future missions, both robotic and crewed, that may need to land in similar terrain.

NASA joins the observation effort

NASA has also announced plans to observe the impact site using its own lunar orbiters. The agency intends to gather additional data on the crater's morphology and the debris field surrounding it, which could reveal details about the rocket stage's final moments.

Officials have not yet confirmed the exact coordinates of the crash site, but the South Korean imagery provides a clear reference point for follow-up studies. The collaboration between space agencies underscores the growing interest in monitoring human-made objects on the Moon.

Scientific value of an accidental crash

Though the crash was unintentional, it offers a unique opportunity to study the Moon's subsurface without the cost of a dedicated mission. The impact excavated material that would otherwise remain buried, giving scientists a glimpse of what lies beneath the visible surface.

Early analyses suggest the crater is larger than expected, which could indicate a softer upper layer or a higher impact velocity than initially calculated. Detailed studies are underway, with findings expected to be published in the coming months.

This is not the first time a rocket stage has hit the Moon, but it is one of the best-documented cases. The availability of before-and-after satellite images marks a step forward in how such events are recorded and studied.

What happens next

Scientists will continue to analyze the crater's features and compare them with models of lunar impacts. The data could refine predictions for future accidental collisions and aid in designing more resilient landers.

As more space debris accumulates in Earth's vicinity, the Moon is becoming an unintended archive of human activity. This latest crash adds a new chapter, one that researchers are keen to read.
