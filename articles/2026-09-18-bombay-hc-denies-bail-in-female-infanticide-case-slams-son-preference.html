Bombay HC Refuses Bail, Calls Out Son Preference

The Bombay High Court has refused bail to a man accused of killing his own granddaughter, using the order to sharply criticise the persistence of son preference in Indian society. The bench described it as unfortunate that families continue to value male children over girls, an attitude that in this case allegedly ended in the death of an infant.

The court treated the gravity of the alleged offence as a decisive factor against granting bail. Where a child's life is taken within the family itself, the bench reasoned, the plea for liberty must yield to the seriousness of the accusation and the message that such crimes carry no leniency.

What the Court Actually Ruled

The order is a bail decision, not a final conviction. The accused remains presumed innocent until trial concludes. Yet the refusal of bail signals the court's assessment that the allegations are serious enough to justify continued custody during the investigation and trial.

Judges weigh flight risk, witness tampering, and the nature of the offence when deciding bail. A killing inside the household complicates all three, since witnesses are often family members and the victim cannot speak for herself.

Why Son Preference Is a Legal, Not Just Social, Problem

India banned prenatal sex determination under the Pre-Conception and Pre-Natal Diagnostic Techniques (PCPNDT) Act, 1994, precisely because sex-selective practices skew the sex ratio. The Child Sex Ratio (girls per 1,000 boys aged 0-6) fell from 945 in 1991 to 919 in 2011, per Census data, before showing marginal recovery in some states.

Laws alone have not erased the preference. The court's remark reflects what demographers and activists have documented for decades: families may not abort a girl, but they can still neglect, discriminate against, or in extreme cases harm her after birth.

Real-World Implications for Families and Investigators

Cases of violence against girl children are chronically under-reported. When the accused is a relative, neighbours and extended family often hesitate to intervene, and post-mortem evidence may be the only independent proof.

For investigators, the ruling underscores the need for airtight forensic documentation, since defence counsel will probe every gap in the chain of evidence. For prosecutors, judicial remarks on motive strengthen the narrative that the killing was not accidental.

Key Insights

  • The Bombay High Court's refusal of bail prioritises the seriousness of the alleged offence over liberty at this stage.
  • The bench's comment on son preference places the case within a documented national pattern of gender bias.
  • PCPNDT Act violations and post-birth discrimination are legally distinct but socially linked problems.
  • Family-inside crimes are harder to detect, making forensic rigour and witness protection critical.
  • The order does not determine guilt; the trial will decide the accused's fate.

What Happens Next

The trial court will proceed with framing of charges and evidence. If convicted of murder, the accused faces a minimum life sentence under the Bharatiya Nyaya Sanhita, with the death penalty possible in rarest-of-rare cases.

Civil society groups argue that bail orders like this one matter beyond the individual case, because they shape how families perceive the risk of harming a girl child. Whether that deterrence holds depends on conviction rates, which remain slow and uneven across Maharashtra's district courts.

For now, the Bombay High Court has drawn a clear line: the preference for a male child, however widespread, cannot be offered as context that softens an allegation of killing a girl.
