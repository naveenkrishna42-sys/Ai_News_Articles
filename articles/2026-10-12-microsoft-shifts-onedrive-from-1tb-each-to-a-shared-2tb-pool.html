Microsoft shifts OneDrive from 1TB each to a shared 2TB pool

✍️ By TIVRA News Tech Desk 📅 2026-10-12 📂 Technology Original source ↗
Key points
  • Expect OneDrive storage to move from per-user limits to a shared pool, affecting households and teams with heavy photo/video backups.
  • Before subscribing, audit your current OneDrive usage across accounts and estimate growth over the next 12 months.
  • Plan for AI features, syncing, and device backups—shared storage can create faster contention during the year.

What Microsoft is changing in OneDrive storage

Microsoft has moved to a new storage model for OneDrive under Microsoft 365 Family and Microsoft 365 Premium, replacing a higher per-person allocation with a smaller, shared pool. Reports summarised by outlets including gHacks indicate the shift is designed to consolidate capacity and standardise how subscriptions allocate storage across linked accounts.

For buyers, the practical takeaway is simple: storage behaviour changes from “everyone has their own runway” to “everyone shares one capacity bucket.” That matters most for families with multiple photo libraries, screenshot-heavy workflows, or users who keep offline folders and camera uploads running continuously.

This also changes how you should evaluate value. Many people buy Microsoft 365 for bundled productivity apps, device syncing, and cloud backup. If the cloud storage cap tightens, your total cost of ownership includes the time and effort to manage storage hygiene (de-duplication, archiving, and deciding what truly needs to stay in OneDrive).

Buyer impact: why shared capacity can feel smaller

Under a shared pool model, your “effective” storage limit is your slice of the overall pool—determined by how many accounts contribute data growth. Even if your own usage is low, others’ uploads can trigger throttling-like experiences: sync pauses, backup delays, and reduced ability to create new files.

In real households, the biggest drivers are camera roll backups, large video libraries, and shared devices where multiple people upload the same project folders. If you currently rely on 1TB per person to avoid cleanup, a shared 2TB pool can compress the headroom faster than expected, particularly when multiple accounts add content throughout the year.

From an enterprise buyer perspective, even though this change is targeted at Family and Premium, the lesson is transferable: Microsoft is leaning toward pooled entitlements across subscription experiences. That trend increases the importance of monitoring and establishing storage governance—who can upload, what gets backed up, and what should move to cheaper archiving.

What to check before you rely on OneDrive

Before renewing or switching plans, you should check current storage usage per Microsoft account and identify the top sources of growth. In practice, the most common culprits are OneDrive Photos, desktop sync folders, and team workflows using shared links or embedded media that quietly consume storage.

Then estimate a 12-month growth rate based on actual behaviour: how many devices upload monthly, how many new photos/videos are generated, and whether you plan to enable additional backup features. If you can’t quantify it, set a conservative buffer—storage caps that feel fine today often become friction points mid-year.

Practical benchmarks for deciding if the pool works for you

Shared storage succeeds when households have complementary usage patterns: one user backs up large libraries, while others keep docs lightweight. It fails when everyone is a heavy uploader. A useful buyer heuristic is to compare current total usage (all linked users) against the shared capacity and add a realistic growth buffer.

For most buyers, the deciding question isn’t “Do I use OneDrive?” but “How much cumulative storage do I create across every account in the subscription?” If you manage one or two small workspaces, 2TB shared can remain comfortable. If multiple users stream phones to cloud, the pool tends to fill quickly.

Also consider how you store large media. If your workflow includes frequent exports, raw video drafts, or high-resolution photo bursts, you may need a strategy: periodic archiving to external storage, trimming old versions, or moving rarely accessed files out of OneDrive.

Pros and cons of the new shared pool approach

  • Pros: Simpler entitlement structure across linked users; potentially better for households where usage is balanced.
  • Pros: Encourages storage governance (cleaner sync habits, fewer abandoned large folders).
  • Cons: Higher chance of “account A is full because account B uploaded” frustration.
  • Cons: Less predictable storage for heavy photographers/videographers within the same subscription.
  • Cons: More buyer effort required: monitoring, pruning, and deciding what belongs in cloud vs. archive.

Value verdict: how to justify Microsoft 365 Family/Premium after the change

Microsoft 365 still bundles major productivity benefits—Word, Excel, PowerPoint, device syncing, and collaboration features. The storage shift changes whether those benefits justify the subscription if cloud backup is your primary reason to pay.

For a value-positive decision, you should have evidence that your household’s combined storage footprint stays comfortably under the shared pool, with a buffer for ongoing uploads. If you are already close to the cap, the shared model makes the subscription feel more like a rotating constraint than a safety net.

For buyers who expect growth in photo/video libraries, the most defensible path is to plan storage early: audit OneDrive usage, restrict backup scope, and define retention rules for older media. In doing so, you protect the core productivity value of Microsoft 365 while avoiding the avoidable cost of mid-year storage emergencies.
