US Intelligence Flags China Risk Over F-35 Sale to Saudi Arabia

US intelligence agencies have reportedly renewed a warning that China could attempt to obtain sensitive F-35 technology once American-made stealth fighters enter service with Saudi Arabia. The warning follows the Trump administration's approval of a fighter jet package for Riyadh valued at approximately $24.3 billion, according to Reuters and Al Jazeera.

The New York Times and The Times of Israel reported that the assessment reiterates long-standing concerns about the exposure of low-observable materials, radar-absorbent coatings and mission-system software to third parties. No specific theft incident has been confirmed; the warning is a risk assessment, not evidence of a breach.

Why the F-35 Is a Permanent Counter-Intelligence Target

The F-35 Lightning II is not valuable merely because it is stealthy. Its real edge lies in sensor fusion, electronic warfare suites, secure data links and the logistics backbone that keeps the fleet flying. Each of these is a high-value intelligence target.

Export customers operate the aircraft under strict end-user agreements, but physical access to airframes, maintenance bays and ground support equipment creates theoretical pathways for technical collection. The US has mitigated this for years through programme security arrangements, contractor oversight and restricted software access.

Real-World Buyer Implications

For Saudi Arabia, the package offers a generational leap in air combat capability and interoperability with US forces. The trade-off is heavier programme security: restricted access to source code, US-controlled maintenance for sensitive components and audit rights that few buyers enjoy in full.

For Washington, the sale is a strategic bet. It deepens a decades-old defence relationship, but it also widens the circle of countries with physical proximity to America's most advanced combat aircraft. Every additional operator increases the counter-intelligence workload.

For other F-35 customers and prospective buyers, the episode signals that future approvals will hinge as much on security architecture as on price. Nations with diversified defence procurement—particularly those with significant Chinese technology ties—should expect tougher conditions, slower delivery and higher lifecycle costs.

Pros and Cons of the Deal

  • Pro: Delivers a genuine fifth-generation capability to Saudi Arabia and strengthens US-Gulf defence alignment.
  • Pro: Supports Lockheed Martin's production line and sustains thousands of supplier jobs across the US and partner nations.
  • Pro: Standardisation with US and NATO operators improves coalition interoperability.
  • Con: Expands the physical footprint of F-35 technology into a region with complex great-power dynamics.
  • Con: Raises the risk—however theoretical—of technical collection by China or its proxies.
  • Con: Tougher security conditions could raise costs and slow training, spares and software updates for Riyadh.

The Bottom Line for Buyers

Stealth aircraft are sold as hardware but governed as secrets. The reported US intelligence warning does not allege that China has stolen F-35 technology from Saudi Arabia. It flags a risk that will shape how the deal is implemented: who touches the aircraft, where it is maintained and which systems remain under US control.

Prospective buyers should read this as a pricing signal. Advanced fighter packages increasingly bundle security guarantees, restricted access and long-term US oversight. That raises the true cost of ownership—but it is also the price of admission to the fifth-generation club.
