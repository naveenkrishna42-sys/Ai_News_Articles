NASA's 'Big Bang' Maneuver Keeps Voyager 2 Alive for Another Year

📅 2026-08-09 📂 Science Original source ↗
Key points
  • Voyager 2, launched 48 years ago, gets a one-year mission extension via a 'Big Bang' maneuver.
  • Engineers remotely adjusted power usage to keep science instruments running despite dwindling fuel.
  • The same technique may be applied to Voyager 1, but no timeline has been confirmed.
  • Commands take nearly 24 hours to reach the probe, making the operation highly risky.

A 48-Year-Old Probe Gets a Second Wind

NASA engineers have pulled off a delicate balancing act in deep space. The Voyager 2 probe, launched in 1977, will continue its science mission for at least one more year thanks to a risky power-management maneuver. The spacecraft, now over 20 billion kilometres from Earth, is running on fumes — literally, in terms of its radioisotope thermoelectric generators.

The mission team calls it the 'Big Bang' maneuver. It involves carefully shutting off a voltage regulator that protects the science instruments from power surges. By bypassing this safety mechanism, engineers can squeeze out more usable electricity from the decaying power source.

This is not a simple switch flip. Commands take nearly 23 hours to reach Voyager 2, and another 23 for any response to return. One wrong setting could permanently silence the probe.

Why the Risk Was Necessary

Voyager 2's power output drops by about 4 watts every year. After 48 years in space, the margin has become razor-thin. The spacecraft's five science instruments — which study cosmic rays, plasma waves, and magnetic fields — need a steady voltage to function. Without the maneuver, engineers would have had to shut down one or more instruments within months.

The new approach frees up enough power to keep all instruments running through August 2027. NASA's Jet Propulsion Laboratory confirmed the operation was successful, though details of the engineering logic were not fully disclosed.

This is the latest in a series of improvised fixes. Over the past decade, engineers have switched off heaters, reduced instrument duty cycles, and even used a backup thruster to save propellant. Each trick buys a little more time for the mission.

Voyager 1: The Next Challenge

Attention now shifts to Voyager 1, launched two weeks after its twin. It is roughly 24 billion kilometres away and faces a similar power crisis. Its instruments are aging too, and engineers have not yet confirmed whether the same 'Big Bang' approach will work there.

The two spacecraft have different power systems and load profiles. What works for Voyager 2 may not transfer directly. NASA has said only that they are studying the possibility.

Voyager 1 already lost one instrument in 2024 due to a hardware glitch. Its plasma science subsystem has been non-functional for years. The team is conservative about promises.

Why These Probes Still Matter

Both spacecraft are in interstellar space — the region beyond the Sun's magnetic bubble. They are the only human-made objects to have crossed this boundary. Every additional month of data helps scientists understand the heliopause, cosmic ray intensity, and the interstellar magnetic field.

No new mission is planned to replace them. The Voyagers are the sole witnesses to this remote environment. Their longevity is a testament to 1970s engineering, but the clock is ticking.

Power is not the only constraint. The spacecraft's thrusters use hydrazine fuel, and the heaters that keep fuel lines from freezing are also aging. Each year, the list of potential failure points grows longer.

What Happens Next

NASA will monitor Voyager 2's performance over the coming months to ensure the maneuver does not degrade data quality. If all goes well, the same fix may be attempted on Voyager 1 by late 2026.

Beyond that, the end is inevitable. The probes will eventually go silent, but they will continue their lonely journey through the galaxy for billions of years. For now, every extra year of science is a victory.
