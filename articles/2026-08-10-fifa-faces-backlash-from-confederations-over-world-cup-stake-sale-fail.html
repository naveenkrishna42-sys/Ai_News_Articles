FIFA faces backlash from confederations over World Cup stake sale failure

📅 2026-08-10 📂 Sports Original source ↗
Key points
  • UEFA, AFC, and CONCACAF have issued a rare joint statement criticising FIFA president Gianni Infantino.
  • The criticism follows the collapse of a proposed sale of a stake in the FIFA World Cup.
  • Confederation presidents are calling for Infantino to step down and discussing rival competitions.
  • The open letter accuses FIFA of breaking trust 'through deception'.

The simmering tensions inside world football's governing body have boiled over. Three of FIFA's six confederations — UEFA, the Asian Football Confederation (AFC), and CONCACAF — have publicly turned on FIFA president Gianni Infantino. Their anger stems from the collapse of a proposed sale of a stake in the FIFA World Cup, a deal that has now unravelled in acrimony.

In an open letter addressed to the football family, the presidents of the three confederations accused FIFA of breaking trust 'through deception'. The letter, published across multiple platforms, marks an unusually direct and coordinated attack on Infantino's leadership. It stops short of naming him in the headline, but the target is unmistakable.

A rare show of unity

UEFA, AFC, and CONCACAF represent a significant chunk of global football, spanning Europe, Asia, and North and Central America. Their joint statement is a rare show of unity among organisations that often have competing interests. The three bodies have historically been wary of each other, but this crisis has forged a common front.

The core grievance is the failed World Cup stake sale. The proposal, which was meant to bring in external investment, has collapsed. The confederations claim they were kept in the dark about key details and that the process was mishandled. The open letter frames the failure as a breach of the fundamental trust that underpins football governance.

Demands for Infantino to quit

The letter goes beyond criticism. It explicitly pushes for Infantino to step down from his role. The confederation presidents are not just questioning the failed deal; they are questioning his fitness to lead. This escalates a long-simmering discontent into a full-blown leadership challenge.

According to reports, the three confederations are also discussing the possibility of organising rival competitions. The idea is to create an alternative to FIFA-sanctioned events, a move that would fracture the sport's existing structure. Such a breakaway has been floated before, but never with this level of seriousness.

FIFA has not yet responded to the open letter. Officials within the organisation have not confirmed any immediate action. The silence from Zurich is telling, given the gravity of the accusations.

What led to this point

The failed stake sale was meant to be a landmark financial move. The plan was to sell a minority share in the World Cup, a property that generates billions of dollars in revenue. The goal was to unlock new capital for FIFA's development programmes, but the negotiations fell apart.

Sources familiar with the matter suggest that the confederations felt sidelined during the talks. They allege that key decisions were made without proper consultation, and that information was withheld. This, they argue, amounts to deception — a strong word in the usually diplomatic world of football politics.

The open letter's language is blunt. It speaks of eroded trust and a broken relationship. For the three confederations, this is not just about a single deal; it is about the broader direction of FIFA under Infantino's stewardship.

The road ahead

The immediate question is whether other confederations — CONMEBOL, CAF, and the OFC — will side with the rebels or with FIFA. Their stance could determine whether this becomes a full-scale revolt or a contained dispute. The coming weeks are likely to see intense behind-the-scenes lobbying.

For Infantino, the challenge is existential. He has survived previous controversies, but this one involves his own peers in the football family. The push for his resignation is not coming from external critics; it is coming from within the sport's leadership.

Rival competitions remain a hypothetical, but the very fact that they are being discussed signals a new level of fracture. If the confederations follow through, the global football calendar could be thrown into chaos. The stakes are as high as they have ever been in the sport's governance.

As the football world watches, the next move lies with FIFA. Whether Infantino chooses to address the letter head-on or let the storm pass will shape the coming months. One thing is clear: the trust that once held the federation together has been severely damaged, and repairing it will not be easy.
