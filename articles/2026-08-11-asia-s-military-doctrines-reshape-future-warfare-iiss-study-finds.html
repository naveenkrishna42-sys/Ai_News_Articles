Asia's Military Doctrines Reshape Future Warfare, IISS Study Finds

📅 2026-08-11 📂 Wars & Conflicts Original source ↗
Key points
  • IISS report examines military doctrines across Asia
  • Focus on future warfare and strategic shifts
  • Rising tensions and technology drive doctrinal changes

Doctrine in Flux

The International Institute for Strategic Studies (IISS) has released the first chapter of its latest report, titled "Fighting Lens: Military Doctrine and the Future of Warfare in Asia." The study examines how Asian militaries are rethinking their war-fighting approaches in response to geopolitical shifts and technological advancements.

The report, published on May 28, 2026, underscores that military doctrine is no longer a static set of rules. Instead, it is evolving rapidly as nations across the region adapt to new threats and capabilities. The IISS, a leading global think tank on military affairs, has based its analysis on years of research and engagement with defence establishments.

Key Drivers of Change

Several factors are pushing doctrinal evolution. The rise of advanced technologies, including drones, cyber tools, and precision-strike systems, is compelling militaries to integrate these into their core strategies. The report notes that traditional conventional warfare models are being supplemented—and in some cases replaced—by hybrid approaches that blend regular and irregular tactics.

Regional tensions, particularly in the South China Sea and the Korean Peninsula, are also accelerating the pace of change. Nations are increasingly focusing on deterrence, rapid response, and the ability to operate in contested environments. The IISS argues that these shifts are not merely reactive but reflect deeper strategic calculations about the future balance of power.

Technological Imperatives

Technology is a central pillar of the report's findings. Autonomous systems, artificial intelligence, and space-based assets are no longer peripheral to military planning. They are becoming central to how Asian powers conceive of victory and defeat. The study highlights that countries are investing heavily in these areas, but doctrinal adaptation lags behind procurement in some cases.

This gap, the IISS warns, could create vulnerabilities. A military may possess advanced hardware but lack the operational concepts to use it effectively. The report calls for greater attention to training, simulation, and multi-domain integration as part of doctrinal reform.

Regional Variations

The report avoids a one-size-fits-all narrative. It notes that different Asian states have distinct doctrinal priorities based on their threat perceptions and strategic cultures. For some, the emphasis is on anti-access and area denial capabilities. For others, the focus is on peacekeeping and humanitarian missions. The IISS stresses that understanding these nuances is critical for policymakers and analysts.

Moreover, the study examines how alliances and partnerships shape doctrinal choices. The United States' network of allies in the region, along with emerging groupings like the Quad, influences how nations adopt and adapt military concepts. The report suggests that interoperability is becoming a key concern, as forces may need to operate together in future contingencies.

Implications for the Future

The IISS report serves as a timely reminder that warfare is not static. As Asia's economic and military weight grows, the doctrines developed here will have global repercussions. The study is part of a broader series that aims to provide a comprehensive picture of future warfare, with subsequent chapters expected to explore other regions and domains.

Analysts will be watching closely to see how these doctrinal shifts translate into concrete defence policies and force postures. The report's release comes at a moment when defence budgets across Asia are rising, and military modernisation is accelerating. Whether these new doctrines will prove effective in actual conflict remains to be seen, but the intellectual groundwork is being laid now.

As the IISS continues its research, the next chapters are likely to delve into maritime security, space warfare, and the role of nuclear deterrence in Asia. For now, this first chapter sets the stage for a deeper conversation about how the region prepares for an uncertain future.
