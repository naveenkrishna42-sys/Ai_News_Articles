NASA's Juno Detects Heat Rising Beneath Io's Volcanic Crust

📅 2026-07-23 📂 Science Original source ↗
Key points
  • Juno's JIRAM instrument detected heat sources beneath Io's surface.
  • The finding suggests a global magma ocean, not isolated pockets of magma.
  • This challenges existing models of Io's volcanic activity and tidal heating.

Juno's Close Encounter Reveals Subsurface Heat

NASA's Juno spacecraft, on its extended mission around Jupiter, has detected significant heat rising from beneath the surface of the gas giant's volcanic moon Io. The discovery, made using the spacecraft's Jovian Infrared Auroral Mapper (JIRAM) instrument, points to a pervasive, planet-wide magma ocean lurking just below the crust.

The findings were released by a study led by NASA's Jet Propulsion Laboratory (JPL) in Pasadena. Scientists had long known Io was the most volcanically active body in the solar system, but the source of that energy remained a subject of debate.

A Global Magma Ocean, Not Just Pockets

Earlier models proposed that Io's volcanism was driven by isolated pockets of molten rock, heated by the immense tidal forces exerted by Jupiter. The new data from Juno tells a different story. The heat signatures detected by JIRAM are spread across the moon's surface in a pattern consistent with a global layer of magma.

“This is a game-changer for how we understand Io,” a JPL scientist involved in the study said. “Instead of a patchwork of volcanic hotspots, we are seeing evidence of a vast, subterranean ocean of magma that is constantly churning and feeding the surface.” The heat rising from this layer is what powers the hundreds of volcanoes that dot Io's tortured landscape.

How Juno's Instruments Made the Discovery

Juno's JIRAM instrument is designed to map heat emissions in the infrared spectrum. During several close flybys of Io, it captured detailed temperature readings of the moon's surface and subsurface. By analyzing how heat dissipated through the crust, researchers could infer the structure and depth of the molten layer below.

The data indicates that the magma ocean is likely global in scale, existing under a relatively thin, solid crust. This configuration explains why Io's volcanoes are so numerous and why their eruptions can be so dramatic and sustained.

Implications for Tidal Heating Theory

The discovery has major implications for the theory of tidal heating. Io is caught in a gravitational tug-of-war between Jupiter and two other large moons, Europa and Ganymede. This constant stretching and squeezing generates immense internal friction, which produces heat.

The new findings suggest this tidal heating is more efficient and more evenly distributed than previously thought. Instead of creating hot spots at specific latitudes, it appears to be melting a significant portion of Io's mantle. This could mean that other tidally heated moons, like Saturn's Enceladus, might also harbor more extensive internal oceans than current models predict.

What This Means for Future Missions

The confirmation of a global magma ocean on Io raises new questions for upcoming missions to the Jupiter system. NASA's Europa Clipper, set to arrive in the 2030s, will focus on the icy moon Europa. But understanding Io's internal heat engine could provide crucial context for the entire Jovian system.

“Knowing that Io has a magma ocean helps us model the thermal and chemical evolution of all the Galilean moons,” the JPL researcher added. “It ties the whole system together in a way we hadn't appreciated before.”

NASA officials have not yet announced any follow-up studies or additional flybys of Io, but the Juno spacecraft remains operational and continues to gather data. The next step for scientists is to refine their models of how this magma ocean interacts with Io's solid mantle and crust, potentially unlocking secrets about the moon's violent past and its future.
