UAE Refuses to Screen Sensitive Hindi Films, Gulf Divide Widens

📅 2026-07-27 📂 Movies Original source ↗
Key points
  • UAE censors have rejected multiple Hindi films citing 'sensitive' content.
  • The move widens a cultural gap between Gulf nations over film screening policies.
  • Bollywood producers face uncertainty over regional release plans.

The United Arab Emirates has quietly refused to clear several Hindi films for theatrical release in the country, citing content it considers 'sensitive.' The decision marks a growing divergence in cultural norms across the Gulf region, leaving Bollywood producers scrambling to adjust their release strategies.

Trade insiders told TIVRA News that at least three major Hindi films slated for early 2026 have been denied certification by UAE censors. The films, which deal with themes of identity, religion, and historical narratives, were flagged for scenes and dialogues that authorities described as 'contrary to local sensitivities.'

This is not an outright ban. Sources say the UAE's National Media Council has not issued a blanket prohibition. Instead, each film is being evaluated case by case. But the pattern is clear — projects that might have sailed through in previous years are now being held back.

Shift in Gulf Dynamics

The UAE's stance stands in stark contrast to Saudi Arabia, which has in recent years opened up its cinemas and even co-produced projects with Indian studios. Saudi authorities have screened multiple Hindi films that were denied in the UAE, including some that touch on similar themes.

Industry analysts point to a strategic realignment. The UAE is increasingly positioning itself as a conservative cultural gatekeeper, while Saudi Arabia is pursuing a more liberal entertainment agenda under its Vision 2030 plan. The result is a fractured Gulf market for Bollywood.

“Producers now have to plan separate cuts for each emirate and for Saudi,” a trade source said. “It adds cost and confusion. No one knows where the red line will be drawn next.”

Bollywood's Gulf Dependence

The Gulf region has long been a lucrative secondary market for Hindi films. The UAE alone accounts for roughly 10-15 percent of the overseas box office for a typical Bollywood blockbuster. The Saudi market, though newer, is growing fast and already matching UAE numbers for some releases.

But the unpredictability of censorship decisions is making it harder for producers to lock in release dates. Several films originally scheduled for a simultaneous India-Gulf release have had to delay their Middle East premieres by weeks while awaiting clearances.

“The UAE is a mature market with a large South Asian diaspora,” said a Mumbai-based distribution executive. “But if the censors keep blocking content, that audience will either travel to Saudi to watch these films or wait for streaming. That hurts the local cinema business.”

What Content Is Being Cut?

Exact details of the rejected films have not been disclosed. But sources indicate that the common thread is content that questions religious orthodoxy, portrays interfaith relationships, or revisits historical events from a non-official perspective. The UAE censors have also been stricter about depictions of violence and sexuality.

One film, a drama about a Hindu-Muslim romance set against a political backdrop, was asked to remove a key dialogue referencing a historical figure. The producers refused and pulled the film from the UAE release entirely.

Another film, a satire on religious hypocrisy, was rejected outright without explanation. The makers are now preparing a revised version for resubmission, but they expect further delays.

Indian filmmakers are watching the developments closely. The Ministry of External Affairs has not commented on the matter, and no official diplomatic intervention is expected. The film industry, for now, is left to navigate the shifting sand alone.

What happens next will depend on how the UAE formalises its criteria. If the current trend continues, Bollywood may have to treat the Gulf as two separate markets — one open, one guarded — and plan its releases accordingly.
