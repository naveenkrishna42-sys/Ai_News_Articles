Another 26 Rescued: What the Bengaluru Operation Revealed

Bengaluru authorities have rescued 26 more child labourers in a fresh operation, adding to a growing list of minors freed from hazardous workplaces across the city. The children, many trafficked from Bihar, Jharkhand, and Odisha, were found working in small manufacturing units, eateries, and repair shops. This latest rescue brings the total number of children saved in recent weeks to over 80, according to officials involved in the raids.

The operation, coordinated by the Department of Labour, city police, and child welfare committees, targeted areas in and around Peenya, Bommanahalli, and KR Market. Most children were between 10 and 16 years old, working 12-hour days for as little as Rs 3,000 per month. The New Indian Express first reported the rescue on Monday.

How the Rescue Unfolded

Acting on specific intelligence, teams conducted simultaneous raids on Sunday morning. Investigators found children sleeping on shop floors, handling welding equipment, and serving at roadside dhabas. Three employers were arrested, and cases were filed under the Child Labour (Prohibition and Regulation) Amendment Act, 2016.

This law bans employment of children below 14 in all occupations and prohibits adolescents (14-18) from hazardous work. Penalties include imprisonment of 2 to 5 years and fines up to Rs 50,000 for first-time offenders. Repeat offenders face up to 7 years in jail. Yet conviction rates remain low—a 2025 National Crime Records Bureau report showed only 12% of child labour cases ended in conviction.

Why Bengaluru Is a Magnet for Child Trafficking

Bengaluru’s sprawling informal economy—garment units, auto garages, hotels, and domestic work—absorbs cheap labour with little oversight. Traffickers promise families in poorer states jobs and education, then push children into bonded conditions. A 2024 study by the Bachpan Bachao Andolan estimated that 40% of child labourers in Karnataka’s capital come from other states.

The city’s construction boom and e-commerce logistics hubs have also created demand for young workers in packing and delivery. Labour inspectors, numbering fewer than 200 for a city of 14 million, cannot cover the estimated 50,000 unregistered enterprises.

What This Means for Buyers and Businesses

Child labour is not just a moral failure—it is a commercial risk. Under Indian law, companies can be held liable if their supply chains use child labour, even indirectly. The Companies Act, 2013 mandates CSR reporting on eradication of child labour, and the upcoming Business Responsibility and Sustainability Report (BRSR) framework requires listed firms to disclose supply chain audits.

For consumers, the warning is clear: heavily discounted products—be it a Rs 100 t-shirt or a Rs 50 meal—often hide labour exploitation. Bengaluru’s informal units supply local markets and online sellers. A 2025 study by the Fair Labour Forum found that 1 in 5 small workshops in the city employed at least one minor.

Pros and Cons of the Current Enforcement Approach

  • Pros: Swift rescue operations show political will; increased media coverage pressures authorities; new digital tracking of rescued children improves rehabilitation.
  • Cons: Low conviction rates weaken deterrence; rehabilitation funds are underutilised; inter-state coordination remains poor; most cases never reach court.
  • Real-world highlight: Rescued children often return to the same exploitative conditions within months due to inadequate follow-up and family debt.

What Happens Next

The rescued children are now in government shelters, and child welfare committees will assess their needs. But without addressing the root causes—poverty, trafficking networks, and weak enforcement—rescues remain a temporary fix. The Karnataka government has announced a task force to audit high-risk industries, but similar promises in 2023 yielded few results.

For Bengaluru’s businesses, the message is simple: verify your labour sources or face legal and reputational damage. For consumers, ask where your cheap goods come from. The true cost of a bargain is often borne by a child.
