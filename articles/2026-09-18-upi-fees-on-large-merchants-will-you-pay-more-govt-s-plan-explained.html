  • Small merchants with turnover under ₹20 lakh remain exempt, and person-to-person transfers stay free.

UPI's Free Ride Ends for Large Merchants—But What About You?

Since 2016, Unified Payments Interface (UPI) has been India's digital payment poster child—free, instant, and ubiquitous. That era is now officially over for large merchants. The National Payments Corporation of India (NPCI) has introduced a Merchant Discount Rate (MDR) on UPI transactions above ₹2,000 for specific merchant categories. The government is reportedly preparing a system to ensure this fee doesn't directly hit customers. But will it succeed?

According to a Hindustan Times report, the government is working on a mechanism to absorb or offset the MDR for consumers, even as merchants begin to see deductions. The move comes after years of lobbying by banks and fintechs who argued that free UPI was unsustainable. Now, the question on every user's mind: will your next ₹2,500 UPI payment cost you extra?

What Exactly Is Changing?

The new rules apply only to merchant transactions—not person-to-person (P2P) transfers. MDR of 0.5% to 1.1% will be levied on transactions above ₹2,000 for large merchants (turnover above ₹20 lakh). Small merchants remain exempt. The fee is charged to the merchant, not the customer—at least on paper. However, merchants can legally pass it on as a surcharge, just like credit card fees.

How the Government Plans to Shield Customers

The government is exploring multiple options: a subsidy mechanism for small-value transactions, mandating that merchants cannot charge surcharges above a cap, or creating a separate fund to reimburse MDR for high-volume categories like utilities and fuel. Officials have indicated that any system will prioritise "customer convenience" and "digital adoption." But industry experts remain sceptical—merchants already operate on thin margins and may pass on costs indirectly through higher product prices.

Kerala's traders' forum has already announced a boycott of UPI transactions on October 15, protesting the fee. The Times of India quoted a shopkeeper's concern: "Our interest should be protected." Meanwhile, The Indian Express warns that ultimately, the customer will pay—either via surcharge or inflated prices.

Real-World Impact: What It Means for Your Wallet

For a ₹5,000 UPI payment to a large merchant, MDR at 0.9% equals ₹45. If passed on, that's a 0.9% effective cost increase. On a monthly spend of ₹20,000 via UPI to large merchants, you could pay an extra ₹180. That's not trivial for middle-class households. However, if the government's shielding system works, you may see no direct charge—but the cost could still be buried in pricing.

  • Pro: UPI becomes financially sustainable for banks, potentially improving service quality and innovation.
  • Pro: Small merchants and P2P transfers remain free, protecting everyday users.
  • Con: Merchants may pass on the fee as surcharge, increasing costs for consumers.
  • Con: Ambiguity remains on how the government will enforce the "no customer impact" promise.
  • Con: Traders' protests could disrupt UPI acceptance in some regions.

Buyer's Guide: Should You Change How You Pay?

If you frequently make large UPI payments to big brands, watch for surcharges at checkout. Compare with credit cards—many offer rewards that offset MDR. For small merchants, UPI remains the cheapest option. The government's system is still evolving, so stay informed. As of now, no direct fee has been imposed on customers, but the fine print matters.

The bottom line: UPI's free era for large merchants is over, but for consumers, the fight to stay free is just beginning. The government's next moves will determine whether your digital wallet stays light or gets lighter.
