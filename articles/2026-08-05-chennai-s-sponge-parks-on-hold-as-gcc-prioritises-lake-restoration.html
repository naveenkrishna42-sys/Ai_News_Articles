Chennai's sponge parks on hold as GCC prioritises lake restoration

📅 2026-08-05 📂 Chennai Original source ↗
Key points
  • GCC has put sponge park projects on hold
  • Focus shifts to lake restoration across Chennai
  • Move aims to improve groundwater recharge and flood resilience

Sponge parks paused in Chennai

The Greater Chennai Corporation (GCC) has temporarily shelved its sponge park projects, redirecting resources and attention towards restoring the city's lakes. The decision marks a notable shift in the civic body's approach to urban water management.

Sponge parks, designed to absorb rainwater and reduce flooding, were part of Chennai's broader resilience plan. However, officials now believe that reviving existing water bodies will deliver more tangible benefits for groundwater recharge and flood control.

Why the shift?

Chennai has faced recurring water stress and flash floods in recent years. While sponge parks offered a modern solution, their impact was seen as limited compared to the potential of restored lakes, which can store large volumes of water and support local ecology.

A senior GCC official, speaking on condition of anonymity, said the decision followed a review of ongoing projects. The corporation found that many lake restoration works had been delayed, and prioritising them would yield quicker, more visible results for residents.

What happens to existing sponge parks?

Projects that are already underway will be completed, but no new sponge parks will be taken up for now. The GCC has not announced a timeline for resuming these projects, and officials have not confirmed whether the pause is temporary or permanent.

Environmental activists have welcomed the focus on lakes but caution that sponge parks should not be abandoned entirely. They argue that a mix of green infrastructure and water body restoration is essential for a city like Chennai, which experiences both droughts and heavy monsoon rains.

Focus on lake restoration

The GCC's lake restoration programme involves desilting, removing encroachments, and improving inlets and outlets. These measures are expected to enhance the carrying capacity of lakes and prevent flooding in downstream neighbourhoods.

Several lakes across the city, including those in the northern and western suburbs, are in various stages of restoration. The corporation has also tied up with state agencies and citizen groups to speed up the work.

Officials say the restored lakes will not only help recharge groundwater but also serve as recreational spaces for the public. Some lakes are being developed with walking tracks and viewing platforms, though these amenities are secondary to the core hydrological goals.

What to watch next

The GCC is expected to release a detailed status report on lake restoration works in the coming months. Residents and environmental groups will be watching closely to see if the shift in focus translates into faster, on-ground results. Whether sponge parks make a comeback will depend on the success of this renewed lake-first strategy.
