UPI Ends Free Era: 0.4% Fee on Merchant Payments Above ₹2,000

✍️ By TIVRA News Markets & Finance Desk 📅 2026-09-15 📂 Business Original source ↗
Key points
  • NPCI will levy a 0.4% Merchant Discount Rate on UPI person-to-merchant transactions above ₹2,000 from October 15, 2026, while small vendors remain exempt.
  • For a ₹5,000 transaction, the merchant pays roughly ₹20 — thin for large retailers, but painful for businesses running on 3-6% net margins.
  • Consumers face no direct charge, but zero-MDR economics historically get recovered through pricing, surcharges or reduced cashback.
  • The real test is whether card and wallet MDR — typically 1.5-2% — finally levels out, or merchants simply push customers back to cash.

UPI's Free Ride Officially Ends on October 15

For nearly a decade, India's Unified Payments Interface operated on a simple promise: zero cost for merchants, zero cost for customers. That era closes on October 15, 2026, when the National Payments Corporation of India (NPCI) begins levying a 0.4% Merchant Discount Rate (MDR) on person-to-merchant UPI transactions above ₹2,000. The notification ends months of speculation and reverses a policy position that successive governments defended as a public good.

Small vendors are exempt. The threshold structure means a kirana store accepting ₹500 via QR code pays nothing, while a large-format retailer processing a ₹15,000 electronics sale absorbs a fee it has never paid before.

What Exactly Changes — And What Doesn't

The fee applies only to person-to-merchant (P2M) flows. Person-to-person transfers, which dominate UPI volumes, remain free. The 0.4% is charged to the merchant, not the consumer — at least on paper. Banks and payment aggregators will collect it at settlement, and it applies per transaction above the ₹2,000 ceiling, not on the cumulative monthly value of a merchant's transactions.

The Arithmetic Merchants Will Run

On a ₹5,000 transaction, the merchant pays ₹20. On a ₹50,000 transaction, ₹200. For a mid-sized retailer doing ₹10 lakh a month in card and UPI sales above the threshold, the annual cost could run into tens of thousands of rupees — a real line item for businesses operating on 3-6% net margins.

Compare this to card MDR, which typically ranges from 1.5% to 2%, and the UPI rate still looks cheap. That gap is precisely the argument the fintech industry has made for years: UPI's zero-MDR model was never sustainable at scale, and someone had to pay for the servers, fraud controls and dispute resolution.

Why This Was Always Coming

UPI processes billions of transactions monthly, and the infrastructure cost of running it has been borne largely by banks and the government through subsidies. As volumes grew, so did the subsidy bill. The 0.4% rate is calibrated to be low enough to avoid pushing merchants back to cash, but high enough to create a revenue stream for the payments ecosystem.

Opposition figures and fintech founders, including former BharatPe co-founder Ashneer Grover, have publicly argued that UPI could have stayed free. Their criticism centres on timing and the risk that small businesses, which are technically exempt but often transact above ₹2,000, get squeezed by ambiguity in implementation.

What Large Merchants Should Do Before October 15

  • Audit transaction mix: Identify what share of your UPI inflows exceed ₹2,000. That number determines your actual cost.
  • Re-examine pricing: A 0.4% fee on a 3% margin business is material. Decide whether to absorb it or adjust pricing.
  • Compare rails: Card MDR at 1.5-2% versus UPI at 0.4% still favours UPI — but only if your average ticket size stays modest.
  • Check aggregator contracts: Some payment aggregators may absorb or markup the fee. Read the fine print before signing renewals.
  • Watch for surcharging: If merchants begin passing the cost to customers, consumer behaviour could shift back to cash for mid-ticket purchases.

Pros and Cons of the New MDR Regime

  • Pro: Creates a sustainable revenue model for banks and payment companies, reducing dependence on government subsidies.
  • Pro: At 0.4%, UPI remains significantly cheaper than cards for merchants.
  • Pro: Small vendors stay exempt, protecting the kirana and micro-merchant base.
  • Con: Consumers may indirectly pay through surcharges or reduced cashback offers.
  • Con: Implementation ambiguity around the ₹2,000 threshold and vendor classification could cause friction.
  • Con: Merchants with thin margins in categories like groceries and electronics may resist or route customers to cash.

The Buyer's Bottom Line

For consumers, nothing changes at the checkout counter on October 15 — the fee is not deducted from your account. But the economics behind the scenes shift. If merchants absorb the cost, prices stay flat and UPI adoption continues. If they pass it on, the zero-cost digital payments story that powered India's cashless boom gets its first real stress test.

For merchants above the small-vendor threshold, the next four weeks are about arithmetic, not ideology. Run the numbers on your own transaction data, negotiate with your payment aggregator, and decide whether 0.4% is a cost of doing business or a reason to revisit your payment mix.
