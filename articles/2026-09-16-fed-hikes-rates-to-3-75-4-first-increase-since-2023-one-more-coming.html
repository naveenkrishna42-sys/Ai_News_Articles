Fed Lifts Rates to 3.75-4%: The First Hike Since 2023

The US Federal Reserve has raised its benchmark federal funds rate to the 3.75-4% range, its first increase since 2023, as inflation continued to weigh on the American economy. The decision, taken by the Kevin Warsh-led Federal Open Market Committee (FOMC), signals one more hike before the end of 2026 — a stance that defied persistent public pressure from President Donald Trump for lower rates.

For Indian investors and borrowers, this is not a distant Washington story. A higher US policy rate lifts global borrowing costs, strengthens the dollar, and reshapes where foreign capital flows. Understanding the mechanics matters more than reacting to headlines.

What Exactly Changed — And Why It Matters

The Fed's move reverses a long pause that had markets comfortable with peak rates. With inflation proving stickier than expected, the FOMC chose to prioritise price stability over growth concerns. The signal of "one more to come this year" is the crucial forward guidance: it tells markets the tightening cycle is not finished.

The Warsh-led committee's willingness to act against political headwinds is significant. It reinforces the Fed's institutional independence — a factor global rating agencies and currency markets watch closely.

Real-World Buyer Implications for Indian Households

For a home loan borrower on an external benchmark-linked rate, a US hike does not directly change your EMI — RBI's repo rate does. But the indirect effects are real. A stronger dollar pressures the rupee, making imports costlier and complicating the RBI's inflation fight. If the rupee weakens sharply, the RBI may delay its own rate cuts, keeping Indian loan rates elevated for longer.

Deposit holders get a silver lining. Banks competing for funds may nudge fixed deposit rates higher, particularly on shorter tenures. Debt mutual fund investors should watch duration risk: rising global yields typically pull bond prices down, hurting long-duration funds more than short-duration ones.

Gold, Equities and the Dollar

Gold, priced in dollars, usually faces headwinds when US rates rise. Yet the metal has shown resilience whenever geopolitical risk dominates. Equity investors should focus on sectors with dollar earnings — IT and pharma — which benefit from a stronger greenback, while import-heavy sectors like oil marketing companies face margin pressure.

Key Real-World Highlights

  • Borrowers: No immediate EMI change, but a prolonged high-rate global environment could delay RBI cuts and keep Indian loan rates firm.
  • Depositors: Expect better short-term FD yields as banks compete for funds.
  • Equity investors: IT and pharma gain from dollar strength; importers and aviation face cost pressure.
  • Gold buyers: Rising US yields are a headwind, but safe-haven demand can offset it.
  • Global capital: Higher US yields can pull foreign portfolio investment away from emerging markets like India in the short term.

Value Justification and What to Watch Next

The Fed's credibility play is the real story. By hiking despite political pressure, the FOMC has bought itself room to manage inflation expectations — the single biggest driver of long-term borrowing costs. For Indian buyers, the practical takeaway is to avoid locking into long-duration debt products at current yields and to keep an eye on the rupee-dollar level, which will determine how quickly the RBI can ease policy.

Watch three data points: the next US inflation print, the rupee's movement against the dollar, and the RBI's commentary at its next policy review. These will decide whether this Fed hike is a one-off correction or the start of a renewed global tightening phase.
