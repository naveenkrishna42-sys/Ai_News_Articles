Climate change fuelling childhood trauma crisis, study warns

📅 2026-07-18 📂 Environment Original source ↗
Key points
  • Research published in Nature identifies climate-related extreme weather as a driver of adverse childhood experiences (ACEs).
  • Events like floods, cyclones, and heatwaves are linked to trauma, displacement, and long-term developmental harm in children.
  • Experts call for the inclusion of climate impacts in child welfare policies and humanitarian response frameworks.

Extreme weather events linked to climate change are not just destroying homes and livelihoods — they are inflicting lasting psychological and developmental damage on children, warns new research published in Nature. The study describes the phenomenon as a humanitarian crisis of the 21st century, calling for urgent policy shifts to address what researchers term climate-related adverse childhood experiences, or climate-ACEs.

What are climate-ACEs?

Adverse childhood experiences refer to potentially traumatic events that occur before the age of 18. These typically include abuse, neglect, or household dysfunction. The new research expands this framework to include climate-driven events such as cyclones, floods, wildfires, and extreme heat.

Children exposed to such events often face displacement, loss of family members, interrupted schooling, and food insecurity. The study argues that these experiences can lead to long-term mental health issues, developmental delays, and chronic physical illnesses.

A growing, global threat

The paper underlines that climate-ACEs are not limited to any one region. In India, for example, children in coastal areas face recurring cyclones, while those in northern plains endure deadly heatwaves. Each event carries the potential for trauma that compounds over time.

Researchers point out that unlike traditional ACEs, climate-ACEs are systemic and recurring. A child who loses their home to a flood one year may face another flood the next, with no time to recover. This cyclical nature makes the harm especially insidious.

Displacement and instability

Displacement is a major driver of climate-ACEs. When families are forced to move, children lose their social networks, access to healthcare, and stability. In relief camps, overcrowding and lack of privacy can increase risks of violence and exploitation.

The study notes that even after physical safety is restored, the emotional toll lingers. Children may experience anxiety, depression, and post-traumatic stress disorder, often without access to mental health support.

Call for policy integration

The authors urge governments and humanitarian agencies to formally recognise climate-ACEs within child protection frameworks. This means training first responders to identify signs of trauma in children, ensuring continuity of education during disasters, and providing long-term psychosocial support.

India's National Disaster Management Authority and state governments are being pressed to incorporate these findings into their standard operating procedures. The study also recommends that climate adaptation funds be used to support child-centric interventions.

Data gaps remain

Quantifying the scale of the problem remains a challenge. Many countries, including India, do not systematically collect data on children's mental health outcomes after climate events. The research calls for better data collection to inform policy and resource allocation.

Without such data, the true extent of the crisis remains hidden. The study warns that millions of children today are growing up in a world where climate instability is the norm, not the exception.

As global temperatures continue to rise, the frequency and intensity of extreme weather events will only increase. The question now is whether child welfare systems can adapt fast enough to protect an entire generation from a crisis they did not create.
