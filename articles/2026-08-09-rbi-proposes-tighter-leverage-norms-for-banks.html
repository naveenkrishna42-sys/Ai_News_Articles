RBI proposes tighter leverage norms for banks

📅 2026-08-09 📂 Banking Original source ↗
Key points
  • RBI has proposed stricter leverage ratio requirements for banks.
  • The move is aimed at curbing excessive risk-taking and ensuring financial stability.
  • The draft norms are open for public feedback until a specified deadline.

The Reserve Bank of India (RBI) on Friday floated draft guidelines proposing tighter leverage norms for banks, a move aimed at curbing excessive risk-taking and bolstering the resilience of the financial system. The proposed framework seeks to recalibrate how banks calculate their leverage ratios, potentially requiring them to hold more capital against their exposures.

The central bank's discussion paper outlines changes to the existing leverage ratio framework, which measures a bank's core capital against its total assets, including off-balance-sheet items. Under the current rules, banks are required to maintain a minimum leverage ratio of 3.5 per cent, but the new proposals could raise this threshold or alter the denominator to capture more risk.

Why the RBI is acting now

The move comes amid growing concerns about build-up of leverage in the banking system, particularly through unsecured lending and derivatives. Senior RBI officials have repeatedly flagged the need for tighter guardrails as credit growth outpaces deposit growth, a trend that has been visible over the past year.

According to the draft, the proposed changes would align Indian norms with global standards set by the Basel Committee on Banking Supervision, which has been pushing for higher leverage buffers since the 2008 financial crisis. The RBI's consultation paper suggests that the current framework may not adequately capture risks from off-balance-sheet exposures, such as letters of credit and guarantees, which have grown rapidly.

What the new norms could mean for banks

If implemented, the tighter norms could force banks to raise additional capital or trim their balance sheets, potentially impacting lending growth in the short term. Public sector banks, which already operate with thin capital buffers, are likely to feel the most pressure, while better-capitalised private lenders may find it easier to comply.

Banking analysts say the proposals are prudent but could create headwinds for credit off-take, especially in segments like retail and micro, small and medium enterprises (MSMEs), which are more reliant on bank funding. However, the RBI has argued that a stronger leverage framework would enhance the system's ability to absorb shocks, thereby supporting sustainable growth over the long run.

Industry response and next steps

The RBI has invited comments from stakeholders, including banks, financial institutions and the public, within a specified period. Industry bodies are expected to submit their feedback, with some likely to seek clarifications on the treatment of specific instruments and transitional provisions.

Bankers have expressed concern about the potential cost of compliance, but many acknowledge the need for a more robust framework in light of recent global banking stress. The central bank has not yet announced a timeline for finalisation of the rules, but it is expected to review the feedback and issue a final circular in the coming months.

Observers will be watching whether the RBI opts for a phased implementation to ease the transition for smaller lenders. The final shape of the norms, including any adjustments to the proposed leverage thresholds, will depend on the feedback received and the evolving macroeconomic conditions.
