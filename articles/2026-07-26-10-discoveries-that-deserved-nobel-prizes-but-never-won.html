10 Discoveries That Deserved Nobel Prizes but Never Won

📅 2026-07-26 📂 Inventions Original source ↗
Key points
  • Alfred Nobel's will set strict criteria for prizes, limiting recognition.
  • Discoveries like plate tectonics and cosmic microwave background were ignored.
  • The list highlights flaws in the Nobel selection process.

Overlooked Genius: The Discoveries That Should Have Won

Every year, the Nobel Prizes celebrate the brightest minds in science. But for every winner, there are discoveries that changed our world yet never received the honour. National Geographic recently listed 10 such breakthroughs that deserved a Nobel but were passed over.

The reasons vary. Some were too controversial at the time. Others were dismissed by the scientific establishment. A few were simply unlucky with the timing of nominations.

Plate Tectonics: The Theory That Shook Geology

Alfred Wegener proposed continental drift in 1912. He was ridiculed for decades. By the time his theory was proven in the 1960s, Wegener had died. The Nobel committee does not award posthumous prizes.

The discovery reshaped geology. It explained earthquakes, volcanoes, and mountain formation. Yet no Nobel was ever given for plate tectonics.

The Big Bang Evidence: Cosmic Hum Ignored

Arno Penzias and Robert Wilson accidentally discovered the cosmic microwave background radiation in 1964. It was direct proof of the Big Bang. They won a Nobel in 1978.

But the real pioneers were Ralph Alpher and Robert Herman. They predicted this radiation in 1948. The Nobel committee passed them over. Alpher and Herman never received the credit they deserved.

Rosalind Franklin's DNA Photo: The Snub That Still Stings

Rosalind Franklin's X-ray diffraction image, Photo 51, was critical to discovering DNA's double helix. Watson and Crick used it without her permission. They won the Nobel in 1962. Franklin had died of cancer four years earlier.

The Nobel does not recognise posthumous work. Many argue the committee should have made an exception. Her contribution was as vital as any of the winners'.

Ozone Layer Hole: The Warning Ignored

Mario Molina and Sherwood Rowland discovered in 1974 that chlorofluorocarbons were destroying the ozone layer. They shared a Nobel in 1995. But the discovery that convinced the world came from the British Antarctic Survey in 1985. Joe Farman, Brian Gardiner, and Jonathan Shanklin found the ozone hole.

Their work led to the Montreal Protocol, which banned CFCs. It is one of the most successful environmental treaties. Yet the three scientists never won a Nobel.

Pulsars: The 'Little Green Men' That Weren't

Jocelyn Bell Burnell discovered pulsars in 1967 while she was a PhD student. The signal was so regular her team jokingly called it LGM-1, for 'little green men'. Her supervisor Antony Hewish took the credit. He shared the Nobel in 1974. Burnell was left out.

Many physicists protested. Burnell herself was gracious, saying Nobels are not the only measure of success. But the omission remains a glaring oversight.

Chemical Bonds: The Man Who Saw Atoms

Linus Pauling won the Nobel in Chemistry in 1954 for his work on chemical bonds. But the foundation of his theory came from Gilbert N. Lewis. Lewis proposed the electron-pair bond in 1916. He never won a Nobel.

Lewis was nominated 41 times. He died in 1946, still unrecognised by Stockholm. His ideas are now taught in every chemistry class.

Penicillin: The Forgotten Woman

Alexander Fleming discovered penicillin in 1928. He shared the Nobel in 1945. But the person who made it into a usable drug was Dorothy Hodgkin. She determined penicillin's structure using X-ray crystallography. She won a Nobel in 1964.

However, the real hero of mass production was Mary Hunt. She found the mold that grew best in deep fermentation. Her contribution was never honoured by the Nobel committee.

Relativity: Einstein's Missed Chance

Albert Einstein won the Nobel in 1921 not for relativity, but for the photoelectric effect. The committee considered relativity too speculative. They avoided it.

Today, relativity is one of the pillars of physics. GPS satellites rely on it. General relativity was later confirmed, but Einstein never received a Nobel for his most famous work.

Vaccines: The Pioneer Snubbed

Edward Jenner developed the smallpox vaccine in 1796. The Nobel Prizes did not exist until 1901. He could not be considered.

But even modern vaccine pioneers have been overlooked. Jonas Salk developed the polio vaccine. He refused to patent it. He never won a Nobel. The committee gave the prize to others who worked on polio, but Salk's name was absent.

The Cosmic Ray Discovery: The Priest Who Was Too Late

Georges Lemaître was a Belgian priest and physicist. He proposed the Big Bang theory in 1927. He also discovered cosmic rays in 1932. His work was ignored for decades. By the time the Nobel committee considered him, he had died.

Lemaître's legacy is now secure. But the prize eluded him.

What This Means for Science

The Nobel Prizes are not infallible. They reflect the biases and limitations of their time. Many discoveries that seem obvious today were initially rejected.

These omissions do not diminish the work. They remind us that recognition is not the same as impact. Science progresses regardless of awards.

The Nobel committee has revised some rules over the years. Posthumous awards are still not allowed, but the limit on living recipients has been expanded. Whether future oversights will be corrected remains to be seen.
