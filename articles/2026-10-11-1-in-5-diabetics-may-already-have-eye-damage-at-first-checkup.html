1 in 5 diabetics may already have eye damage at first checkup

Early diabetic eye damage: why “no symptoms” can be misleading

Ahead of World Sight Day, Vision Eye Centre is warning that a significant share of people living with diabetes may already have eye damage detected at the first screening itself. The key message for patients and families is straightforward: diabetic eye disease can progress silently, long before pain, blurring, or obvious vision changes appear.

Diabetes affects the retina and the eye’s lens through blood vessel damage and metabolic stress. When these changes start, the eye may still look “normal” to the individual, but a proper examination can detect early retinopathy and other complications that later become harder to treat.

What the “one in five” warning means for screening strategy

When a clinic reports that roughly one in five diabetics show eye damage at first screening, it reframes how buyers and caregivers should think about eye health in diabetes—not as an occasional check, but as a planned part of chronic care. For many people, the first screening happens late, often after years of diabetes, when damage is already established.

From a practical standpoint, early findings change the follow-up schedule. A person with no visible disease may need periodic screening, while someone with early retinopathy or cataract risk factors needs closer monitoring and faster ophthalmology review.

Clinically, this aligns with risk-based care: the longer someone has diabetes and the higher their average glucose control, the greater the likelihood of retinal changes. Even then, individual risk varies—so the eye exam remains the deciding factor.

How to judge value in eye care: what to ask at the first visit

For patients considering care at a dedicated eye centre, “comprehensive” should translate into specific, repeatable steps. Ask whether the clinic performs a dilated fundus examination (rather than just a surface check), documents baseline findings, and provides a clear follow-up plan based on the results.

Where available, imaging and functional assessments can help establish a baseline and track progression. Patients should also understand how systemic diabetes control interacts with eye outcomes—eye treatment is strongest when paired with good diabetes management.

As a buyer guide for families: avoid fragmented care. If diabetic care is managed by a physician and eye care by a separate provider, ensure reports are shared so that screening intervals and risk categories don’t get lost between appointments.

  • Pros: Early detection can prevent irreversible vision loss if treatment starts before advanced retinal damage.
  • Pros: Baseline documentation helps decide follow-up frequency and treatment urgency.
  • Pros: Integrated counselling can connect eye findings to diabetes control targets.
  • Cons: Screening fatigue can lead to missed appointments—clinics should offer a practical follow-up calendar.
  • Cons: Patients may underestimate risk if they feel no symptoms; education becomes part of adherence.

Practical benchmarks for diabetic eye screening (what “regular” should look like)

There’s no one-size schedule for every patient, but the underlying benchmark for good care is consistency. Most diabetes-related eye programmes emphasize at least periodic screening, with sooner reassessment if retinopathy or cataract risk is detected.

Instead of thinking in vague terms (“every few years”), use exam results to drive the interval. If early changes appear, clinics often recommend more frequent reviews to monitor progression and detect treatable complications before they affect central vision.

For families trying to plan financially and logistically, asking for the follow-up interval in writing—along with what symptoms should trigger an earlier visit—reduces both cost surprises and avoidable delays.

World Sight Day takeaway: act on screening, not assumptions

World Sight Day is often treated as a “reminder,” but the Vision Eye Centre warning makes it a call to action. If one in five diabetics can already show eye damage at first screening, waiting for symptoms is a high-risk strategy.

The most reliable path to protecting vision is to make diabetic eye screening routine, ensure the exam is comprehensive, and follow through on risk-based follow-up. For patients, that means scheduling the first exam promptly; for families and caregivers, it means helping maintain adherence so treatment—when needed—starts while outcomes are still best.
