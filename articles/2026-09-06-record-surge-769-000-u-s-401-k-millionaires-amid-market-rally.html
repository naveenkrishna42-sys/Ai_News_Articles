Why the 401(k) Millionaire Count Is Soaring

In the first half of 2026, Fidelity reported that 769,000 U.S. retirement accounts crossed the $1 million threshold – a 12% jump from the previous year. The surge mirrors a 15% rally in the S&P 500 and a 10% gain in the Nasdaq Composite, which lifted equity‑heavy portfolios.

While the headline sounds celebratory, the underlying data reveal a nuanced picture. More workers are tapping into their accounts to cover short‑term expenses, a trend that could erode the gains if not managed prudently.

Real‑World Implications for Savvy Buyers

For high‑net‑worth individuals, the record balances open doors to premium financial products – low‑fee advisory services, tax‑efficient withdrawal strategies, and private‑market exposure. However, the rising withdrawal rate (8.3% vs 5% historically) signals a liquidity squeeze that could force premature asset sales.

Financial planners now stress a three‑bucket approach: growth, income, and cash. The growth bucket continues to benefit from market upside, while the cash bucket safeguards against unexpected expenses, reducing the temptation to raid the retirement account.

1. Benchmarking Against Historical Peaks

In 2019, the millionaire count stood at 620,000. Adjusted for inflation, today’s average balance is roughly 18% higher. Yet the real‑rate return – after accounting for inflation – hovers near 4%, underscoring the need for diversified, inflation‑protected assets.

2. Pricing Context: Fees Matter More Than Ever

Average expense ratios for 401(k) plans have slipped to 0.45%, but high‑balance accounts often pay lower fees through tiered pricing. A 0.15% fee differential on a $1.5 million balance translates to $2,250 saved annually – a non‑trivial amount over a 20‑year horizon.

3. Value Justification: Beyond the Numbers

While market gains are the headline driver, the real value lies in the compounding effect of disciplined contributions. Workers who consistently max‑out the $22,500 annual limit (plus $7,500 catch‑up for those 50+) see their balances double in roughly 9‑10 years at a 7% return.

Pros, Cons, and Key Takeaways

  • Pros: Record high balances enable access to elite advisory services; lower fees for large accounts improve net returns.
  • Cons: Rising withdrawal rates threaten long‑term growth; market‑driven gains may mask underlying contribution shortfalls.
  • Key Insight: Diversifying into cash‑flow‑focused investments (e.g., annuities, dividend stocks) can protect against premature withdrawals while preserving upside.
