Iran's 4.5-hour Konkur exam: Fail and you may be drafted into the army

📅 2026-07-17 📂 Education Original source ↗
Key points
  • The Konkur exam is a 4.5-hour university entrance test in Iran.
  • Male students who fail risk being conscripted into the military for two years.
  • The pressure has led to a booming test-prep industry and widespread anxiety.
  • Authorities have not confirmed any changes to the conscription policy.

For millions of Iranian teenagers, the Konkur exam is more than a test. It is a four-and-a-half-hour ordeal that can decide their entire future, and for male students, failure carries a particularly heavy price: a mandatory two-year stint in the military.

This high-stakes university entrance examination, held annually, has become a source of deep anxiety across the country. The pressure is not just academic; it is existential.

The exam that decides your fate

Konkur is Iran's equivalent of India's JEE or NEET, but the stakes are arguably higher. The single, gruelling test determines admission to the country's most prestigious universities. Students spend years preparing, often with private tutors and coaching centres that have sprung up as a multi-million-dollar industry.

The test itself is a marathon. Candidates answer hundreds of multiple-choice questions covering mathematics, science, literature, and languages, all within a strict time limit. Every mark counts, as competition for top universities is fierce.

The military draft: A looming threat for young men

What makes Konkur uniquely terrifying for male students is the direct link between exam results and military service. Iranian law mandates conscription for men over 18, but university admission grants a deferment. Fail the exam, and the deferment vanishes.

For many, this is not an abstract threat. Military service in Iran can be dangerous, especially for those deployed to restive border regions. The prospect of losing two years of their life — and potentially their safety — pushes students to extreme lengths.

"It's not just about getting into a good college," one student told local media. "It's about avoiding the army. The fear is real."

A booming test-prep industry

The anxiety has fuelled a massive shadow economy. Private coaching institutes, online courses, and mock test services have proliferated. Families often spend a significant portion of their income on preparation, hoping to secure their child's future.

Critics argue that the system perpetuates inequality. Wealthier families can afford high-quality coaching, giving their children a significant advantage. For poorer students, the stakes are even higher, as failure means not just lost opportunity but mandatory military service.

Authorities have not confirmed any changes to the conscription policy. The government has periodically hinted at reforms, but no concrete steps have been announced.

Mental health toll

The pressure has taken a visible toll on student mental health. Reports of anxiety, depression, and even suicide linked to exam stress have surfaced in Iranian media. Counsellors and psychologists have warned that the system creates an unhealthy environment for young people.

Despite the grim picture, some students manage to find a path forward. Those who do pass often see their lives transformed, gaining access to top-tier education and career opportunities. But for those who fail, the consequences are immediate and severe.

The Konkur exam remains a crucible for Iran's youth, with the army draft looming as a constant, punishing alternative. What happens next depends on whether Tehran moves to decouple academic performance from military service — a decision that could reshape the lives of millions.
