Anaemia and Alcohol Key Drivers of Brain Clots, ICMR Study Finds

📅 2026-07-20 📂 Health Original source ↗
Key points
  • Anaemia and alcohol are primary risk factors for brain clots, according to an ICMR study.
  • The research analyzed data from multiple Indian hospitals over a three-year period.
  • Findings may shift focus from traditional causes like infections or dehydration.

Anaemia and alcohol consumption are the leading risk factors behind cerebral venous thrombosis — a type of brain clot — a new study by the Indian Council of Medical Research has found. The findings challenge long-held assumptions about the condition, which is more common in India than in Western countries.

The study, conducted across several major hospitals in India over three years, analysed medical records of patients diagnosed with CVT. Researchers found that nearly 40% of those affected had chronic anaemia. Alcohol use was present in a similar proportion of cases. The two factors together accounted for the majority of incidents.

What Is Cerebral Venous Thrombosis?

Cerebral venous thrombosis occurs when a blood clot forms in the dural venous sinuses, which drain blood from the brain. This can lead to increased pressure inside the skull, causing headaches, seizures, and in severe cases, stroke or death.

Unlike arterial clots, CVT is relatively rare but disproportionately affects younger adults and women. Previous research had linked it to infections, oral contraceptive use, pregnancy, and dehydration. The ICMR study now adds anaemia and alcohol to that list.

Why Anaemia Matters

Anaemia reduces the blood's oxygen-carrying capacity. The body compensates by producing more red blood cells, which can thicken the blood and make clotting more likely. In patients with iron-deficiency anaemia — the most common type in India — this risk appears significantly elevated.

Alcohol, on the other hand, can cause dehydration and affect platelet function. Chronic drinking may also lead to liver damage, which disrupts the production of clotting factors. The combination of both risk factors creates a particularly dangerous situation.

Implications for Diagnosis and Prevention

The study's authors recommend that doctors routinely screen for anaemia and alcohol use in patients presenting with unexplained headaches or neurological symptoms. Early detection could prevent clots from forming.

Public health officials may also need to revisit awareness campaigns. While stroke prevention typically focuses on hypertension, diabetes, and smoking, the ICMR findings suggest that addressing anaemia and alcohol use could reduce the burden of CVT in India.

Limitations and Next Steps

The study relied on hospital-based data, which may not capture milder cases that go undiagnosed. Researchers have not yet determined whether treating anaemia or reducing alcohol intake lowers CVT risk — they plan to investigate this in a follow-up trial.

For now, the message is clear: anaemia and alcohol are not just background factors; they are major drivers of a serious neurological condition. Indian clinicians and policymakers should take note.

The ICMR is expected to release detailed guidelines for CVT prevention later this year. Until then, doctors are advised to maintain a high index of suspicion in anaemic patients or those with a history of heavy drinking.
