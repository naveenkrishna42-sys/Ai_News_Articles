Ebola Outbreak in Eastern DR Congo Hits Women and Children Hardest: UN

📅 2026-08-08 📂 Health Original source ↗
Key points
  • UN says women and children account for the majority of Ebola cases and deaths in eastern DR Congo.
  • The outbreak has surpassed 4,000 cases, with child deaths exceeding 300, according to UNICEF.
  • Health workers threaten to abandon response due to unpaid wages, as Africa CDC and WHO call for urgent community-led action.

The ongoing Ebola outbreak in eastern Democratic Republic of Congo (DRC) is taking a disproportionate toll on women and children, the United Nations has reported. The revelation comes as the outbreak's case count has now surpassed 4,000, with the Africa Centres for Disease Control and Prevention (Africa CDC) and the World Health Organization (WHO) jointly calling for urgent, community-led action to contain the spread.

Women and children bear the brunt

According to UN agencies, the demographic profile of those affected reveals a troubling pattern. Women, often primary caregivers in households, are repeatedly exposed to the virus while nursing sick family members. Children, meanwhile, face heightened vulnerability due to weaker immune systems and the challenges of implementing strict infection control in densely populated communities.

UNICEF has confirmed that child deaths from Ebola have now passed 300. The agency also warns that the outbreak is undermining essential health services across the region, with routine vaccinations and maternal care being disrupted as healthcare facilities focus on the emergency response.

Mounting pressure on health workers

Behind the grim statistics lies a deeper crisis: the health workers on the frontlines are struggling. Reports indicate that many have not received their wages, and they are now threatening to abandon the Ebola response altogether. The combination of unpaid salaries and the lethal risk they face daily has created a volatile situation that could unravel containment efforts.

This threat comes at a critical juncture. The outbreak, which began over a year ago, has proven stubbornly persistent. While previous Ebola outbreaks in the DRC were contained relatively quickly, this one has spread across a region marked by conflict and population displacement, making it harder to trace contacts and isolate cases.

Call for community-led action

Africa CDC and WHO have shifted their strategy, emphasising the need for community-led interventions rather than top-down approaches. Trust, they argue, is the missing ingredient. In many affected areas, residents remain sceptical of health workers and government officials, a legacy of decades of instability and misinformation.

Community engagement, including the involvement of local leaders and traditional healers, is now seen as essential. The agencies have urged international donors to step up funding not just for medical supplies but for grassroots education and support programmes.

What happens next

The coming weeks will test the effectiveness of this new approach. With case numbers still climbing and health workers on the verge of walking out, the window to act is narrowing. The UN and its partners are racing to resolve the wage disputes while scaling up community outreach, but the clock is ticking.

As the world watches, the focus will be on whether the DRC, with international support, can finally turn the tide on this outbreak. The stakes could not be higher, especially for the women and children who continue to bear the heaviest burden.
