RBI bars banks, NBFCs from selling stressed assets back to defaulting borrowers

📅 2026-07-16 📂 Banking Original source ↗
Key points
  • RBI prohibits sale of stressed assets back to defaulting borrowers or their related parties.
  • Move aims to prevent defaulters from regaining control of assets at discounted prices.
  • Banks and NBFCs must now find genuine third-party buyers for such assets.

The Reserve Bank of India (RBI) has tightened the rules around stressed asset sales. In a circular issued on Thursday, the central bank barred banks and non-banking financial companies (NBFCs) from selling acquired stressed assets back to the original defaulting borrowers or their related parties.

The move is aimed at preventing a common practice where defaulters would buy back their own assets at a discount through shell companies or relatives. This often allowed them to retain control while escaping full repayment.

What the new rule means

Under the updated guidelines, lenders cannot sell a stressed asset—such as a defaulted loan or repossessed property—to the borrower who originally defaulted. The ban also covers any entity connected to the borrower, including family members, business partners, or firms where the defaulter holds a stake.

Banks and NBFCs must now ensure that the buyer is an unrelated third party. The RBI has directed lenders to put in place robust due diligence processes to verify the identity and independence of the buyer before completing any sale.

Why the RBI stepped in

The central bank has been concerned about the misuse of asset reconstruction and recovery mechanisms. In many cases, defaulters used the sale process to regain assets at a fraction of the original loan value, undermining the purpose of recovery.

This practice also hurt other creditors and distorted the market for stressed assets. The RBI's new rule seeks to close this loophole and ensure that asset sales lead to genuine change in ownership.

Officials familiar with the matter said the circular is part of a broader effort to clean up the banking system. The RBI has been pushing for faster resolution of bad loans and stricter accountability for defaulters.

Impact on banks and NBFCs

For lenders, the rule means additional compliance burdens. Banks will now have to conduct more thorough background checks on buyers. They may also find it harder to sell certain assets quickly, especially those with limited market interest.

However, the long-term benefit could be higher recovery rates. By forcing sales to genuine buyers, the RBI expects assets to fetch better prices. This could improve the balance sheets of banks and NBFCs, which have been struggling with high levels of non-performing assets (NPAs).

The circular applies to all scheduled commercial banks, including regional rural banks, as well as all NBFCs. Lenders have been given immediate effect.

What happens next

The RBI has asked banks and NBFCs to review their existing asset sale agreements and ensure compliance with the new rule. Any ongoing sale processes that involve defaulting borrowers must be halted.

The central bank is expected to monitor the implementation closely. Industry experts say this could lead to a surge in demand for stressed assets from genuine investors and asset reconstruction companies.

Lenders are now scrambling to update their internal policies. The coming months will show how effectively the new norm curbs the practice of defaulters buying back their own debts.
