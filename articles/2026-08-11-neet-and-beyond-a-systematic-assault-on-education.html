NEET and beyond: A systematic assault on education

📅 2026-08-11 📂 Education Original source ↗
Key points
  • The NEET paper leak is part of a wider pattern of systemic failures in education governance.
  • Experts warn that privatisation and profit motives are undermining the integrity of exams.
  • The article calls for comprehensive reform beyond immediate fixes to restore trust in the system.

The National Eligibility cum Entrance Test (NEET) has become the flashpoint of a much deeper crisis. What began as a single paper leak has exposed fractures running through the entire education system. The Indian Express's analysis suggests that this is not an isolated scandal but part of a systematic assault on the very idea of fair, accessible education.

A crisis of credibility

The NEET paper leak, which came to light earlier this year, has shaken public faith in the country's most crucial medical entrance exam. Students who spent years preparing for a few hours of examination now face uncertainty. The authorities have promised investigations, but the damage to credibility runs deeper than any single inquiry can repair.

This is not merely about one bad paper or a few unscrupulous actors. The pattern repeats across state boards, competitive exams, and even school assessments. Every year, there is a new scandal, a new leak, a new promise of reform. And every year, the system lurches forward without addressing the root causes.

The anatomy of the assault

The assault on education takes many forms. There is the obvious one: the criminal networks that steal and sell question papers. But there are subtler, more corrosive forces at work. The growing commercialisation of education has turned learning into a commodity, with coaching factories churning out rote memorisers rather than thinkers.

Regulatory bodies, meant to safeguard standards, have often become bottlenecks or, worse, partners in the very practices they should police. The result is a system where merit is increasingly a function of money and connections, not ability. The NEET leak is just the most visible symptom of this rot.

Privatisation without accountability

The expansion of private education has brought infrastructure and investment, but it has also created perverse incentives. Profit-seeking institutions prioritise admissions over learning outcomes. Exams become gatekeeping mechanisms, not measures of competence. When the gate itself is compromised, the entire edifice collapses.

In states where private coaching dominates, the pressure on students is immense. Families spend lakhs on tuition, often borrowing heavily. The promise of a medical seat or a government job is the only return they expect. When that promise is broken by a leak or a scandal, it is not just an academic failure; it is a betrayal of trust.

The institutional void

Public institutions, which should be the backbone of a fair system, have been systematically weakened. Underfunding, political interference, and a culture of impunity have eroded their capacity to act. The National Testing Agency (NTA), which conducts NEET, was created to centralise and professionalise exams. Instead, it has become the face of their dysfunction.

Reform cannot stop at arresting a few middlemen or re-conducting a single exam. It requires rebuilding the institutions that are meant to protect the integrity of education. This means transparent recruitment, robust oversight, and a clear separation between the political executive and examination bodies.

What the students are saying

Student protests have erupted in several cities, and their demands are not just about re-exams or compensation. They are asking for a system that does not treat them as pawns in a larger game. Their anger is directed at the casualisation of their futures, the sense that their hard work can be nullified by forces they cannot control.

The government's response so far has been reactive. Committees have been formed, FIRs filed, and promises made. But students have heard this before. What they want is a guarantee that the next exam will be fair, and the one after that. That requires a structural commitment, not a press conference.

Beyond NEET

The NEET controversy is a mirror. It reflects a broader malaise that affects school education, higher studies, and even professional certifications. If the system continues to treat symptoms rather than causes, the next scandal is only a matter of time.

Some experts argue for a complete overhaul: a single, digitised examination framework with tamper-proof protocols, backed by severe penalties for malpractice. Others call for a return to school-based assessments, reducing the stakes of a single exam. Both approaches have merit, but neither can work without political will.

The coming months will show whether the authorities are serious about reform or merely managing a crisis. The re-conduction of NEET is a test, but the real examination is of the system's ability to change. For millions of students, the clock is ticking.
