DESI unveils largest 2D map of universe with 5.6 trillion pixels

📅 2026-08-11 📂 Science Original source ↗
Key points
  • The Dark Energy Spectroscopic Instrument (DESI) has produced the largest 2D map of the universe.
  • The map contains 5.6 trillion pixels and nearly 4 billion cosmic objects.
  • The map is publicly accessible, allowing anyone to explore the cosmos.
  • This survey helps study the accelerating expansion of the universe.

A Cosmic Cartography Milestone

Scientists have unveiled the largest two-dimensional map of the universe ever created, a colossal mosaic comprising 5.6 trillion pixels and capturing nearly 4 billion cosmic objects. The map was produced by the Dark Energy Spectroscopic Instrument (DESI), a powerful survey project designed to probe the universe's expansion history.

This new atlas covers a substantial portion of the sky, offering an unprecedented view of galaxies, quasars, and other celestial phenomena. The sheer scale of the dataset marks a significant leap forward in observational astronomy.

What the Map Reveals

The map is not just a static image; it is a treasure trove of data that allows astronomers to trace the large-scale structure of the universe. By cataloguing the positions of billions of objects, researchers can study how galaxies cluster together, revealing the underlying cosmic web of dark matter.

One of the primary goals of the DESI survey is to better understand dark energy, the mysterious force driving the accelerating expansion of the universe. The new map provides a crucial baseline for measuring how that expansion has changed over time.

Public Access and Exploration

Unlike many scientific datasets that remain locked behind institutional walls for years, this map has been released to the public. Anyone with an internet connection can now explore the universe in remarkable detail, zooming in on distant galaxies or panning across vast cosmic voids.

This open-access approach is expected to spur a wave of citizen science and independent research. The team behind DESI hopes that making the data freely available will accelerate discoveries and foster a broader public interest in cosmology.

A Collaborative Effort

The DESI project is an international collaboration involving hundreds of researchers from institutions across the globe. The instrument itself, mounted on a telescope in Arizona, uses 5,000 robotic fibres to capture the light from individual galaxies and quasars simultaneously.

Since beginning its survey, DESI has already produced several landmark results, including detailed measurements of baryon acoustic oscillations—the fossilised ripples from the early universe. This new map builds on that work, providing a more complete picture of cosmic history.

While the map is already the largest of its kind, it is not the final word. The DESI collaboration plans to continue observing, aiming to eventually map more than 40 million galaxies and quasars over its five-year mission.

As the survey progresses, scientists expect to refine their measurements of dark energy and potentially uncover new physics beyond the standard cosmological model. The universe, it seems, is yielding its secrets one pixel at a time.
