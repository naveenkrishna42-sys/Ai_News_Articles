ICMR-NIN Study: Training Regimens Shape Athletes' Body, Metabolism, Gut Health

📅 2026-07-23 📂 Science Original source ↗
Key points
  • ICMR-NIN study compared endurance, strength, and mixed training regimens in athletes.
  • Each regimen produced distinct changes in body composition, metabolic markers, and gut bacteria.
  • Findings could lead to personalized training and nutrition plans for optimal health.

Training Type Leaves Distinct Mark on Athletes

Not all workouts are created equal—at least not when it comes to how they reshape the body from the inside out. A new study from the Indian Council of Medical Research's National Institute of Nutrition (ICMR-NIN) has found that different training regimens lead to distinct changes in athletes' body composition, metabolism, and gut health.

The research, conducted on a cohort of athletes, compared three broad categories of training: endurance-focused (like long-distance running or cycling), strength-focused (such as weightlifting or powerlifting), and a mixed regimen combining elements of both. The goal was to understand how each approach uniquely influences physiological systems.

Body Composition and Metabolic Shifts

The study observed that endurance athletes showed a significant increase in lean muscle mass alongside a reduction in body fat percentage. Their metabolic profiles indicated a higher reliance on fat as a fuel source during exercise. In contrast, strength athletes developed greater overall muscle mass and exhibited higher resting metabolic rates, meaning they burn more calories even at rest.

Athletes following a mixed regimen showed intermediate effects, balancing gains in muscle with improvements in cardiovascular efficiency. The researchers noted that these differences were not just about how the athletes looked, but how their bodies processed energy at a cellular level.

Gut Microbiome: A New Frontier

Perhaps the most striking finding involved the gut microbiome—the collection of bacteria living in the digestive tract. Endurance athletes had a significantly higher diversity of gut bacteria, which is often linked to better immune function and reduced inflammation. Strength athletes, meanwhile, showed a higher abundance of bacteria associated with protein metabolism and amino acid synthesis.

Mixed-training athletes fell somewhere in between, but still showed distinct microbial communities compared to sedentary controls. The study suggests that exercise type can directly influence which bacterial species thrive, potentially affecting nutrient absorption and recovery.

Implications for Personalized Training

The ICMR-NIN findings open the door to more personalized training and nutrition regimens. For instance, an endurance athlete might benefit from a diet rich in fiber to support their diverse gut bacteria, while a strength athlete could focus on protein intake tailored to their metabolic needs.

While the study does not prescribe specific diets, it underscores that 'one-size-fits-all' approaches to training and nutrition may be outdated. Coaches and athletes alike could use this data to fine-tune regimens for better performance and long-term health.

Experts caution that the sample size was limited and that more research is needed to confirm these patterns across different sports, ages, and genders. However, the core message is clear: how you train changes more than just your muscles.

What comes next? The ICMR-NIN team plans to expand the study to include female athletes and different age groups, aiming to build a comprehensive database that could eventually inform public health guidelines for physical activity.
