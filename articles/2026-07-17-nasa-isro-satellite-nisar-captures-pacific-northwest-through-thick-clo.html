NASA-ISRO Satellite NISAR Captures Pacific Northwest Through Thick Clouds

Radar Penetrates Cloud Cover in New NISAR Image

The NASA-ISRO Synthetic Aperture Radar (NISAR) satellite has delivered its first clear image of the Pacific Northwest region of the United States, cutting through the area's famously thick cloud cover. The image, released by NASA on Wednesday, showcases the satellite's unique ability to observe Earth regardless of weather or daylight.

NISAR, a joint mission between NASA and the Indian Space Research Organisation (ISRO), uses two radar systems — an L-band radar built by NASA and an S-band radar built by ISRO. These systems can penetrate clouds, smoke, and darkness, offering a consistent view of the planet's surface.

What the Image Shows

The captured image covers a swath of the Pacific Northwest, including parts of Washington, Oregon, and British Columbia. It reveals a detailed topography of mountains, forests, rivers, and urban areas, all visible through the region's persistent cloud cover.

Radar reflections highlight different surface types. Dense forests appear brighter, while water bodies like Puget Sound and the Columbia River show up as dark, smooth areas. Urban centers such as Seattle and Portland reflect radar signals strongly, appearing as bright patches on the image.

This level of detail is significant because the Pacific Northwest is often shrouded in clouds, making optical satellite imaging difficult. NISAR can monitor changes in forests, water levels, and urban expansion without waiting for clear skies.

Why NISAR's All-Weather Capability Matters

NISAR's ability to see through clouds is a major advancement for Earth observation. Many regions of the world, including large parts of India, Southeast Asia, and the Amazon, are frequently covered by clouds. Optical satellites can miss critical events like floods, landslides, or deforestation during such conditions.

"NISAR will provide consistent, reliable data on Earth's surface changes, even in the most cloud-prone areas," a NASA official said. The satellite's radar can also measure subtle movements in the ground, making it useful for monitoring earthquakes, volcanic activity, and groundwater changes.

Joint Mission, Global Benefits

The NISAR mission, launched in early 2025, is designed to observe Earth's land and ice surfaces every 12 days. It will collect data on ecosystems, ice sheet dynamics, and natural hazards. The satellite's global coverage includes India, where ISRO will use the data for agricultural monitoring, disaster management, and urban planning.

For India, NISAR's cloud-penetrating radar is particularly valuable during the monsoon season when optical satellites struggle to get clear images. The Indian Space Research Organisation has already begun calibrating the satellite's instruments over test sites in the country.

Officials have not yet confirmed the exact date of NISAR's full operational phase, but early images like this one demonstrate the satellite's readiness. The mission is expected to operate for at least three years, providing a continuous stream of data for scientists and policymakers.

In the coming months, NASA and ISRO plan to release more images from different parts of the world, including India. The focus will shift to observing seasonal changes, tracking deforestation, and monitoring glacial movements in the Himalayas.
