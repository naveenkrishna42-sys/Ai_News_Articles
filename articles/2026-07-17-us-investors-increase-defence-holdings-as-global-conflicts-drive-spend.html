US investors increase defence holdings as global conflicts drive spending

📅 2026-07-17 📂 Wars & Conflicts Original source ↗
Key points
  • US investors are increasing allocations to defence and aerospace stocks amid global conflicts.
  • Ongoing wars in Ukraine and the Middle East have driven up military spending worldwide.
  • Major defence contractors have reported higher order backlogs and revenue growth.
  • Analysts expect defence budgets to remain elevated for the foreseeable future.
  • The shift marks a significant change in investor sentiment towards the sector.

US investors are quietly but steadily expanding their holdings in defence and aerospace stocks, betting that the world's ongoing wars will keep military budgets high for years. The trend has accelerated as conflicts in Ukraine and the Middle East show no signs of abating.

Major fund managers and institutional investors have raised their allocation to the sector by as much as 15 percent over the past year, according to filings and market data. The move reflects a growing conviction that global defence spending has entered a new, sustained phase of growth.

War drives demand

The wars in Ukraine and Gaza have pushed countries from Europe to Asia to increase their defence budgets. NATO members have pledged to spend at least 2 percent of GDP on defence, a target many are now exceeding. India, Japan, and Australia have also announced significant hikes in military spending.

For US defence contractors, this has translated into a surge in orders. Lockheed Martin, Raytheon, and Northrop Grumman have all reported record backlogs, with demand for missiles, drones, and fighter jets outstripping production capacity. Investors are betting that this demand will remain strong even after the current conflicts end.

Investor sentiment shifts

For years, defence stocks were considered a niche play, often shunned by ESG-focused funds. That is changing. The sector has outperformed the broader market for three consecutive quarters, and inflows into defence-focused exchange-traded funds have hit multi-year highs.

“The narrative has shifted,” said one portfolio manager at a large US asset manager. “Investors no longer see defence as a temporary play tied to a single conflict. They see it as a structural growth story tied to geopolitical rivalry.” The manager spoke on condition of anonymity as his firm does not allow public comments on portfolio strategy.

Rising budgets, rising profits

The numbers back up the optimism. Global military expenditure crossed $2.4 trillion in 2025, a record high. The US alone accounts for nearly 40 percent of that total, and the Biden administration has proposed a defence budget of $886 billion for 2026, a 3 percent increase after adjusting for inflation.

For companies like General Dynamics and L3Harris, that means steady revenue streams from long-term contracts. Margins have also improved as supply chain disruptions ease and production volumes rise. Analysts expect earnings for the sector to grow by 12 to 15 percent annually over the next three years.

Risks remain

Not everyone is convinced the boom is sustainable. Critics point to the possibility of peace deals in Ukraine or the Middle East that could reduce demand. Others warn that the sector's valuations are stretched, with some stocks trading at over 25 times forward earnings.

Political risk also looms. A future US administration could push for defence spending cuts, especially if the federal deficit becomes a bigger concern. And export controls on sensitive technologies could limit growth for companies that rely on international sales.

Still, for now, the money keeps flowing. The Pentagon's push for next-generation systems — including hypersonic weapons, space-based sensors, and AI-driven combat platforms — ensures that defence contractors will have work for years to come.

Investors will watch the upcoming US budget negotiations closely. Any sign of bipartisan support for higher defence spending could trigger another wave of buying. For now, the sector remains a favourite among those who believe the world is entering a prolonged era of military competition.
