Danuri Orbiter Captures First Images of SpaceX Rocket Crash Site on Moon

📅 2026-08-09 📂 Breaking News Original source ↗
Key points
  • Danuri orbiter has captured before and after images of the lunar surface showing a new crater.
  • The crater is believed to have been caused by a SpaceX rocket stage that crashed into the Moon.
  • NASA plans to observe the impact site to study the effects of space debris on the lunar surface.

First Glimpse of the Crash Site

South Korea's Danuri orbiter has captured the first images of the lunar crater formed by a SpaceX rocket stage that crashed into the Moon. The images, released by the Korea Aerospace Research Institute (KARI), show a distinct new crater on the lunar surface, confirming the impact that scientists had predicted earlier this year.

The orbiter, which has been circling the Moon since December 2022, used its high-resolution cameras to photograph the area before and after the impact. The stark difference between the two sets of images leaves little room for doubt: a new crater has been carved into the Moon's rugged terrain.

Tracking the Wayward Rocket

The rocket stage in question was part of a SpaceX Falcon 9 launch, which had been drifting in space for years before its trajectory brought it into the Moon's path. While SpaceX's Falcon 9 rockets are not the first to crash into the Moon, this incident has drawn significant attention due to the growing concern over space debris.

Scientists had been tracking the object's course for weeks, with some initially speculating that it might belong to China's Chang'e 5-T1 mission. However, later analysis confirmed it was a SpaceX rocket component. The impact itself was not directly observed at the moment it happened, but the aftermath is now visible in Danuri's images.

NASA's Follow-Up Observations

NASA has announced that it will attempt to observe the impact site using its Lunar Reconnaissance Orbiter (LRO). The agency's scientists are keen to study the crater in detail, as it offers a rare opportunity to understand how man-made objects interact with the lunar surface.

By comparing the crater's size, shape, and ejecta pattern with those of natural meteorite impacts, researchers hope to refine their models of lunar geology. This data could also prove valuable for future missions that might need to avoid or utilise such debris fields.

A Growing Problem in Space

Space debris has become a pressing issue for space agencies and private companies alike. The Moon, though far less crowded than Earth's orbit, is not immune to the problem. As more nations and companies send missions to the lunar surface, the risk of accidental impacts increases.

Scientists have warned that such debris could contaminate scientifically valuable sites or pose hazards to future landers. The Danuri images serve as a stark reminder that even a seemingly empty void like the Moon is now touched by human activity.

What Lies Ahead

The international scientific community is now focused on analysing the Danuri data. KARI has said it will continue to monitor the site, while NASA's LRO is expected to provide higher-resolution images in the coming weeks. These observations could help answer lingering questions about the exact nature of the impact and its implications.

For now, the images stand as a testament to the reach of human technology—and its unintended consequences. As lunar missions multiply, the need for clearer guidelines on space debris management will only grow.
