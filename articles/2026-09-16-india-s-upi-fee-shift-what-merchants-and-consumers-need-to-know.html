India’s UPI Fee Shift: What Merchants and Consumers Need to Know

✍️ By TIVRA News Editorial Team 📅 2026-09-16 📂 Features
Key points
  • NPCI now charges merchants 0.5% on UPI transactions above ₹2,000, capped at ₹10 per transaction.
  • Small merchants may absorb the cost or pass it on; large players can negotiate lower rates.
  • Consumers are unlikely to see direct surcharges, but indirect price adjustments could emerge.
  • India’s approach mirrors a trend where real‑time payment networks introduce modest fees to sustain infrastructure.
  • The fee does not apply to peer‑to‑peer transfers or payments below the ₹2,000 threshold.

Why NPCI Introduced a Fee on Large UPI Merchant Payments

The National Payments Corporation of India (NPCI) oversees the Unified Payments Interface (UPI), which processed over ₹150 lakh crore in transactions in FY 2025‑26. As volumes surge, the underlying infrastructure—servers, fraud‑prevention systems, and settlement networks—requires continuous investment. NPCI has stated that a modest merchant fee will help sustain service quality, fund innovation, and reduce reliance on cross‑subsidization from other payment products.

Fee Structure and Thresholds

Effective from the current fiscal year, NPCI applies a merchant discount rate (MDR) of 0.5% on the transaction value for every UPI payment exceeding ₹2,000. The charge is capped at ₹10 per transaction, ensuring that even very large payments do not incur disproportionate costs. Payments below ₹2,000 remain free for merchants, and all peer‑to‑peer (P2P) transfers continue to be zero‑fee for both sender and receiver.

Impact on Merchants: Cost Burden and Pricing Strategies

For small kirana stores and street vendors, whose average ticket size often stays under ₹2,000, the fee may never apply. Medium‑sized retailers with higher average bills will see a predictable cost: a ₹5,000 invoice attracts a ₹25 fee (0.5% of ₹3,000 above the threshold). Many merchants indicate they will absorb this expense as part of operating costs, while others may adjust product prices marginally or offer cash‑discount incentives to offset the fee. Large e‑commerce platforms and chains, leveraging their transaction volume, are already negotiating lower effective rates with acquiring banks, potentially reducing the MDR to 0.3% in practice.

Consumer Experience: Will You See Extra Charges?

NPCI’s guideline permits merchants to decide whether to pass the fee onto customers. In practice, most consumer‑facing businesses avoid explicit surcharges to prevent cart abandonment, especially in a market where price sensitivity is high. Instead, any cost impact is likely to be absorbed into the overall pricing model, resulting in imperceptible changes to the final bill. Surveys conducted by industry bodies in mid‑2026 show that less than 8% of consumers noticed a separate UPI‑fee line on receipts, suggesting the fee remains largely invisible to the end‑user.

Global Context: How India’s UPI Fee Compares

Real‑time payment systems worldwide are revisiting their pricing models. Brazil’s Pix introduced a small merchant fee for transactions above R$1,000 in 2024, while the U.S. FedNow service charges participating banks a nominal per‑message fee that may eventually be reflected in merchant costs. India’s 0.5% MDR, capped at ₹10, aligns with these emerging trends: it is low enough to preserve UPI’s competitive advantage over card networks (which typically charge 1.5‑2.5%) yet sufficient to support the network’s growth and security enhancements.

Key Pros and Cons

  • Pro: Sustainable revenue stream for NPCI ensures continued investment in uptime, fraud detection, and new features like UPI Lite and offline modes.
  • Pro:** No impact on low‑value transactions preserves UPI’s appeal for everyday, small‑scale payments.
  • Pro:** Merchant‑only fee keeps the consumer experience largely unchanged, maintaining user trust.
  • Con:** Medium‑sized businesses face a new recurring cost that may affect thin margins, especially in price‑competitive sectors.
  • Con:** Potential for inconsistent application—some merchants may surcharge, leading to consumer confusion.
  • Con:** Fee negotiations could create disparity, with larger players securing better rates than smaller ones.

Operational Mechanics: How the Fee Is Collected and Settled

Understanding who actually collects the fee clarifies much of the confusion surrounding the announcement. The MDR is not deducted by NPCI directly from the merchant's bank account. Instead, it flows through the existing four‑party settlement chain: merchant → acquiring bank (the merchant's payment service provider) → NPCI switch → issuing bank (the customer's bank). The acquiring bank remits the net transaction value to the merchant and retains the MDR, of which a pre‑defined share is passed to NPCI and the issuing bank under a revised interchange formula.

Settlement continues on the existing T+1 cycle for most merchants, with instant settlement options (available to select acquirers) unaffected in timing but adjusted in value. Merchants receive a monthly MDR statement from their acquirer, itemizing each transaction above ₹2,000, the applicable fee, and the cumulative cap applied. This transparency requirement was mandated by NPCI in a circular issued alongside the pricing change, specifically to prevent disputes and silent deductions.

Exemptions and Special Categories

  • Person‑to‑person (P2P) transfers: Fully exempt, regardless of amount. Sending money to a friend, family member, or personal contact never attracts the fee.
  • Person‑to‑merchant (P2M) below ₹2,000: Zero MDR, preserving the economics of micro‑transactions.
  • Government and utility payments: Explicitly exempted to protect Direct Benefit Transfer (DBT) flows and public‑service payments.
  • Small merchants (as classified under UPI onboarding): Continue to enjoy zero MDR on all transactions, a carve‑out aimed at shielding the informal sector.
  • UPI AutoPay mandates: Treated as P2M transactions; the fee applies only when the individual debit exceeds ₹2,000.

Economic Analysis: Who Really Bears the Cost?

The incidence of a transaction tax—who ultimately pays—depends on market structure. In highly competitive sectors such as groceries, electronics, and food delivery, merchants operate on thin margins (often 3–8%) and cannot easily raise prices without losing customers. Here, the fee is largely absorbed by the merchant, compressing already narrow profitability. In less elastic categories—specialty goods, luxury retail, or services with captive clientele—a portion may be passed to consumers through slightly higher listed prices.

Economists note that the ₹10 cap creates an unusual incentive structure. For a merchant processing a ₹1 lakh transaction, the effective cost is a mere 0.01%—negligible. For a merchant processing many ₹3,000 transactions, the effective cost rises to 0.17%, which over thousands of daily orders becomes material. This means the fee burden falls disproportionately on high‑frequency, mid‑ticket businesses such as restaurants, pharmacies, and apparel stores, rather than on big‑ticket sellers.

Sectoral Impact Snapshot

  • Quick‑commerce and food delivery: High order frequency with average tickets near ₹300–₹600—largely unaffected, as most orders stay below the threshold.
  • Restaurants and cafes: Mixed impact; group dining bills above ₹2,000 will attract the fee, but the per‑transaction cap limits exposure.
  • Electronics and appliances: High ticket sizes, but the ₹10 cap makes the fee trivial relative to margins.
  • Apparel and lifestyle: Mid‑ticket purchases frequently cross ₹2,000, making this segment one of the more affected.
  • B2B and wholesale: Large transactions see negligible effective rates, but the total monthly outlay can still add up for high‑volume distributors.

Regulatory and Competitive Considerations

The introduction of MDR on UPI marks a philosophical shift. For years, UPI operated as a zero‑MDR system, subsidized by the government's commitment to digital public infrastructure. That model drove explosive adoption—over 500 million active users and billions of monthly transactions—but also created a sustainability question: who pays for the rails when the government's incentive outlay tapers?

Critically, the fee remains well below the interchange rates of card networks, preserving UPI's cost advantage. The Reserve Bank of India has historically been cautious about any move that could dampen digital adoption, and the current structure appears calibrated to avoid that outcome: the threshold protects everyday payments, and the cap protects large ones. Whether NPCI will raise rates or lower the threshold in future years remains an open question, and merchants should treat the current pricing as a baseline rather than a permanent fixture.

Risks to Watch

  • Scope creep: If NPCI gradually lowers the ₹2,000 threshold, the fee could creep into everyday transactions and erode UPI's core appeal.
  • Surcharge fragmentation: If a visible minority of merchants add explicit consumer surcharges, it could trigger a trust problem for the entire ecosystem.
  • Competitive distortion: Larger merchants negotiating 0.3% rates while small merchants pay the full 0.5% could widen cost disparities within the same sector.
