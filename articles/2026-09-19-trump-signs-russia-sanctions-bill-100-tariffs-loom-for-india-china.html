Trump Signs Russia Sanctions Bill: A New Trade Flashpoint

US President Donald Trump has signed into law a bill that allows for tariffs of up to 100% on imports from any country purchasing Russian crude oil. The legislation, passed with bipartisan support, primarily targets India and China, the two largest buyers of Russian oil since the 2022 invasion of Ukraine. The move escalates economic pressure on Moscow but creates fresh trade tensions with New Delhi and Beijing.

India, which has ramped up Russian oil imports to over 1.5 million barrels per day, now faces a stark choice: reduce energy purchases or risk punitive tariffs on its exports to the US. The law does not immediately impose tariffs; it grants the President authority to do so, leaving markets guessing about timing and scope.

What the Law Actually Does

The bill amends existing sanctions frameworks to include secondary sanctions on financial institutions and entities facilitating Russian oil trade. Crucially, it authorises the President to levy tariffs on goods from countries that continue buying Russian crude. The tariffs can go up to 100% of the value of affected imports, effectively pricing many products out of the US market.

Implementation details remain unclear. The White House has indicated it will use the authority "strategically," but no timeline has been announced. For Indian exporters, this ambiguity is as damaging as the tariffs themselves, as buyers delay orders amid uncertainty.

Impact on India: Trade and Energy

India's trade with the US totalled over $130 billion in 2025, with a trade surplus of about $45 billion. Key exports at risk include pharmaceuticals, textiles, gems and jewellery, and auto components. If 100% tariffs are applied, Indian exporters could lose $10-15 billion (₹83,000-1.25 lakh crore) annually, according to industry estimates.

On the energy front, India has defended its Russian oil purchases as necessary for energy security and affordability. Russian crude is typically $15-20 per barrel cheaper than Brent, saving India billions of dollars annually. Replacing those barrels would increase the import bill and pressure the rupee.

Key Real-World Highlights

  • Immediate uncertainty: No tariffs imposed yet, but the threat alone is disrupting export orders and investment decisions.
  • Energy security vs. trade access: India must weigh cheap Russian oil against access to the US market, its largest export destination.
  • Diplomatic balancing act: New Delhi reaffirms "mutually beneficial" ties but has not committed to reducing Russian oil purchases.
  • Global spillover: China, the other major target, may retaliate, further fragmenting global trade.

What Buyers and Exporters Should Watch

For Indian exporters, the priority is diversifying markets and building resilience. Sectors with high US exposure should assess tariff scenarios and explore alternative buyers in the EU, Middle East, and Southeast Asia. Importers of Indian goods in the US may seek exemptions for critical products, but the political climate favours tough action.

Energy traders should monitor Russian crude flows and Urals pricing. Any reduction in Indian purchases would tighten global supply and potentially raise prices for other buyers. The rupee may face depreciation pressure if export earnings fall.

Ultimately, the law is a tool, not a verdict. Its impact depends on how aggressively the Trump administration wields it. India's response, whether accommodation or defiance, will shape not just bilateral ties but the broader architecture of global trade and sanctions.
