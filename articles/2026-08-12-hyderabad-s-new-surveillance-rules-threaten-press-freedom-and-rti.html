Hyderabad’s New Surveillance Rules Threaten Press Freedom and RTI

📅 2026-08-12 📂 Hyderabad Original source ↗
Key points
  • The Hans India flags new surveillance measures in Hyderabad as a threat to Article 19(1)(a).
  • The measures could impact RTI activists and journalists in Telangana.
  • Authorities have not yet clarified the scope of the new rules.

Journalists and right-to-information activists in Hyderabad are on edge after the state government introduced new surveillance measures that legal experts say could seriously undermine constitutional guarantees. The Hans India, in its report on Monday, described the development as a direct threat to Article 19(1)(a) of the Constitution, which protects the freedom of speech and expression.

The provisions, which have not been made fully public, are believed to give authorities wider powers to monitor digital communications, including emails, messaging apps, and voice calls. While the government has framed the move as a step to strengthen national security, critics argue that the lack of transparency and judicial oversight could lead to misuse against journalists, whistleblowers, and activists.

What the Rules Envisage

According to the report, the new surveillance framework does not require the same level of prior approval as existing laws. This means that requests to intercept communications may be processed faster, with less scrutiny. Legal experts point out that while surveillance is permitted under certain circumstances, any dilution of procedural safeguards could tip the balance between security and civil liberties.

The Hans India’s report specifically flags the impact on RTI applicants who routinely seek information from government departments. If their communications are monitored, sources within the bureaucracy may become reluctant to share documents, defeating the very purpose of the RTI Act, 2005.

Chilling Effect on Journalism

Journalists in Telangana say that the new measures could have a chilling effect on reporting. Investigative stories that depend on confidential sources are likely to suffer if reporters cannot guarantee anonymity. A senior journalist, speaking on condition of anonymity, said that the move comes at a time when media houses are already facing pressure from multiple quarters.

The report also notes that the surveillance rules could be used to track down the sources of leaks, which would make it harder for the press to act as a public watchdog. In a democracy, the freedom of the press is not a privilege but a necessity, and any erosion of that freedom must be met with public scrutiny.

Legal and Constitutional Questions

Article 19(1)(a) is not an absolute right. It can be restricted under Article 19(2) for reasons of sovereignty, integrity, security, public order, decency, or morality. However, any restriction must be reasonable and must not be arbitrary. Legal experts argue that the new measures, as reported, may not meet the test of reasonableness because they lack clear guidelines on who can be monitored and for how long.

The Supreme Court, in the landmark K.S. Puttaswamy case, held that the right to privacy is a fundamental right. Surveillance that is not backed by a valid law and procedural safeguards violates this right. The Hans India’s report suggests that the new rules may be in tension with that judgment.

No Official Clarification Yet

Authorities in Hyderabad have not yet issued a public statement explaining the scope of the new measures. Officials have declined to comment on the record, citing operational exigencies. This silence has only deepened concerns among civil liberties groups, who are planning to file a writ petition in the Telangana High Court.

In the coming weeks, the debate is likely to shift to the judiciary and the state legislature. Activists are demanding that the government release the full text of the rules and allow for a public consultation. Until then, the question remains: how far can the state go in the name of security without eroding the very freedoms it is meant to protect?
