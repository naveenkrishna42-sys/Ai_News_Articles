Waltz Defends Potential Law of War Violations in Iran Conflict

📅 2026-07-19 📂 Wars & Conflicts Original source ↗
Key points
  • Mike Waltz suggested certain laws of war are outdated for current threats.
  • Critics argue his stance could set a dangerous precedent for international norms.
  • The debate highlights tensions between military necessity and legal constraints.

US National Security Advisor Mike Waltz has sparked a fierce debate by defending potential violations of the laws of war in the ongoing conflict with Iran. In remarks reported by Just Security, Waltz argued that some international legal frameworks are outdated for modern warfare.

His comments come as the US military campaign against Iran enters its third week. Waltz did not specify which laws he considers obsolete but suggested that strict adherence to certain rules could hamper effective operations against a determined adversary.

Waltz's Argument for Flexibility

Speaking at a defence forum in Washington, Waltz said the nature of the threat from Iran demands a reassessment of long-standing legal constraints. He pointed to Iran's use of civilian infrastructure for military purposes as a challenge that traditional laws of war did not anticipate.

“We are dealing with an enemy that hides behind civilians and uses hospitals as command centres,” Waltz stated. “The old rulebook was not written for this reality.” He stopped short of endorsing blanket disregard for international law but called for a pragmatic approach.

Legal Experts Push Back

International law scholars have reacted with alarm. They argue that any suggestion of discarding laws of war, even selectively, could unravel decades of legal protections for civilians. The Geneva Conventions, they stress, are not optional.

“This is a slippery slope,” said a professor of international law at Georgetown University. “Once you start carving out exceptions for one conflict, you create precedents that others will exploit.” The professor spoke on condition of anonymity given the sensitivity of the topic.

Human rights groups have also weighed in. Amnesty International called Waltz's remarks deeply concerning, warning that they could embolden other nations to ignore their legal obligations. The organisation urged the US to reaffirm its commitment to international humanitarian law.

Context of the Iran Conflict

The US launched military strikes against Iranian nuclear and military facilities after a series of provocations in the Persian Gulf. The operation has involved precision bombing campaigns and cyberattacks. Iranian officials have reported significant damage to their infrastructure.

Casualty figures remain disputed. Iran claims hundreds of civilians have been killed, while the US military says it has taken extraordinary measures to avoid civilian harm. Independent verification is difficult as access for journalists remains restricted.

The conflict has also drawn in regional proxies. Houthi rebels in Yemen have stepped up attacks on Saudi Arabia, while Hezbollah has exchanged fire with Israeli forces along the Lebanon border. The risk of a wider war looms large.

What the Law Says

The laws of armed conflict, primarily rooted in the Geneva Conventions, require parties to distinguish between combatants and civilians. Attacks must be proportional, and indiscriminate force is prohibited. Violations can constitute war crimes.

Waltz's suggestion that these rules are outdated has no support in mainstream legal opinion. Even military commanders, who often chafe at restrictions, publicly affirm their commitment to the laws of war. The Pentagon has not formally endorsed Waltz's view.

Senate Foreign Relations Committee members have demanded clarification. Some Republican senators have backed Waltz, arguing that the US faces existential threats that demand flexibility. Democrats have been more critical, warning of diplomatic fallout.

What happens next depends on whether the White House adopts Waltz's stance as policy or treats it as a personal opinion. The administration is expected to issue a formal statement in the coming days. For now, the debate underscores the tension between military necessity and the rule of law in an era of asymmetric warfare.
