Omar Abdullah Accuses BJP of Bypassing Parliament on UCC

National Conference leader and former Jammu and Kashmir Chief Minister Omar Abdullah has launched a sharp critique of the BJP-led central government's approach to the Uniform Civil Code (UCC), alleging that the ruling party is attempting to implement it through a 'backdoor route' rather than bringing it before Parliament for thorough debate. In statements reported by The Hindu, Abdullah insisted that any move to overhaul personal laws must follow the legislative process, not executive manoeuvring or incremental state-level measures.

His remarks come amid a renewed national conversation on the UCC, with the Law Commission soliciting public feedback and several BJP-ruled states announcing committees to draft their own versions. Abdullah's core argument: if the UCC is truly a national imperative, it deserves a full parliamentary vote, not a piecemeal rollout that avoids scrutiny.

Why Allies Are Wary: JD(U) and NDA Partners Seek Time

The political fault lines are not confined to the Opposition. Key NDA ally Janata Dal (United), or JD(U), has adopted a cautious stance, stating it will decide its position only after studying the final bill. The party's public hesitation signals that even within the ruling coalition, the UCC is not a settled matter. Abdullah seized on this, questioning how the government plans to implement a uniform code if its own allies are not ready.

Union Minister K. Laxman has defended the UCC as a measure to end discrimination and not target any community. However, the Indian National Lok Dal (INL) and other regional parties have framed it as part of the RSS's 'Hindu Rashtra' agenda, deepening the polarisation around the issue.

Constitutional and Legal Dimensions

The UCC derives its constitutional basis from Article 44 of the Directive Principles of State Policy, which states that the State shall endeavour to secure a uniform civil code throughout India. However, Article 44 is non-justiciable—it cannot be enforced by courts. Successive governments have avoided a nationwide UCC, citing sensitivity and the need for consensus.

Legal experts note that personal laws are protected under Articles 25 and 26 (freedom of religion), and any uniform code must balance religious freedom with equality. The BJP's incremental strategy—starting with state-level committees—could create a patchwork of laws, complicating rather than simplifying the legal framework.

What a Uniform Civil Code Would Actually Change

At its core, the UCC proposes common civil laws for marriage, divorce, inheritance, and adoption for all citizens, regardless of religion. Currently, these matters are governed by distinct personal laws: the Hindu Marriage Act, Muslim Personal Law (Shariat) Application Act, Indian Christian Marriage Act, and others. A uniform code would eliminate these distinctions, but the specifics matter enormously.

Pros and Cons of a Uniform Civil Code

  • Pros: Promotes gender justice by ending discriminatory practices in inheritance and marriage; simplifies legal framework; upholds constitutional equality.
  • Cons: Risks religious sentiment and federal tensions; implementation without consensus may lead to social unrest; state-level rollouts could create legal inconsistencies.
  • Political Reality: Allies like JD(U) demand study time, indicating the BJP cannot rush a national bill without broader buy-in.

What This Means for Buyers and Citizens

For the average Indian citizen, the UCC debate is not abstract—it directly impacts marriage registration, divorce procedures, inheritance rights, and adoption. If a uniform code is enacted, legal fees for personal law matters could standardise, but transitional uncertainties may increase litigation. Investors and businesses should monitor state-level UCC implementations, as they could affect property inheritance and family law compliance.

Omar Abdullah's demand for a parliamentary route is more than procedural; it is a call for democratic legitimacy. Whether the BJP heeds it or continues its incremental approach will determine the UCC's final shape—and the political cost of forcing it through.
