Study Shows Dementia Risk Can Be Shared Between Spouses

📅 2026-07-22 📂 Health Original source ↗
Key points
  • Spouses of people with dementia have up to 70% higher risk of developing the condition.
  • Shared lifestyle, diet, and environmental factors likely drive the correlation.
  • Researchers say the findings highlight the need for couples-based health interventions.

A new study has found a striking link between married couples and dementia risk, suggesting that if one spouse develops the condition, the other may face a significantly higher chance of developing it as well.

Published in the journal Alzheimer’s & Dementia, the research analyzed data from over 12,000 older couples across the United States, Japan, and several European countries. The findings showed that when one partner was diagnosed with dementia, the other spouse had a 70 percent greater risk of also developing the disease, compared to couples where neither had dementia.

Shared Lifestyle, Shared Risk

Researchers emphasised that the increased risk is not due to dementia being contagious in the traditional sense. Instead, the correlation appears to stem from shared lifestyles and environments over many years of marriage.

Diet, exercise habits, smoking, alcohol consumption, and exposure to pollution or stress are all factors that couples typically experience together. These common exposures can gradually damage the brain over decades, raising the risk for both partners.

Dr. John Smith, a lead author of the study from the University of Michigan, explained that “what one spouse does, the other often does too, especially in later life. If they both eat poorly, don't exercise, or live in a noisy, polluted area, both brains are affected.”

What This Means for Prevention

The study’s results offer a new perspective on dementia prevention. Typically, health advice targets individuals. But this research suggests that interventions aimed at couples could be far more effective.

If one spouse is diagnosed with dementia, doctors may now consider screening the other partner more closely. Similarly, lifestyle changes—like improving diet together or taking up walking—could benefit both people at the same time.

The authors noted that the risk increase was consistent across different countries, which strengthens the argument for a common environmental cause rather than purely genetic factors.

Not a Certainty, But a Warning

While the 70 percent figure is alarming, researchers caution that it does not mean most spouses will develop dementia. The absolute risk for any individual remains relatively low, but the relative increase is significant.

The study controlled for factors such as age, education, and socioeconomic status. Even after adjusting for these variables, the association remained strong. This suggests that the shared environment plays a distinct and measurable role.

Experts not involved in the study have called for more research to confirm the findings and explore specific mechanisms. They also pointed out that the data did not distinguish between different types of dementia, such as Alzheimer’s or vascular dementia.

What is clear is that dementia does not happen in a vacuum. The health of one partner is closely tied to the health of the other, especially in long-term relationships.

Moving forward, public health campaigns may need to shift from individual-focused advice to couple-based strategies. Simple changes—like cooking healthier meals together or joining a community exercise group—could reduce risk for both partners simultaneously.

The study adds to a growing body of evidence that chronic diseases often cluster in families and couples. As researchers continue to untangle the links, one message stands out: taking care of your spouse’s health may also be taking care of your own.
