RBI bars banks from selling seized assets back to defaulting borrowers

📅 2026-07-20 📂 Banking Original source ↗
Key points
  • RBI prohibits sale of seized immovable assets to defaulting borrowers or related parties.
  • Move aims to prevent collusion and ensure transparent recovery process.
  • Banks must now auction assets to third parties or follow other recovery channels.

The Reserve Bank of India has barred banks from selling seized immovable assets back to the same defaulting borrowers. The circular, issued on Monday, applies to all scheduled commercial banks.

The regulator's directive is clear. Banks cannot sell a property seized under the Securitisation and Reconstruction of Financial Assets and Enforcement of Security Interest Act, 2002, to the borrower who defaulted, or to any related party. This includes the borrower's relatives, business partners, and entities where the borrower holds a controlling stake.

Why the clampdown

The RBI has been monitoring recovery practices. Sources indicate that some banks were selling seized properties back to defaulters at undervalued prices. This defeated the purpose of asset seizure and recovery.

A senior banking official, speaking on condition of anonymity, said the practice allowed borrowers to reclaim assets cheaply. It also gave room for collusion between bank officials and borrowers. The new rule closes that loophole.

What banks must do now

Banks must now sell seized immovable assets through public auction or private treaty to unrelated third parties. The sale must be transparent and at fair market value.

If a bank cannot sell the asset, it can retain the property and treat it as a non-performing asset on its books. Alternatively, it can transfer the asset to an asset reconstruction company.

The circular also clarifies that the restriction applies only to the original borrower and related parties. Genuine third-party buyers face no restrictions.

Impact on recovery timelines

The move could slow down recovery for some banks. Selling to a defaulting borrower was often the quickest route to closing a case. Now, banks must find genuine buyers, which can take longer.

But the RBI believes the long-term benefits outweigh short-term delays. Transparent auctions will fetch better prices. This will improve the overall recovery rate for the banking system.

Legal experts say the circular strengthens the SARFAESI Act's intent. The law was designed to help banks recover dues quickly, not to let defaulters reclaim assets at bargain prices.

Industry bodies have not yet issued a formal response. Bankers expect some operational challenges, especially in smaller towns where finding third-party buyers for large commercial properties is difficult.

What happens next

Banks have been given immediate effect to comply. The RBI will review compliance in its next supervisory assessment. Banks that violate the circular face penalties.

Observers expect a rise in the number of properties going to auction. Genuine buyers may find more opportunities to acquire assets from banks at competitive prices. The coming quarters will show whether the rule achieves its goal of cleaner recoveries.
