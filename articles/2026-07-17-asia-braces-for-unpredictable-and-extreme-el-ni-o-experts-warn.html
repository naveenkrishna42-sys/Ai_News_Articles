Asia Braces for 'Unpredictable and Extreme' El Niño, Experts Warn

📅 2026-07-17 📂 Environment Original source ↗
Key points
  • El Niño expected to cause extreme and unpredictable weather across Asia.
  • Region faces heightened risk of droughts, floods, and agricultural disruption.
  • Governments and communities urged to prepare for cascading impacts.

New Delhi: Asia is bracing for what meteorologists are describing as an 'unpredictable and extreme' El Niño event, with warnings that the climate phenomenon could unleash a cascade of weather disasters across the continent.

The alert comes from the Guardian, citing experts who say the current El Niño, which began developing in recent months, is shaping up to be one of the most volatile on record. The pattern, which warms sea-surface temperatures in the Pacific, is already disrupting rainfall and temperature norms from India to Indonesia.

Farmers Face Uncertain Monsoon

For India, where the June-September monsoon delivers nearly 70% of annual rainfall, the stakes are especially high. A strong El Niño typically suppresses monsoon rains, leading to drought in key agricultural states.

Farmers in states like Maharashtra and Uttar Pradesh are watching the skies with growing anxiety. The India Meteorological Department has not yet issued a definitive forecast for this year, but historical data suggests a higher probability of below-normal precipitation.

Agricultural economists warn that a weak monsoon could hit kharif crops like rice, sugarcane, and pulses, driving up food inflation and straining rural incomes. The government has begun reviewing contingency plans, including seed banks and water conservation measures.

Floods and Droughts in Tandem

The unpredictability of this El Niño is what sets it apart. While some regions will face drought, others could see sudden, intense downpours and flash floods.

Southeast Asian nations like Indonesia and the Philippines are bracing for both extremes. In recent years, El Niño has triggered severe forest fires in Indonesia due to prolonged dry spells, while also causing deadly landslides during unexpected heavy rains.

Meteorologists say the warming of the central and eastern Pacific is influencing the jet stream, creating a seesaw effect that makes long-range weather forecasting difficult. 'We are seeing signals that are highly erratic,' one expert noted. 'This is not a typical El Niño.'

Urban Infrastructure Under Stress

Asian megacities are also on notice. From Bangkok to Mumbai, urban planners are worried about water shortages and drainage failures.

Mumbai, which faced devastating floods in 2005 and again in 2017, is particularly vulnerable. The Brihanmumbai Municipal Corporation has started clearing storm drains and reviewing emergency protocols, but officials admit that the city's ageing infrastructure may not cope with a sudden deluge.

In Bangkok, authorities are grappling with the dual threat of drought and flooding. The Chao Phraya River, which supplies water to millions, could run dangerously low if the monsoon fails, yet also overflow if intense rains arrive without warning.

Power grids across the region are also at risk. Hydroelectric projects, which supply a significant portion of energy in countries like Laos and Nepal, depend on consistent water flows. A prolonged drought could force costly shifts to fossil fuels.

Regional Cooperation Needed

The transboundary nature of El Niño means no single country can manage its impacts alone. Water-sharing agreements between nations that rely on common river systems, such as the Mekong and the Indus, will be tested.

India has already initiated talks with Nepal and Bangladesh on coordinated water management. But experts say more urgent action is needed, including data sharing and joint disaster response drills.

The Association of Southeast Asian Nations (ASEAN) has convened a special meeting of environment ministers to discuss early warning systems and food security. However, critics argue that political will has been slow to match the scale of the threat.

What happens next depends on how the Pacific Ocean behaves over the next few weeks. Meteorologists will closely monitor sea-surface temperatures for signs of intensification. For millions of Asians, the coming months will be a test of resilience against a climate system that is growing more erratic by the year.
