Pakistan-Saudi-Turkiye defence pact: New regional order or paper promise?

📅 2026-08-08 📂 Top Stories Original source ↗
Key points
  • Saudi Arabia, Turkey, and Pakistan have signed a mutual defence pact in Mecca.
  • Iran has dismissed the agreement as a 'paper agreement' that won't bring security.
  • Experts suggest the pact could diminish US influence in West Asia.
  • The alliance's practical implications for regional order remain uncertain.

Mecca pact: A new axis takes shape

In a move that has sent ripples through West Asia, Saudi Arabia, Turkey, and Pakistan have pledged mutual defence. The agreement, signed in Mecca, comes amid escalating turmoil across the Middle East. The three nations, each with significant military capabilities, have framed the pact as a step toward regional stability.

But the announcement has been met with a mix of skepticism and concern. Iran, in particular, has dismissed the deal. In a sharp rebuke, Tehran said a 'paper agreement' with Turkey and Pakistan would not bring security to Riyadh. The comment underscores the deep fault lines that continue to divide the region.

What the pact actually promises

The details of the agreement remain sparse, but officials have indicated it covers mutual defence and security cooperation. For Saudi Arabia, the pact offers a hedge against perceived threats from Iran and its proxies. For Turkey, it bolsters its growing footprint in the Gulf and beyond. For Pakistan, it cements its role as a key player in the Islamic world's security architecture.

Yet, questions linger about the operational reality of such an alliance. Military pacts are only as strong as the willingness of signatories to act. Turkey and Pakistan have historically had complex relationships with both Saudi Arabia and Iran. Whether they would commit troops or resources in a conflict remains unclear.

Iran's sharp response

Iran's dismissive tone reflects its broader anxiety about encirclement. The Islamic Republic has long viewed Saudi-Pakistan ties with suspicion, and Turkey's involvement adds another layer. Tehran's statement, reported by NDTV, suggested the pact was more symbolic than substantive. 'Paper agreements will not alter the balance of power,' an Iranian official was quoted as saying.

The remark is telling. Iran has its own alliances, including with Russia and China, and has shown resilience against diplomatic isolation. But the Saudi-Turkish-Pakistani axis, if it solidifies, could complicate Tehran's regional calculus, particularly in conflict zones like Syria, Yemen, and the Gulf.

Israel watches warily

The pact also has implications for Israel. While neither Saudi Arabia nor Pakistan formally recognises Israel, the region's shifting alliances have been fluid. Israeli officials have not commented publicly, but analysts suggest the pact could either pressure Israel or push it closer to Gulf states that share its concerns about Iran. The Times of India framed the pact as an 'Islamic Nato' in the making, a label that may be premature but reflects the perceived weight of the alliance.

For Israel, the key question is whether this bloc will act cohesively against Iranian proxies like Hezbollah and Hamas. So far, there is no indication of coordinated military planning, and internal rivalries among the three nations could limit their collective action.

A diminished US role?

Experts cited by Telegraph India argue the pact signals a diminished US role in West Asia. Washington has long been the region's security guarantor, but its focus has shifted to the Indo-Pacific. The Saudi-Turkish-Pakistani initiative could be seen as an attempt to fill a vacuum, though none of the three nations has the logistical or intelligence capabilities of the US.

The United States has not issued an official response, but the pact could complicate its own alliances. Turkey, a Nato member, has often clashed with Washington over various issues, while Pakistan's ties with China have grown deeper. Saudi Arabia, meanwhile, has sought to diversify its security partnerships. A new bloc, even if symbolic, could erode Washington's leverage.

What to watch next

The coming months will reveal whether the Mecca pact translates into concrete action. Observers will look for joint military exercises, intelligence sharing, or diplomatic coordination. Iran's response will be crucial, as will Israel's calculations. The pact may remain a political statement, but in a region where rhetoric often precedes conflict, even a paper agreement could have real consequences.
