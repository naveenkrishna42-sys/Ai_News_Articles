Madhavan says son Vedaant only watches Japanese anime, not Indian films

📅 2026-07-17 📂 Movies Original source ↗
Key points
  • Madhavan says son Vedaant refuses to watch Indian cinema
  • Vedaant prefers Japanese anime and Western content
  • Actor notes changing tastes among younger generation

Actor opens up about his son's viewing preferences

R Madhavan, the versatile actor known for films like 'Rocketry: The Nambi Effect' and '3 Idiots', has revealed that his teenage son Vedaant has no interest in Indian cinema. The actor shared that Vedaant only watches Japanese anime and refuses to engage with Bollywood or regional Indian films.

Madhavan made the comment during a recent media interaction, where he discussed how his son's tastes reflect a broader generational shift. 'He's completely into Japanese anime. Studio Ghibli, Naruto, Attack on Titan — that's his world,' the actor said.

Parental perspective on changing entertainment habits

When asked if he tries to introduce Vedaant to his own films, Madhavan laughed and admitted failure. 'I've tried showing him some of my work. He just says, "Dad, that's too slow." He finds Indian cinema predictable compared to the layered storytelling in anime,' the actor explained.

Madhavan suggested that this isn't unique to his household. 'Most kids his age are watching Japanese or Korean content. They've grown up with YouTube, Netflix and global streaming platforms. Indian cinema has to compete with the entire world now,' he said.

Global appeal of anime among Indian youth

The actor's comments align with broader trends. Anime has seen explosive growth in India over the past decade, with platforms like Crunchyroll and Netflix investing heavily in dubbing and subtitling for Indian languages. Fan conventions now draw thousands of young attendees in cities like Mumbai, Delhi and Bengaluru.

Industry insiders note that Japanese anime offers something Indian cinema often lacks: compact seasons, genre experimentation and morally grey characters. 'A 12-episode anime can tell a tighter story than a 3-hour Bollywood film,' said a Mumbai-based film critic. 'Young audiences value that efficiency.'

Madhavan's own relationship with cinema

Despite his son's preferences, Madhavan remains optimistic about Indian storytelling. He pointed out that the film industry is adapting, with more OTT-driven content that appeals to younger viewers. 'We're seeing shows and films that are more global in their sensibilities,' he said.

The actor also revealed that Vedaant occasionally watches select Indian content — but only if it's recommended by friends online. 'Peer approval is everything for this generation. Not even a father's recommendation can beat that,' Madhavan joked.

Madhavan is currently promoting his upcoming projects, while Vedaant continues to build his anime collection. The actor said he has made peace with the situation. 'As long as he's consuming stories that make him think, I'm happy.'

What this means for Indian cinema

Madhavan's candid admission highlights a challenge facing the Indian film industry: retaining the attention of a generation raised on global streaming. While box office numbers remain strong for mass entertainers, younger, urban audiences are increasingly turning to foreign content for their storytelling fix.

Industry experts say this is both a threat and an opportunity. 'Indian creators need to learn from anime's pacing and ambition. But they also have a unique cultural voice that global audiences are discovering,' said a trade analyst.

Whether Vedaant will eventually warm to his father's films remains uncertain. For now, the Madhavan household is a divided audience — with one member firmly Team Anime.
