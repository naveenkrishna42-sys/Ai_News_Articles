Nepal Observes National Mourning as Rasuwa Flood Toll Climbs to 1,355

Nepal on Monday observed a national day of mourning for victims of the catastrophic flash floods that struck Rasuwa district earlier this month, as the official death toll climbed to 1,355 with nearly 5,000 people still listed as missing. The 13th-day mourning period aligns with traditional post-disaster rituals observed across South Asia, marking the conclusion of the formal grieving cycle before mass cremations and memorial rites.

Government officials confirmed that rescue operations remain concentrated in hydropower construction zones, where approximately 900 workers were deployed at the time of the disaster. Of these, 121 are feared trapped inside tunnel networks carved into the Himalayan foothills, presenting one of the most complex search-and-rescue challenges the region has faced in recent memory.

Scale of the Disaster and Geographic Impact

The floods struck Rasuwa, a mountainous district bordering Tibet, with sudden glacial melt compounded by sustained monsoon rainfall. River systems in the region feed directly into the Trishuli basin, which supports critical hydropower infrastructure supplying electricity to Kathmandu and northern India. The simultaneous destruction of access roads, bridges, and tunnel portals has made aerial extraction the only viable rescue channel.

Local administration reports indicate that entire villages were submerged within minutes, leaving survivors stranded on ridgelines with no vehicular access. Relief camps have been established in district headquarters, though supply lines remain constrained by continued landslides and damaged road networks.

Rescue Operations and Hydropower Worker Crisis

Rescue teams from the Nepal Army, Armed Police Force, and international humanitarian agencies have prioritised the hydropower corridors, where construction sites house the largest concentration of unaccounted personnel. Tunnel rescue operations require specialised equipment, including high-capacity pumps and breathing apparatus, much of which has been airlifted from Kathmandu and India.

The 121 workers feared trapped inside tunnels represent the most time-critical rescue segment, as survival windows in confined flood conditions rarely exceed 72 hours. Officials have not released revised estimates for those workers as of the mourning period's commencement.

Government Response and Recovery Outlook

The Nepal government has mobilised emergency relief funds and is coordinating with neighbouring India and China for cross-border assistance. Prime Minister KP Sharma Oli's administration faces mounting pressure to deliver transparent casualty reporting, particularly given the discrepancy between initial figures (1,300) and the current verified toll (1,355).

Recovery operations are expected to extend into the next quarter, with reconstruction of hydropower assets likely to delay several regional electricity projects by 12 to 18 months. The disaster has reignited debate over infrastructure placement in ecologically sensitive Himalayan zones, a policy conversation that will shape Nepal's energy and disaster-preparedness strategies for years.
