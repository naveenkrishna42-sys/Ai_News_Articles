Antarctica's Blood Falls holds ancient marine microbes, study finds

📅 2026-08-04 📂 Science Original source ↗
Key points
  • Microbes in Blood Falls are descendants of ancient marine organisms.
  • The brine originates from a marine source trapped millions of years ago.
  • Study sheds light on life's survival in extreme, isolated environments.

In one of the most hostile environments on Earth, scientists have discovered living descendants of ancient marine creatures. The find comes from beneath Antarctica's Blood Falls, a strikingly red glacier outflow that has puzzled researchers for over a century.

The blood-like colour, caused by iron-rich brine seeping from the glacier's interior, has now been linked to a hidden ecosystem. A new study, published in the journal Nature, provides molecular evidence for a relict marine community thriving in a subglacial brine-fed system in the Antarctic Dry Valleys.

A window into a lost ocean

The research suggests that the brine trapped beneath Taylor Glacier is not merely meltwater. Instead, it appears to originate from an ancient seawater source, isolated for millions of years. The microbes found in this salty, oxygen-free liquid are believed to be descendants of organisms that once lived in a marine environment long before the continent froze over.

These microorganisms have adapted to extreme conditions—sub-zero temperatures, high salinity, and complete darkness. Their survival offers a rare glimpse into how life can persist in isolation, far from the reach of sunlight or surface processes.

Scientists used advanced molecular techniques to analyse the genetic material in the brine. The results point to a community that has evolved separately from surface life, retaining traits of its marine ancestry.

Why Blood Falls runs red

Blood Falls has fascinated explorers since its discovery in 1911. The vivid red streaks staining the ice are caused by iron in the brine oxidising upon contact with air. But the source of that iron-rich water remained unclear for decades.

Earlier studies confirmed that the brine flows from a subglacial reservoir beneath Taylor Glacier. Yet the new findings go further, showing that the water's chemistry and microbial content match an ancient marine origin, not a modern surface source.

The discovery also raises questions about how such a system remained isolated for so long, and whether similar habitats exist elsewhere on Earth—or on other planets.

Implications for life beyond Earth

The study holds particular significance for astrobiology. If microbial life can survive for millions of years in a dark, salty, oxygen-deprived environment beneath Antarctic ice, then similar conditions on moons like Europa or Enceladus might also harbour life.

Researchers note that the Blood Falls ecosystem is not just a curiosity; it is a natural laboratory for understanding the limits of life. The microbes' ability to metabolise using iron and sulphur compounds, rather than sunlight, demonstrates a metabolic flexibility that could be common in extraterrestrial settings.

The team behind the study says further exploration of the subglacial system is needed. Drilling into the brine reservoir would allow direct sampling, but such operations are technically challenging and carry risks of contamination.

What to watch next

Scientists are now planning more detailed genetic analyses to determine how these microbes evolved over millions of years. The findings could also prompt new missions to other subglacial lakes in Antarctica, such as Lake Vostok, where similar relict communities may exist.

As technology improves, the secrets of these hidden ecosystems may slowly come to light—offering clues not just about Earth's past, but about the possibility of life elsewhere in the universe.
