India approves first dengue vaccine, Takeda's Qdenga gets CDSCO nod

📅 2026-07-21 📂 Health Original source ↗
Key points
  • Qdenga (TAK-003) becomes the first dengue vaccine approved for use in India.
  • The vaccine, developed by Japanese pharmaceutical company Takeda, targets all four dengue virus serotypes.
  • The Central Drugs Standard Control Organisation (CDSCO) gave the green light after domestic clinical trials.

India's drug regulator has cleared the country's first dengue vaccine, marking a significant step in the fight against a disease that hospitalises thousands every year. The Central Drugs Standard Control Organisation (CDSCO) approved Qdenga, a vaccine developed by Japanese pharmaceutical company Takeda, for use in the country.

The decision, reported by multiple news outlets on Tuesday, comes after years of clinical trials and regulatory scrutiny. Qdenga, also known by its scientific name TAK-003, is a live-attenuated vaccine designed to protect against all four serotypes of the dengue virus.

How the vaccine works

Qdenga is administered as a two-dose schedule, with the second shot given three months after the first. Unlike some earlier dengue vaccines, it does not require a prior dengue infection to be effective. The vaccine uses a weakened version of the dengue virus to stimulate an immune response without causing the disease.

According to Takeda, the vaccine has shown efficacy in preventing dengue fever and reducing hospitalisations in clinical trials conducted across dengue-endemic countries. In India, a city-based private university conducted the chemical trial for the vaccine as part of the local approval process.

The approval is particularly relevant for India, which reports a high burden of dengue cases each year. The country sees seasonal outbreaks, especially during the monsoon months, with the virus spreading through Aedes mosquitoes.

Regulatory process and local trials

The CDSCO's approval followed a review of data from global and domestic trials. The Indian trial, conducted by a private university, evaluated the vaccine's safety and immunogenicity in the local population. Officials have not yet disclosed detailed results from the Indian trial, but the regulator deemed the data sufficient for approval.

India's drug approval process requires that vaccines demonstrate safety and efficacy in the local context. The Qdenga trial in India was part of a broader global development programme that included participants from several countries.

Qdenga has already received approval in the European Union, the United Kingdom, and several other countries. However, the World Health Organization has not yet issued a formal recommendation for its widespread use in national immunisation programmes.

What this means for public health

Public health experts have welcomed the approval but caution that a vaccine alone will not end dengue. Vector control measures, such as eliminating mosquito breeding sites and using insect repellents, remain critical. The vaccine is expected to be available in private markets initially, with pricing yet to be announced.

The government has not indicated whether Qdenga will be included in the national immunisation programme. Such a decision would require additional cost-effectiveness studies and supply agreements with Takeda.

Dengue causes an estimated 20,000 to 30,000 deaths globally each year, with children and young adults particularly vulnerable. In India, the disease places a heavy burden on healthcare systems during outbreak seasons.

What to watch next: The manufacturer's pricing strategy and whether the government will procure the vaccine for public health programmes. Availability in clinics and hospitals is expected in the coming months, but the exact launch date has not been confirmed.
