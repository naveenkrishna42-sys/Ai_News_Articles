Musi river sediment safe, says MRDCL; activists cry foul

📅 2026-08-03 📂 Hyderabad Original source ↗
Key points
  • MRDCL asserts Musi river sediment contains no hazardous material
  • Activists question the claim, citing previous pollution concerns
  • Demand for independent testing and transparency grows

Hyderabad's Musi river, long a receptacle for the city's industrial and domestic waste, is at the centre of a fresh controversy. The Musi Riverfront Development Corporation Limited (MRDCL) has announced that sediment from the river is free of hazardous material. But environmental activists are pushing back, demanding independent verification.

The MRDCL's assertion came during a review of the riverfront beautification project, which involves large-scale desilting and dredging. Officials say tests conducted on sediment samples show no toxic elements, a finding they claim clears the way for using the dredged material in construction and landscaping.

Activists question the findings

However, activists who have tracked the Musi's pollution load for years are not convinced. They argue that the river's sediment, accumulated over decades of untreated effluent discharge, cannot be clean simply because a few samples tested benign. "The Musi has been a dumping ground for everything from tannery waste to pharmaceutical residue," said a local environmental campaigner, speaking on condition of anonymity. "One or two tests cannot erase that history."

The dispute hinges on methodology. The MRDCL has not shared the full details of the sampling process — how many samples were taken, from which depth, and at what locations. Activists say sediment contamination is often patchy; a clean patch near a bridge does not mean the entire stretch is safe.

Previous pollution concerns linger

Past studies by the Telangana State Pollution Control Board and other agencies have flagged high levels of heavy metals and organic pollutants in the Musi. The river receives effluents from industrial clusters in Jeedimetla, Patancheru, and Bollaram — areas notorious for chemical contamination. While the state has invested in sewage treatment plants, legacy pollution in the riverbed remains a concern.

Activists also point to the use of dredged material in public spaces. If the sediment is indeed contaminated, they argue, putting it in parks or along the riverfront could expose residents to health risks. "We are not against the beautification project," said another activist, "but we want proof that the material is safe before it is spread across the city."

Demand for independent testing

The core demand from civil society groups is simple: let an independent laboratory test the sediment. They have written to the Hyderabad Metropolitan Development Authority and the state environment department, requesting a transparent, third-party analysis. So far, there has been no official response.

The MRDCL, for its part, has reiterated its confidence in the tests. Officials say the samples were collected and analysed by accredited labs, following standard protocols. "We have nothing to hide," a corporation spokesperson said, adding that the findings would be shared with the public shortly.

But the trust deficit remains. In a city where environmental claims have often been contested, the burden of proof lies with the authorities. The activists' demand for independent testing is not unreasonable; it is a call for accountability in a project funded by public money.

What to watch next

The coming weeks will determine whether the MRDCL releases the full test data and agrees to an independent audit. If it does, the controversy may fizzle out. If it does not, the dispute could escalate into protests and legal challenges, further delaying the riverfront project. For now, the Musi's mud remains a matter of public debate — and the city is watching closely.
