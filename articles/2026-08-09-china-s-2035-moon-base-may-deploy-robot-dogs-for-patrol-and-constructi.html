China's 2035 moon base may deploy robot dogs for patrol and construction

📅 2026-08-09 📂 Science Original source ↗
Key points
  • Chinese space scientists propose using robot dogs at a lunar research station slated for 2035.
  • The quadruped robots would patrol, assist construction, and explore difficult terrain.
  • The plan aligns with China's broader lunar exploration goals, including a crewed presence.

Robotic sentinels for the lunar frontier

China's ambitious plan to establish a research station on the Moon by 2035 could include a surprising addition: robot dogs. Space scientists involved in the project have suggested that quadruped robots, similar to those being tested on Earth, could patrol the lunar surface, assist in construction, and explore areas that are difficult for wheeled rovers to reach.

The proposal, reported by Interesting Engineering and other outlets, highlights a growing interest in using legged robots for space exploration. Unlike traditional rovers, these machines can traverse rocky slopes, climb over obstacles, and maintain stability on uneven terrain, making them ideal for the Moon's challenging environment.

From Earth to the Moon

Robot dogs are already being deployed in various roles on Earth, from industrial inspections to military applications. Adapting them for space, however, requires significant engineering. The lunar environment poses extreme temperature swings, radiation exposure, and a vacuum that affects materials and electronics.

Scientists working on China's lunar programme envision these robots as multifunctional assets. They could carry sensors to monitor the station's perimeter, transport lightweight equipment, or even assist astronauts during extravehicular activities. Their agility would allow them to navigate craters and boulder fields where wheeled vehicles often get stuck.

Construction and exploration roles

Beyond patrolling, the robot dogs could play a hands-on role in building the station. With robotic arms or tools attached, they might help assemble structures, lay cables, or repair surface systems. This would reduce the need for human spacewalks, which are risky and resource-intensive.

For exploration, these robots could venture into lava tubes or shadowed craters where sunlight never reaches — areas of scientific interest that may hold water ice. Their small size and stability would allow them to enter spaces that larger rovers cannot access, sending back data and images to mission control.

Part of a larger lunar vision

China's lunar research station, expected to be operational by 2035, is a cornerstone of the country's space ambitions. The station is planned to support long-duration human stays and scientific research, serving as a base for deeper exploration of the Solar System.

The inclusion of robot dogs would add a layer of autonomy and resilience to the mission. If humans are absent or resting, these machines could continue routine tasks, ensuring that the station remains productive around the clock.

Details about the robots' design, power systems, and communication capabilities have not been fully disclosed. Officials have not yet confirmed a specific timeline for testing or deployment, but the concept reflects a broader trend in space agencies worldwide to integrate legged robotics into planetary missions.

What to watch

As China advances its lunar programme, observers will look for prototype tests on Earth, possibly in simulated lunar environments. The next few years may see the first demonstrations of these quadruped robots in desert or volcanic terrains that mimic the Moon's surface.

The success of such trials could determine whether robot dogs become a permanent feature of the 2035 station — and possibly inspire other nations to adopt similar technologies for their own lunar projects.
