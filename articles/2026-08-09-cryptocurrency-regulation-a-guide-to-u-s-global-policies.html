Cryptocurrency Regulation: A Guide to U.S. & Global Policies

📅 2026-08-09 📂 Crypto Original source ↗
Key points
  • The U.S. uses a patchwork of agencies like SEC, CFTC, and FinCEN to oversee crypto.
  • Global approaches range from outright bans in China to EU's MiCA framework.
  • Tax treatment varies, with the IRS treating crypto as property, not currency.

The U.S. Approach: A Patchwork of Agencies

In the United States, cryptocurrency regulation is not a single, unified law but a complex web of overlapping authorities. The Securities and Exchange Commission (SEC) treats many digital assets as securities, while the Commodity Futures Trading Commission (CFTC) oversees crypto derivatives. Meanwhile, the Financial Crimes Enforcement Network (FinCEN) applies anti-money laundering rules to exchanges.

This fragmented structure has led to confusion for investors and businesses alike. The SEC, under Chair Gary Gensler, has aggressively pursued enforcement actions against major exchanges like Coinbase and Binance, arguing that most tokens are unregistered securities. Yet, Congress has failed to pass comprehensive legislation, leaving the industry in legal limbo.

Global Divergence: From Bans to Frameworks

Around the world, regulatory responses vary dramatically. China has imposed a complete ban on cryptocurrency trading and mining, a stark contrast to its earlier position as a mining hub. In Europe, the Markets in Crypto-Assets (MiCA) regulation, passed in 2023, provides a comprehensive licensing regime for issuers and service providers, setting a global benchmark.

Other nations are taking a more welcoming stance. Japan recognizes Bitcoin as legal property under the Payment Services Act, while Singapore's Payment Services Act requires licensing for crypto firms. The UK is moving toward a tailored framework, aiming to position itself as a crypto hub post-Brexit.

Taxation: A Key Battleground

Tax treatment of cryptocurrencies is another critical aspect. In the U.S., the Internal Revenue Service (IRS) classifies virtual currencies as property, meaning every sale or exchange is a taxable event. This has created a compliance burden for users, who must track gains and losses meticulously.

In contrast, Germany exempts long-term holdings from capital gains tax after one year, and Portugal has historically offered tax-free crypto gains, though recent changes have introduced new levies. India, meanwhile, imposes a flat 30% tax on crypto income and a 1% TDS on transactions, a policy that has sparked debate about its impact on adoption.

Stablecoins and the Road Ahead

Stablecoins, pegged to fiat currencies, have drawn particular scrutiny. The U.S. has proposed legislation like the Lummis-Gillibrand bill to regulate them, focusing on reserve requirements and transparency. Meanwhile, the collapse of TerraUSD in 2022 underscored the risks of algorithmic stablecoins, prompting calls for stricter oversight.

International coordination remains elusive, though bodies like the Financial Action Task Force (FATF) have issued guidelines on travel rules for crypto transfers. The G20 has also endorsed a global framework, but implementation is uneven.

What to Watch Next

As the industry matures, expect more clarity but also more friction. The U.S. Supreme Court could weigh in on SEC authority, and the 2026 elections may shift priorities in Congress. Globally, watch for how emerging economies balance innovation with financial stability.
