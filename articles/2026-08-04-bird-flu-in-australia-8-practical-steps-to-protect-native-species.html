Bird flu in Australia: 8 practical steps to protect native species

📅 2026-08-04 📂 Health Original source ↗
Key points
  • Australia confirms first mass bird flu mortality in wildlife, with suspected cases in a second state.
  • Experts recommend eight practical measures for the public to reduce virus spread and protect native species.
  • Little penguin colonies face potential population loss of up to 40% from the outbreak.

Australia is confronting an unprecedented wildlife crisis as the H5N1 bird flu strain spreads beyond initial hotspots, prompting scientists and officials to urge immediate public action. The first mass mortality event in native wildlife has been confirmed, and suspected cases have emerged in a second state, leading Agriculture Minister Murray Watt to warn that “this is the beginning.”

While the situation is grave, experts from The Conversation emphasise that individuals are not powerless. A new guide outlines eight practical steps that can meaningfully reduce the virus's impact on Australia’s unique birds and mammals.

Understanding the threat

The H5N1 strain, which has devastated poultry flocks and wild bird populations globally, has now reached Australian shores. The recent confirmation of mass deaths among wild birds marks a troubling milestone, as Australia had previously remained isolated from the worst outbreaks affecting other continents.

Conservationists are particularly concerned about the little penguin, Australia’s smallest penguin species. Modelling suggests that some colonies could lose up to 40% of their population, a blow that would take years to recover from.

Eight ways to help

Experts stress that while the scale of the outbreak may seem overwhelming, individual actions collectively make a difference. Here are the recommended steps:

  • Report dead or sick birds to local wildlife authorities promptly, providing precise locations and, if possible, photographs.
  • Avoid touching or handling dead birds or animals without protective gloves and a mask, as the virus can transmit to humans.
  • Keep domestic pets, especially cats and dogs, away from wild birds and carcasses to prevent them from becoming vectors.
  • Clean and disinfect bird feeders, baths, and any outdoor equipment that may come into contact with wild birds.
  • Maintain good biosecurity on rural properties, including limiting access to ponds or feed areas that attract waterfowl.
  • Do not feed wild birds, as this encourages crowding and increases transmission risk.
  • Stay informed through official channels such as the Department of Agriculture, Fisheries and Forestry updates.
  • Support local conservation groups working on habitat restoration and wildlife rescue, either through donations or volunteer work.

These measures are designed to limit both the spread of the virus and the chances of human exposure. Officials have not yet confirmed the full extent of the outbreak, but early indications suggest it could affect multiple species across several regions.

Government response and warnings

Federal and state authorities are coordinating surveillance and response efforts, with rapid testing of suspected cases underway. Minister Watt’s stark warning underscores the urgency, as the virus shows signs of establishing a foothold in Australian ecosystems.

Wildlife agencies are preparing contingency plans for carcass disposal and, in some cases, vaccination trials for captive populations. However, for wild animals, prevention through public cooperation remains the most viable strategy.

What to watch next

In the coming weeks, authorities will release more data on the outbreak’s geographic spread and species affected. The public is advised to monitor official advisories and act on new guidance as it emerges. The coming months will be critical in determining whether Australia can contain this outbreak or if it becomes endemic.
