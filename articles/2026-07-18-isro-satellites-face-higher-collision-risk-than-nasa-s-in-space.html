ISRO satellites face higher collision risk than NASA's in space

📅 2026-07-18 📂 Space Original source ↗
Key points
  • ISRO satellites are statistically more likely to collide than NASA satellites due to orbital congestion.
  • The analysis highlights challenges in space debris mitigation and coordination.
  • Experts call for improved tracking and international cooperation to prevent accidents.

Indian Space Research Organisation (ISRO) satellites are significantly more prone to collisions in orbit than their NASA counterparts, according to a recent analysis. The finding underscores growing concerns about space traffic management as more countries and private companies launch payloads into increasingly crowded orbits.

The disparity stems from the orbital paths and altitudes where ISRO typically places its satellites. Many of India's spacecraft operate in low Earth orbit (LEO) at altitudes between 400 and 600 kilometres, a region already dense with debris and active satellites from other nations.

Why ISRO satellites face higher risk

NASA satellites, by contrast, often occupy higher orbits or have more manoeuvrability to avoid potential collisions. ISRO's satellites, particularly those in polar sun-synchronous orbits, share similar trajectories with many defunct satellites and fragments.

The analysis, which examined collision probability metrics, found that ISRO satellites have a statistically higher chance of close approaches than NASA spacecraft. This is partly due to the sheer volume of objects in these popular orbits.

Space debris and collision avoidance

Space debris mitigation has become a pressing issue for all spacefaring nations. The Kessler Syndrome, a scenario where collisions cascade out of control, looms as a real threat. ISRO has its own debris tracking systems, but the density of objects in its preferred orbits makes avoidance manoeuvres more frequent.

Experts note that ISRO satellites often lack the propulsion systems needed for significant orbital changes. This limits their ability to dodge debris, unlike many larger NASA satellites equipped with thrusters for collision avoidance.

International coordination efforts

The findings highlight the need for better coordination among space agencies. Currently, no global treaty governs space traffic, and data sharing remains inconsistent. ISRO and NASA do exchange some orbital data, but it is not comprehensive.

“The gap in collision probability is not just a technical issue but a policy one,” said an analyst familiar with the data. “Without shared standards for debris mitigation, the risk will only grow.”

India has taken steps to improve its space situational awareness, including setting up a dedicated debris tracking centre. However, the sheer number of objects in LEO means that even the best tracking cannot prevent every close call.

What this means for future missions

ISRO's ambitious plans, including the Gaganyaan human spaceflight program and a growing constellation of Earth observation satellites, will put more spacecraft in harm's way. Each new launch adds to the congestion, raising the odds of a collision.

The space agency has acknowledged the problem and is exploring options like adding propulsion systems to smaller satellites and designing them for end-of-life disposal. But retrofitting existing designs is costly and time-consuming.

Other nations face similar challenges, but the data suggests India's satellites are among the most vulnerable. As commercial players like SpaceX and OneWeb also fill LEO with thousands of satellites, the risk environment is becoming more complex for everyone.

Observers say that without a coordinated international framework, the probability of a major collision involving an Indian satellite will continue to rise. The next few years will test whether spacefaring nations can cooperate on traffic management before an accident forces their hand.
