CJP's Saurav Das Says He Was Stopped at Delhi Airport Bound for Oxford

✍️ By TIVRA News Editorial Desk 📅 2026-11-24 📂 Top Stories Original source ↗
Key points
  • Saurav Das of Citizens for Justice and Peace says he was prevented from boarding a UK-bound flight at Delhi's IGI Airport on Monday, where he intended to speak at the University of Oxford.
  • No agency — Bureau of Immigration, Delhi Police or MHA — has publicly confirmed or explained the action in the aggregated coverage, and the legal basis remains unstated.
  • Stops at Indian airports usually stem from a Look Out Circular, an impounded passport, or a court order; the distinction determines the remedy available to the traveller.

What Saurav Das Says Happened at Delhi Airport

Saurav Das, a member of Citizens for Justice and Peace (CJP), says he was stopped by airport authorities at Delhi's Indira Gandhi International Airport on Monday while attempting to board a flight to the United Kingdom. In remarks to reporters, Das said he was travelling to deliver a lecture at the University of Oxford and was turned back before departure.

Das described the government in sharply critical terms, calling it a "coward government" in comments carried by The Times of India. He has not, as of reporting, released the specific written grounds — if any — cited by officers for the stoppage.

Reports from The Hindu, The Wire and Deccan Chronicle carry the same core claim: that Das was barred from boarding an outbound international flight. None of the aggregated reports cite an on-record statement from the Bureau of Immigration, Delhi Police or the Ministry of Home Affairs confirming or explaining the action.

The Oxford Lecture and the Political Fallout

Why a university invitation became a flashpoint

Das's stated destination was a speaking engagement at Oxford, which places the episode inside a wider pattern of Indian activists and commentators reporting travel disruption ahead of overseas appearances. CJP has been involved in several politically sensitive litigations over the years, and its members' movements have drawn official scrutiny before.

CJP's response

Ratna Singh, a fellow CJP member, framed the stoppage as part of a broader squeeze on dissent, telling ANI that youth are being targeted for questioning those in power. That is the sharpest on-record political reaction in the aggregated coverage; no government spokesperson was quoted responding to it.

What is confirmed — and what is not

Confirmed: Das publicly stated he was prevented from flying to the UK. Unconfirmed: the legal basis, the issuing authority, and whether any written order exists. Political characterisations from either side should be read as claims, not findings.

How Stops at Indian Airports Actually Work

A traveller can be prevented from boarding an international flight at three distinct checkpoints: airline check-in, immigration clearance, and the boarding gate. Each is handled by a different authority, and the legal instrument behind a stop differs in each case.

The most common mechanism is a Look Out Circular (LOC), issued by an investigating agency or a ministry to the Bureau of Immigration. LOCs are opened for reasons ranging from pending criminal cases to summons or security considerations, and the subject typically learns of one only at the counter, minutes before departure.

In other instances, a passport may have been impounded under the Passports Act, or a court order may restrict travel. Das has not stated publicly which of these applied, and the distinction matters: an LOC is an administrative flag, while impounding is a statutory action backed by a written order.

What This Means for Travellers and Rights Watchers

For ordinary travellers, the practical lesson is narrow but real: an LOC can surface only at the airport, with no prior notice and limited recourse at the counter. Remedy lies through the issuing agency or the courts, not with the airline or the immigration officer on duty.

For civil-liberties groups, the case feeds an existing argument that travel restrictions function as a low-visibility pressure tactic — hard to challenge because no formal order is served on the individual. The counter-position from agencies is that LOCs are lawful instruments open to judicial review.

Expect the next developments to hinge on documentation: whether Das files a challenge seeking disclosure of any order, and whether the agencies put their reasoning on record. Until then, the episode remains an allegation with a political tail, not an adjudicated finding.
