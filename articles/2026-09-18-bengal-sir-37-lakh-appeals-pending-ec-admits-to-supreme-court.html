Bengal SIR: 37 Lakh Appeals Pending, EC Admits to Supreme Court

✍️ By TIVRA News Editorial Team 📅 2026-09-18 📂 India Original source ↗
Key points
  • Over 37 lakh appeals against West Bengal's Special Intensive Revision (SIR) remain pending before tribunals, with only around 1.02 lakh heard so far.
  • The EC's affidavit to the Supreme Court pegs the disposal rate at roughly 2.6%, raising questions about tribunal capacity ahead of municipal polls.
  • The affidavit reportedly lacks a break-up of appeals seeking inclusion versus exclusion of names, a key transparency gap flagged by the court.
  • With municipal elections approaching, unresolved rolls could leave lakhs of genuine voters without clarity on their electoral status.

The Numbers Behind Bengal's SIR Backlog

The Election Commission has informed the Supreme Court that more than 37 lakh appeals arising from West Bengal's Special Intensive Revision (SIR) of electoral rolls remain pending before the appellate tribunals. According to the affidavit, only around 1.02 lakh appeals have been heard so far — a disposal rate of approximately 2.6%. The disclosure came in response to the court's scrutiny of how the SIR process is being implemented across the state.

The scale is significant. West Bengal has roughly 7.5 crore electors on its rolls. If even a fraction of the 37 lakh pending appeals involve wrongly deleted names, the practical impact on the 2026 municipal polls could run into lakhs of disenfranchised voters.

What the EC Affidavit Discloses — And What It Doesn't

The affidavit confirms the raw backlog but, as The Indian Express reported, does not provide a break-up of appeals seeking inclusion of names versus those challenging exclusion. That distinction matters: inclusion appeals represent voters who claim they were wrongly removed, while exclusion appeals seek removal of ineligible entries. Without this split, courts and observers cannot assess how many genuine electors are affected.

The Supreme Court has repeatedly pressed the EC on timelines and procedural fairness. The commission's filing is the latest in a series of status updates, but the gap between appeals filed and appeals decided continues to widen.

Why the Tribunal Backlog Matters for Voters

Appellate tribunals are the first and often only remedy for a voter whose name is struck off during SIR. If hearings crawl at 2.6%, a voter deleted in early 2026 may not get a decision before municipal polling day. That effectively removes the remedy the law promises.

For buyers of electoral data services, political consultancies and grassroots organisations, the backlog also distorts voter-list analytics. Campaigns working off stale or partially revised rolls risk misallocating resources — a direct commercial cost in a state where booth-level micro-targeting drives spending.

Real-World Highlights and Concerns

  • Disposal bottleneck: Only ~1.02 lakh of 37 lakh+ appeals heard — tribunals clearly lack capacity to clear the queue at current pace.
  • Transparency gap: No inclusion/exclusion split in the affidavit makes it impossible to quantify how many genuine voters are at risk.
  • Election timing risk: Municipal polls drawing near mean unresolved appeals could translate into last-minute litigation and roll chaos.
  • Operational cost: Political parties and data vendors must budget for repeated list verification cycles, raising campaign costs.
  • Judicial oversight: The Supreme Court's continued monitoring is the main check on how fast tribunals actually deliver.

What Happens Next

The Supreme Court is expected to seek a concrete timeline for clearing the backlog and may direct the EC to publish a category-wise break-up of appeals. Any such order would set a benchmark for future SIR exercises in other states.

For now, the arithmetic is stark: at the reported pace, clearing 37 lakh appeals would take years, not months. Unless tribunal strength is expanded or procedures simplified, Bengal's municipal polls will proceed with a substantial cloud over the integrity of its voter rolls.
