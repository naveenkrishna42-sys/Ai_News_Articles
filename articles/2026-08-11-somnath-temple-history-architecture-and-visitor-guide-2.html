Somnath Temple: History, Architecture, and Visitor Guide

📅 2026-08-11 📂 Sacred Places Original source ↗
Key points
  • Somnath is one of the 12 Jyotirlingas, sacred to Lord Shiva.
  • The temple has been destroyed and rebuilt multiple times, symbolizing resilience.
  • Best visited between October and March; nearest airport is Diu, railway station is Veraval.

The Eternal Jyotirlinga

Somnath Temple stands on the western coast of Gujarat, where the Arabian Sea meets the land. It is one of the twelve Jyotirlingas, the most sacred abodes of Lord Shiva. The name means 'Lord of the Moon', referring to the legend of Soma, the moon god, who built a golden temple here to recover from a curse.

For centuries, this site has drawn pilgrims from across India. The current structure, built in the Chalukya style, rises 50 metres above the shore. Its shikhara (spire) is visible from kilometres away, and the flag atop it changes three times a day — a tradition that continues without interruption.

A History of Destruction and Rebuilding

The temple's history is one of repeated destruction and reconstruction. Arab invaders, including Mahmud of Ghazni in the 11th century, sacked it. Each time, devotees rebuilt it. The British, and later independent India, oversaw its restoration. The present structure was completed in 1951, financed by public donations.

Despite the upheavals, the site's spiritual continuity never broke. The original lingam, though damaged, was reinstalled after each rebuilding. This resilience has made Somnath a symbol of Hindu faith and cultural identity. The temple trust maintains detailed records of its past, and a museum nearby displays artefacts from earlier periods.

Architecture and Spiritual Significance

The temple's design follows the classical Māru-Gurjara style. Intricate carvings of deities, floral motifs, and celestial dancers adorn its walls. The main sanctum houses the jyotirlinga, a smooth black stone emblem of Shiva. The temple faces east, so the first rays of the sun fall directly on the lingam during the equinoxes.

Inside, the ambulatory pathway allows devotees to circumambulate the sanctum. The acoustic design ensures that chants resonate clearly through the hall. The temple is also aligned with the sea — the waves crashing against the shore are a constant backdrop to the rituals. Non-Hindus can enter the outer courtyard but not the inner sanctum.

Light and Sound Show

Every evening, a sound-and-light show recounts the temple's story in Hindi and English. It is a 30-minute presentation projected onto the temple walls. The show is free and starts after sunset. It offers a vivid narration of the temple's many lives, making it a good introduction for first-time visitors.

Practical Visiting Information

The best time to visit is between October and March, when the weather is pleasant. Summers are extremely hot, and the monsoon brings heavy rain. The temple opens at 6:00 AM for the first darshan, and closes at 9:30 PM. Aarti, the ritual of light, is performed at dawn and dusk — these are the most moving times to attend.

To reach Somnath, fly into Diu Airport, about 60 km away, which has direct flights from Mumbai. The nearest railway station is Veraval, 7 km away, connected to major cities like Ahmedabad and Rajkot. From Veraval, auto-rickshaws and taxis are available. Ahmedabad, 400 km away, is the major international gateway.

Nearby attractions worth combining in the same trip: the Triveni Sangam, where three rivers meet the sea, just a short walk from the temple. The Somnath Beach offers a quiet stretch of sand. The ancient city of Junagadh, with its Girnar hills and Uparkot fort, is a 100 km drive and makes a good day trip.

Plan at least half a day for the temple complex, including the museum and the light show. Modest clothing is expected, and shoes must be removed before entering. Carry minimal valuables, as the crowd can be dense during festivals. The temple trust runs a free cloakroom for luggage.

If you are visiting during the festival of Maha Shivaratri, be prepared for large crowds and extended queues. That said, the energy during these nights is extraordinary — thousands of lamps, continuous chanting, and the sea breeze carrying the sound of conch shells. For a quieter experience, visit on a weekday morning.
