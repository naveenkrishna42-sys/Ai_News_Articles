Manufacturing Majorities: Defections and Democracy in India

📅 2026-08-11 📂 Politics Original source ↗
Key points
  • The practice of manufacturing artificial majorities through defections is a growing concern in Indian politics.
  • Such defections often bypass constitutional intent, undermining democratic representation.
  • The politics of resistance has emerged as a response to these engineered majorities.

The Mechanics of an Artificial Majority

In India's parliamentary system, the numbers in the House are the ultimate currency. But what happens when those numbers are assembled not through elections, but through engineered defections? The term 'artificial majority' has become a recurring feature in the country's political lexicon, describing a government or coalition that secures its strength not at the ballot box, but by persuading—or pressuring—elected members to switch sides.

This practice, while not new, has gained a sharper edge in recent years. Critics argue that it reduces democracy to a numbers game, where the mandate of the people is overridden by the arithmetic of convenience. The result is a legislature that may claim a majority, but one that is constitutionally and ethically fragile.

Defections and the Constitution

The Tenth Schedule of the Indian Constitution, commonly known as the anti-defection law, was designed precisely to curb such instability. It disqualifies legislators who voluntarily give up their party membership or defy party whips. Yet, loopholes remain. Bulk defections, where two-thirds of a party's legislators merge with another, are often used to bypass disqualification—a provision intended to protect genuine mergers, but frequently exploited for political gain.

Constitutional experts point out that such maneuvers violate the spirit of the law. The intent was to ensure that the mandate given by voters to a party is not casually discarded. When legislators switch sides in droves, often within days of an election, the question arises: who do these representatives truly serve? The voters who elected them on one platform, or the new dispensation that offered them a ministerial berth or other inducements?

The Politics of Resistance

In response to these engineered majorities, a 'politics of resistance' has emerged. Opposition parties, civil society groups, and even within the ruling blocs, there are voices that contest the legitimacy of such governments. Protests in the well of the House, walkouts, and legal challenges in courts have become the tools of those who feel the democratic process has been subverted.

This resistance, however, is not merely about numbers. It is about the foundational principle that a government's legitimacy rests on the consent of the governed, expressed through free and fair elections. When that consent is circumvented, it undermines public faith in institutions. The judiciary, at times, has stepped in to scrutinize such defections, but the process is slow, and the political damage is often done.

Constitutional Integrity at Stake

The debate is not just about the letter of the law, but about its soul. An artificial majority may pass legislation, but it lacks the moral authority that comes from a genuine electoral mandate. This can lead to a crisis of governance, where policies are implemented without broad-based support, and dissent is seen as disloyalty rather than a healthy democratic check.

Political analysts note that the problem is exacerbated by the increasing centralization of power within parties, where individual legislators have little autonomy. In such a scenario, the lure of office or the fear of reprisal can push members to act against their own party's stated ideology.

The Road Ahead

As India moves forward, the challenge is to strengthen the anti-defection framework without stifling legitimate political realignment. Some suggest that the two-thirds merger provision should be tightened, while others call for a greater role for the Election Commission in scrutinizing defections. The Supreme Court has, in the past, flagged concerns about the misuse of the Tenth Schedule, but a comprehensive legislative fix remains elusive.

What is clear is that the issue will not disappear. With coalition politics becoming the norm in several states, the temptation to manufacture majorities will persist. The coming months are likely to see further debates in Parliament and the courts over the limits of political expediency. For Indian democracy, the key test will be whether its institutions can defend the principle that a majority, to be legitimate, must be earned, not manufactured.
