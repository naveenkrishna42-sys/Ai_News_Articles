First Images Show Crater From SpaceX Rocket Impact on Moon

📅 2026-08-08 📂 Science Original source ↗
Key points
  • South Korea's lunar orbiter has captured the first images of the crater formed by a SpaceX rocket stage crashing into the Moon.
  • The impact was confirmed by scientists, and NASA plans to observe the lunar impact site.
  • The images provide a closer look at the aftermath of the accidental collision.

For the first time, we have visual proof of the scar left on the Moon by a stray piece of SpaceX hardware. South Korea's lunar orbiter, Danuri, has sent back images of the crater formed when a discarded rocket stage slammed into the far side of the Moon earlier this year.

The images, shared by the Korea Aerospace Research Institute (KARI), show a relatively fresh, sharp-edged crater in a region of the lunar surface. The impact site is located near the Hertzsprung crater, on the Moon's far side, which is permanently facing away from Earth.

This is the first confirmed visual of the aftermath of what was an accidental, high-speed collision. Scientists had been tracking the rogue rocket stage since it was spotted on a collision course with the Moon. The debris was originally part of a SpaceX Falcon 9 launch, which had been drifting in space for years after completing its mission.

How the Crash Happened

The rocket stage, weighing several tonnes, was travelling at a speed of over 9,000 kilometres per hour when it hit the lunar surface. The impact likely excavated a crater several metres wide, ejecting material far across the barren landscape.

The exact timing and location of the crash were not known with certainty until the Danuri orbiter passed over the region. The spacecraft, which has been orbiting the Moon since late 2022, used its high-resolution camera to capture the fresh scar.

Experts say the images are crucial for understanding how such impacts alter the lunar surface, and for refining models of impact cratering on airless bodies.

Why the Far Side Matters

The far side of the Moon is a scientifically valuable but difficult-to-observe region. It is shielded from Earth's radio noise, making it an ideal location for future radio telescopes. But it also means that impacts there are harder to spot and study.

The Danuri orbiter's ability to photograph this region in detail is a significant achievement. It provides a rare, close-up view of a recent impact event, which is not just a curiosity but a data point for planetary science.

The crater's appearance—its size, shape, and the pattern of ejecta—offers clues about the angle and speed of the impact, as well as the properties of the lunar soil at that location.

NASA's Interest

NASA has also expressed interest in the event. The US space agency had earlier announced plans to attempt observations of the rocket part's lunar impact, using its Lunar Reconnaissance Orbiter (LRO). The LRO has been mapping the Moon for over a decade and has imaged many fresh craters, including those created by other spacecraft impacts.

However, the first images have come from the Korean orbiter. The KARI team has been processing the data and has released the images to the public, marking a milestone for South Korea's lunar exploration programme.

The confirmation of the crater and its location will help scientists coordinate further observations, both from orbit and potentially from the surface in the future.

What Happens Next

Now that the impact site is known, scientists can plan more detailed studies. They will compare the crater with other fresh craters on the Moon to understand the impact process better.

There is also a broader conversation about space debris and the need for responsible disposal of spacecraft hardware. This incident has highlighted the fact that even defunct rocket parts can become hazards, not just in Earth's orbit but across the solar system.

As more nations and private companies send missions to the Moon, such impacts may become more common. The data from Danuri will be valuable for future mission planning, ensuring that lunar operations are conducted safely and sustainably.

For now, the images stand as a reminder of the growing human footprint on our celestial neighbour—and of the need to keep track of our hardware, even when it is billions of kilometres away.
