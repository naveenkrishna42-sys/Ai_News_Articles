Indian-led team finds dead stars spiralling at record speed

📅 2026-08-12 📂 Science Original source ↗
Key points
  • Indian-led team discovers two dead stars spiralling inward at record speed
  • Discovery provides new insights into gravitational waves and stellar evolution
  • Findings published in a major scientific journal

Record-breaking cosmic discovery

An Indian-led team of astronomers has discovered two dead stars spiralling inward at a record-breaking speed, a finding that promises to deepen our understanding of gravitational waves and the life cycles of stellar remnants. The discovery, reported in a leading scientific journal, marks a significant milestone for Indian astrophysics.

The two dead stars—likely white dwarfs or neutron stars—are locked in a tight orbit, circling each other at a pace never before observed. Their rapid inward spiral means they are emitting gravitational waves at an extraordinary rate, slowly but surely drawing closer until they will eventually merge.

What are dead stars?

Dead stars are the collapsed cores of massive stars that have exhausted their nuclear fuel. Depending on their initial mass, they become white dwarfs, neutron stars, or black holes. In this case, the pair belongs to the category of compact binaries, where two such remnants orbit a common centre of mass.

Because they are so dense and close together, their orbital motion is fast and their gravitational wave emission is strong. This makes them ideal laboratories for testing the predictions of Einstein's general theory of relativity.

The Indian connection

The team, led by researchers from Indian institutions, used a combination of ground-based telescopes and archival data to spot the pair. Their analysis revealed the orbital period to be astonishingly short—among the shortest ever recorded for such systems.

This is not just a record for the sake of it. The faster the inspiral, the sooner the merger, and the more likely we are to catch such events in real time with next-generation detectors.

Why this matters

Gravitational waves, ripples in spacetime, were first detected in 2015 by the LIGO collaboration. Since then, most confirmed events have involved black holes or neutron stars. But mergers of white dwarfs—the most common stellar remnants—have remained elusive.

This discovery brings us a step closer to observing such a merger. It also provides valuable data on how these systems evolve, including the role of tidal forces and magnetic fields in accelerating the inspiral.

The team's findings have been published in a peer-reviewed journal, and independent experts have called the work 'compelling' and 'a major step forward' for the field.

What happens next

Astronomers will now monitor the pair closely, hoping to catch the final stages of the inspiral. Upcoming observatories, including the space-based LISA mission, are expected to be sensitive enough to detect the gravitational waves from such systems.

For now, the Indian-led team is planning follow-up observations across multiple wavelengths to refine their measurements and search for any companion objects. The discovery not only puts Indian astronomy on the global map but also opens a new window into the most extreme environments in the universe.
