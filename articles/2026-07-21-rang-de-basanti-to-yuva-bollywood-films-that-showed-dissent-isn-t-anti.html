Rang De Basanti to Yuva: Bollywood films that showed dissent isn't anti-national

📅 2026-07-21 📂 Movies Original source ↗
Key points
  • Rang De Basanti (2006) used student activism against corruption to question the idea of patriotism.
  • Yuva (2004) explored political awakening and dissent among ordinary citizens.
  • These films challenged the narrative that questioning authority is unpatriotic.

In recent years, the line between dissent and anti-nationalism has become a flashpoint in public discourse. But Bollywood, often a mirror to society, has long grappled with this theme. Films like Rang De Basanti and Yuva offered a nuanced take: questioning authority is not a betrayal of the nation, but a form of patriotism itself.

These movies did not shy away from showing young Indians angry with the system. They argued that dissent — whether against corruption, political apathy, or social injustice — can be a deeply patriotic act.

Rang De Basanti: Rebellion as love for country

Rakeysh Omprakash Mehra's 2006 film Rang De Basanti remains a landmark. It follows a group of Delhi University students who, after being inspired by the stories of Bhagat Singh and his comrades, take up arms against a corrupt defence minister.

The film's young protagonists are initially apathetic. But when a friend dies in a fighter jet crash caused by corrupt deals, they channel their anger into direct action. The film does not portray them as terrorists. Instead, it frames their actions as a desperate attempt to reclaim a nation stolen by the powerful.

Critics at the time called it a call to arms. Supporters saw it as a plea for accountability. The film's enduring power lies in its refusal to equate dissent with disloyalty. The characters' final act — assassinating the minister — is presented as a sacrifice for the nation, not against it.

Yuva: Ordinary people, extraordinary questions

Mani Ratnam's Yuva (2004) took a different route. It wove three parallel stories of young men from different walks of life, all connected by a single political assassination attempt.

One protagonist, Michael (Ajay Devgn), is a disillusioned student who becomes a political activist. He challenges the system from within, fighting for the rights of the poor. Another, Arjun (Vivek Oberoi), is a selfish careerist who learns to care about society after a personal tragedy.

The film shows that dissent can take many forms — from street protests to quiet defiance in everyday life. It does not glorify violence but argues that apathy is the real enemy. The climax, where the characters choose to stand up against a corrupt politician, reinforces the idea that questioning power is the essence of democracy.

Other films that questioned the narrative

Several other Bollywood films have walked this line. Hazaaron Khwaishein Aisi (2005) explored the Naxalite movement and the price of dissent. Dev (2004), also by Mani Ratnam, showed a police officer torn between duty and conscience when confronting state violence.

More recently, Article 15 (2019) and Newton (2017) tackled systemic failures without labelling dissent as anti-national. These films did not offer easy answers. They simply asked: is it unpatriotic to demand justice?

What the debate misses

The current political climate often paints any criticism of the government as treason. But these films remind us that India's freedom struggle was built on dissent. Gandhi, Bhagat Singh, and Ambedkar all challenged the state of their time.

Bollywood, for all its commercial excesses, has occasionally captured this truth. Dissent, when rooted in a desire for a better nation, is not anti-national. It is the fuel for progress.

The conversation around patriotism and protest is unlikely to fade. But as long as filmmakers continue to ask uncomfortable questions, the screen will remain a space where dissent is not silenced, but examined.
