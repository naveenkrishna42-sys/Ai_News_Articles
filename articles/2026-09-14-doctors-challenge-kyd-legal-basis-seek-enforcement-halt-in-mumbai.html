Mumbai Doctors Move Against KYD Enforcement

A section of Mumbai's medical community has formally questioned the legal basis of the Know Your Doctor (KYD) verification framework and sought a halt to its enforcement. The challenge, reported by Mumbai Mirror, centres on whether the current regulatory apparatus has the statutory authority to compel practising doctors to complete a verification and display process that many clinics were not originally built to support.

The dispute matters beyond the courtroom. KYD-style verification is designed to give patients a reliable way to confirm a doctor's registration, qualifications and disciplinary record before booking a consultation. If enforcement pauses, that verification layer weakens at exactly the point where patients are most dependent on it.

What KYD Verification Actually Requires

KYD is not a single form. It typically combines identity checks, registration number validation against state medical council records, qualification authentication and periodic re-verification. For a multi-doctor clinic, that means every consultant, visiting specialist and locum must be individually cleared and kept current.

Why Doctors Say the Legal Basis Is Weak

The core argument is procedural, not clinical. Petitioners contend that the enforcement mechanism relies on administrative circulars and platform-level mandates rather than a specific statutory provision that clearly empowers the authority to penalise non-compliance. Without that anchor, they argue, penalties, delisting or suspension of practice privileges become legally vulnerable.

There is also a federal friction. Medical registration is a state subject in practice, while digital health mandates often flow from central advisories. Doctors argue this creates overlapping obligations, with a Mumbai clinic potentially answerable to multiple bodies using different verification standards.

Compliance Cost and Privacy Exposure

Small and mid-sized clinics carry a disproportionate load. A single-speciality clinic with three doctors may spend ₹10,000 to ₹15,000 a year on verification fees alone, plus administrative hours diverted from patient care. Larger hospital chains absorb this easily; standalone practitioners do not.

Privacy is the second fault line. KYD workflows require uploading identity documents, degree certificates and registration proofs to third-party portals. Doctors want clarity on data retention, breach liability and whether verification data can be reused for commercial profiling by booking platforms.

Real-World Impact If Enforcement Halts

  • Patients lose a trust shortcut: Verified badges are a quick signal of legitimacy, especially for first-time consultations and online bookings.
  • Quacks gain room: Weak enforcement historically correlates with a rise in unregistered practitioners, particularly in dense suburban clusters.
  • Platforms face ambiguity: Aggregators may continue voluntary verification to protect their own brand, creating a two-tier system.
  • Hospitals keep verifying: Large chains will likely maintain internal credentialing regardless of the outcome, since insurers and accreditation bodies demand it.
  • Costs shift, not disappear: If the mandate pauses, verification may move from a shared regulatory cost to a private, uneven one.

Buyer and Patient Implications

For patients, the practical advice is unchanged: check the registration number on the state medical council website rather than relying solely on a clinic badge. For clinic owners, the smart move is to maintain verification records even during the dispute, because insurers, corporate tie-ups and accreditation audits will ask for them regardless of the KYD outcome.

For digital health platforms, the commercial risk is reputational. A platform that quietly drops verification to cut costs will struggle to justify premium consultation pricing. Verification is now part of the product, not a back-office formality.

The Road Ahead

The matter now turns on statutory interpretation. If the authority can point to a clear enabling provision, enforcement will likely resume with tighter procedural safeguards. If not, expect a revised framework with explicit legislative backing, defined data-protection clauses and a graded compliance timeline for small clinics.

Either way, the underlying demand from patients for verifiable doctor credentials will not disappear. The question is who pays for that assurance, and under whose legal authority it is enforced.
