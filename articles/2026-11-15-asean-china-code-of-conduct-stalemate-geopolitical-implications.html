South China Sea Code of Conduct: A Decade of Diplomatic Deadlock

Efforts to establish a legally binding Code of Conduct (CoC) for the South China Sea (SCS) have once again faltered, with ASEAN and China failing to reach a conclusive agreement during recent talks. This latest impasse in Manila underscores the persistent, deep-seated disagreements that have plagued negotiations for over two decades, leaving a critical global waterway vulnerable to escalating tensions and legal ambiguity. The failure to formalize a robust framework continues to raise concerns among claimant states and international observers alike, highlighting the precarious balance of power and international law in the Indo-Pacific.

The SCS, a vital artery for global trade and rich in natural resources, is subject to overlapping territorial and maritime claims by China, Vietnam, the Philippines, Malaysia, Brunei, and Taiwan. The absence of an effective CoC means maritime incidents, resource disputes, and freedom of navigation challenges persist without a clear, mutually accepted dispute resolution mechanism.

Adherence to UNCLOS: The Central Sticking Point

At the core of the diplomatic stalemate lies the fundamental disagreement over the interpretation and application of the 1982 United Nations Convention on the Law of the Sea (UNCLOS). Many ASEAN nations, particularly the Philippines, along with international powers like India, insist that any CoC must explicitly conform to UNCLOS principles, which uphold freedom of navigation and define maritime entitlements. Philippine President Ferdinand Marcos Jr. has repeatedly stressed that an effective CoC must be consistent with international law, reflecting the sentiment of nations that view China's expansive claims as incompatible with established legal frameworks.

China, conversely, often frames its claims based on historical maps and what it terms 'indisputable sovereignty,' frequently clashing with UNCLOS-derived exclusive economic zones (EEZs) of other states. Beijing's preferred CoC framework has been perceived by some as an attempt to legitimize its control and restrict the activities of external powers within the SCS, rather than solely a mechanism for de-escalation based on international legal norms.

Geopolitical Ramifications and Regional Stability

The continued failure to conclude a CoC carries significant geopolitical ramifications. It fuels uncertainty, encourages unilateral actions, and increases the likelihood of maritime incidents, such as those involving Chinese coast guard vessels and Philippine supply boats in disputed areas. The lack of a clear framework also empowers China to assert its claims more aggressively, potentially undermining the sovereignty of smaller ASEAN nations.

External powers, including the United States, Japan, and Australia, also maintain a keen interest in the SCS, primarily advocating for the preservation of freedom of navigation and the rules-based international order. India, a significant maritime player in the Indo-Pacific, has consistently reiterated its support for free navigation and adherence to international maritime law, underscoring the broader regional and global implications of the SCS dispute. The absence of a CoC means these external powers continue to conduct freedom of navigation operations, sometimes leading to confrontations with Chinese forces, further complicating the security landscape.

The Path Forward: Sustained Diplomacy Amidst Distrust

Breaking the current diplomatic deadlock requires a significant shift in positions, particularly from China, towards a CoC that genuinely aligns with UNCLOS and respects the sovereign rights of all claimant states. For ASEAN, the challenge lies in maintaining a unified and resolute stance, preventing internal divisions from being exploited, and ensuring that any agreement is truly effective and legally binding rather than merely a symbolic gesture.

Without a robust CoC, the South China Sea will remain a flashpoint, jeopardizing regional peace, economic stability, and the principles of international law. The onus is on all parties, especially the major claimants, to prioritize long-term stability and common prosperity over narrow national interests or historical claims that lack grounding in contemporary international jurisprudence.
