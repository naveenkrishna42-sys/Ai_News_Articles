Rethinking the ‘Spectacle’ of Faith in Public Displays

📅 2026-07-17 📂 Religion Original source ↗
Key points
  • Growing debate over the commercialization of religious events in India
  • Critics argue spectacles dilute the essence of faith
  • Devotees and leaders call for introspection and simpler practices

A Shift in the Air

For decades, grand religious processions, elaborate temple decorations, and massive public gatherings have defined the practice of faith in many parts of India. But a quiet rethinking is underway. From the streets of Varanasi to the corridors of social media, voices are questioning whether the spectacle has overshadowed the spiritual core.

The conversation is not new, but it has gained urgency. The New Indian Express reports that many devotees and religious leaders are now asking a simple question: Has faith become a performance?

The Price of Grandeur

Every year, lakhs of rupees are spent on towering idols, laser shows, and sound systems at religious festivals. Organisers compete for the most impressive display, sometimes turning sacred spaces into entertainment zones. Critics say this focus on scale and glamour distracts from the essence of prayer and inner reflection.

“We have forgotten that faith is personal,” a senior priest told the publication. “It does not need a stage.” The sentiment is echoed by younger devotees who find the noise and crowds overwhelming. They argue that true devotion can exist without the blare of loudspeakers or the glare of spotlights.

Commercialisation Creeps In

The rise of corporate sponsorship has added another layer. Brands now tie up with festivals for visibility, turning religious events into marketing opportunities. While this brings in funds, it also blurs the line between devotion and commerce. Many observers feel this erodes the sanctity of traditions that were once community-driven and volunteer-led.

There is also concern about the environmental toll. Massive temporary structures, non-biodegradable decorations, and waste left behind after events have sparked calls for sustainable practices. Some temples have already begun opting for eco-friendly materials and limiting the scale of celebrations.

Voices for Simplicity

Prominent spiritual figures have started advocating for a return to simplicity. They urge followers to focus on daily rituals, meditation, and charitable acts instead of one-time grand shows. “A quiet prayer at home is worth more than a noisy procession,” a well-known swami said in a recent discourse.

This shift is also visible online. Hashtags like #SimpleFaith and #BackToBasics are gaining traction, with users sharing stories of meaningful personal practices. The movement is not about rejecting tradition, but about refining it—stripping away excess to reveal the substance beneath.

Resistance and Reality

Not everyone is on board. For many communities, large-scale events are a source of pride and identity. They argue that spectacle has always been part of religious expression, from ancient temple festivals to royal processions. The challenge, they say, is to balance grandeur with genuine devotion.

Local economies also depend on these gatherings. Vendors, artists, and small businesses thrive during festival seasons. A sudden shift away from spectacle could hurt livelihoods. Reformers acknowledge this and suggest gradual change, such as reducing noise levels or cutting down on single-use plastics, rather than an abrupt halt.

What Comes Next

The debate is far from settled. As more voices join the conversation, religious institutions are being forced to listen. Some have already formed committees to rethink event guidelines. The coming years may see a quieter, more introspective approach to public faith—one that values meaning over magnitude.
