Scientists drill 33 feet into Alpine glacier to retrieve 2,000-year climate record

📅 2026-07-23 📂 Science Original source ↗
Key points
  • Scientists drilled 33 feet into a vanishing Alpine glacier to recover a 2,000-year climate record.
  • The ice cores contain trapped air bubbles and chemical traces that reveal past temperature and greenhouse gas levels.
  • The research aims to understand how current warming compares to natural climate shifts over two millennia.

Race against time on the ice

A team of glaciologists has successfully drilled 33 feet into a rapidly vanishing Alpine glacier, extracting ice cores that hold a continuous climate record spanning 2,000 years. The operation, conducted in the Swiss Alps, is part of an urgent effort to preserve climate data before the glacier disappears entirely due to rising global temperatures.

The researchers faced treacherous conditions as the glacier, once hundreds of feet thick, has thinned dramatically over the past three decades. Cracks and meltwater streams made the drilling site unstable. Despite these challenges, the team recovered a pristine core that contains trapped air bubbles, pollen, and chemical isotopes.

What the ice reveals

Preliminary analysis of the core shows a detailed timeline of temperature fluctuations, volcanic eruptions, and changes in atmospheric carbon dioxide levels. The ice layers act like a natural archive, with each annual band preserving a snapshot of the climate at the time it formed.

Scientists say the core offers the most precise record yet of how the Alpine region has warmed since the Roman period. Data from the deepest sections indicate that the current rate of warming is unprecedented in the last two millennia. The ice also captured traces of lead pollution from ancient Roman mining and silver production, providing a unique window into human activity.

Melting threat to climate archives

The glacier's rapid melt is a growing concern for paleoclimatologists. Similar ice bodies across the Alps have lost more than half their volume since 1850, and many are expected to vanish within decades. Each lost glacier means the permanent destruction of irreplaceable climate data.

Dr. Elena Moretti, the project lead, told reporters that the team plans to perform high-resolution chemical analysis over the next six months. The goal is to reconstruct seasonal temperature and precipitation patterns with unprecedented accuracy.

Implications for future policy

The findings are expected to strengthen calls for aggressive emissions reductions. By comparing natural climate variability with human-induced warming, the data can help improve climate models that predict future changes for the region. Alpine glaciers supply fresh water to millions of people in Europe, and their decline threatens agriculture, hydropower, and tourism.

The research team is collaborating with the World Glacier Monitoring Service to share the core data with the global scientific community. They hope the record will serve as a baseline for governments drafting climate adaptation strategies.

The ice cores are now stored in a cold laboratory in Zurich, where they will undergo tests for trapped greenhouse gases and isotopic signatures. The results are expected to be published in a peer-reviewed journal by early next year.

What happens next

The team is already planning a follow-up expedition to another endangered glacier in the Mont Blanc massif. With each passing summer, the window to retrieve these ancient climate records narrows. Scientists are racing the melt — and the clock.
