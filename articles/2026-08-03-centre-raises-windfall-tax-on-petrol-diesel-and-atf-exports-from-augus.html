Centre raises windfall tax on petrol, diesel and ATF exports from August 3

📅 2026-08-03 📂 Business Original source ↗
Key points
  • Export levy on petrol, diesel and ATF hiked effective August 3
  • Move follows volatile global oil prices amid Iran war concerns
  • Tax aims to balance domestic supply and export profitability

New Delhi: The central government has raised the windfall tax on exports of petrol, diesel and aviation turbine fuel (ATF), effective from August 3. The move comes as global oil prices remain volatile, exacerbated by geopolitical tensions in West Asia, including the ongoing conflict involving Iran.

What has changed

The export levy on all three fuels has been hiked, though the government has not yet specified the exact new rates in its official notification. According to initial reports, the increase applies to petrol, diesel and ATF shipped out of India, a step aimed at capturing a larger share of the windfall profits that refiners earn when international prices spike.

This is not the first time the Centre has adjusted the windfall tax. Since its introduction in July 2022, the levy has been reviewed periodically, with rates raised or cut depending on the movement of global crude prices and the margin between international product prices and domestic production costs.

Why the hike now

The decision comes against a backdrop of rising crude prices, partly driven by fears of supply disruptions from the Iran conflict. Indian refiners, who export a significant portion of their output, stand to gain from higher global prices. The tax is designed to siphon off a portion of these excess profits while ensuring domestic fuel supplies remain steady.

Industry analysts point out that the timing is critical. With the global market in flux, the government wants to prevent exporters from prioritising overseas sales over local demand. The hike also helps the exchequer earn additional revenue, which can be used for welfare schemes.

Impact on fuel prices

For consumers, the immediate effect is likely to be minimal. The tax applies to exports, not domestic retail sales. However, if the levy reduces export volumes, it could indirectly influence local supply dynamics. Indian oil marketing companies have kept retail petrol and diesel prices unchanged for over a year, and any pressure on their margins could complicate that stance.

Aviation fuel, a major cost for airlines, may see some spillover. While the tax targets exports, a reduction in ATF exports could tighten domestic availability, potentially pushing up prices for carriers. That said, the government has not indicated any change to domestic fuel pricing formulas.

Global context

The hike aligns with similar measures taken by other oil-exporting nations. Countries like India, which are large refiners but not major crude producers, use windfall taxes to balance the interests of the industry and the public. The volatility in oil prices, driven by the Iran war and supply concerns, has made such adjustments more frequent.

Global benchmark Brent crude has been trading in a wide range in recent weeks, reacting to news of strikes and diplomatic efforts. Indian refiners, including Reliance Industries and Nayara Energy, have been among the most active exporters in the region, making them direct targets of the tax.

What to watch

Market participants will closely monitor the next review cycle, expected within a fortnight. If crude prices cool off, the government may roll back the hike. Conversely, if tensions escalate, further increases are possible. For now, exporters will have to factor in the higher levy, which could squeeze their margins on overseas sales.

The government's decision reflects a balancing act—supporting domestic consumers while not discouraging refinery output. With global uncertainties persisting, the windfall tax is likely to remain a dynamic tool in India's fiscal kit.
