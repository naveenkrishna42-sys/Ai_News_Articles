Bollywood films that captured the spirit of protest: Rang De Basanti to Yuva

📅 2026-07-21 📂 Movies Original source ↗
Key points
  • Rang De Basanti (2006) used student activism to question state apathy.
  • Yuva (2004) showed how ordinary people can challenge political violence.
  • Madras Cafe (2013) and Shahid (2013) highlighted dissent in conflict zones.
  • Article 15 (2019) tackled caste-based protest and systemic silence.

Cinema as a mirror to dissent

Bollywood has long grappled with the idea of protest — not as a slogan, but as a lived reality. From the angry young man of the 1970s to the college activist of the 2000s, the Indian screen has often asked: what does it mean to say no?

Films like Rang De Basanti and Yuva did not just depict protest. They became cultural touchstones, sparking debates in hostel rooms, newsrooms, and courtrooms. Here are a few that stood out.

Rang De Basanti (2006): When history met the present

Rakeysh Omprakash Mehra's film wove together two timelines — the revolutionary struggle of Bhagat Singh and the disillusionment of modern Delhi students. Aamir Khan played DJ, a carefree university student who, after a friend's death in a defence corruption scandal, transforms into a real-life revolutionary.

The film did not glorify violence. It asked a simpler question: can apathy be a form of consent? The scene where the friends silently watch a news report about a crashed fighter jet — and then decide to act — became one of the most discussed moments in Indian cinema.

Rang De Basanti sparked actual protests. Students in several cities held candlelight marches demanding justice in the Jessica Lal murder case, directly inspired by the film's message.

Yuva (2004): The politics of the street

Mani Ratnam's Yuva was a sprawling tale of ambition and conscience set against the backdrop of West Bengal politics. Ajay Devgn played Michael, a small-time party worker who witnesses a massacre and decides to stand up against his own party.

The film did not offer easy answers. Michael's protest is messy, personal, and costly. He loses his job, his friends, and nearly his life. Yet the story insists that resistance begins with one person refusing to look away.

Critics noted that Yuva was one of the first mainstream Bollywood films to show that the right to protest is not just a legal right — it is a moral one, especially when institutions fail.

Madras Cafe (2013) and Shahid (2013): Two views of dissent

Both films released the same year, and both dealt with protest in conflict zones. In Madras Cafe, John Abraham played an intelligence officer caught between state secrets and the human cost of war. The film showed that protest in a region like Sri Lanka is never black and white.

Shahid, based on lawyer Shahid Azmi, was more direct. Rajkummar Rao played a human rights lawyer who defended terror suspects. His protest was legal — filing petitions, fighting in court — but it cost him his life. The film asked: what does it mean to dissent when the state calls you a traitor?

Article 15 (2019): The silence before the scream

Anubhav Sinha's Article 15 used the murder of two Dalit sisters in Uttar Pradesh as its starting point. Ayushmann Khurrana played a police officer who initially follows procedure but slowly realises that the system is designed to ignore caste-based violence.

The film's protest was quiet — a walk away from a dinner table, a refusal to file a false report. It showed that sometimes the bravest protest is simply doing your job honestly in a broken system. The film sparked conversations about caste and privilege in urban multiplexes, where such topics are rarely discussed openly.

What the future holds

Bollywood continues to explore protest, but the tone has shifted. Recent films like Jai Bhim and Sardar Udham look back at historical dissent, while smaller films like Bulbbul and Thappad focus on domestic resistance. The audience, too, has changed. Social media now amplifies or kills a film's message within days.

As new films hit the screens, one question remains: will cinema continue to be a space where the right to protest is examined, or will it retreat into safer stories? The answer may define the next decade of Indian storytelling.
