The Prosecutor Endorses His Own Screen Portrait

Ujjwal Nikam, the public prosecutor who secured the conviction of Ajmal Kasab in the 26/11 Mumbai attacks trial, has publicly vouched for Prahaar: The Ujjwal Nikam Story. Speaking about the Rajkummar Rao-starrer, Nikam said the film "shows what actually happened" during the proceedings and stressed that it involves "no sensationalisation."

That statement matters more than a typical celebrity endorsement. In a case where the evidence chain, identification parade and confessional material were dissected for months in a special court, the prosecutor's own reading of the dramatisation carries evidentiary weight for audiences deciding whether to treat the film as history or entertainment.

What Nikam's Sign-Off Actually Signals

Faithfulness over drama

Nikam's central claim is procedural rather than promotional: he argues the film reproduces courtroom sequence rather than inventing one. For viewers, this raises the stakes of the screenwriting choices — the reconstruction of Kasab's capture, the identification proceedings and the confession's admissibility are the beats that would either hold or break his claim of accuracy.

Why prosecutors rarely bless adaptations

Legal professionals usually distance themselves from screen versions of live cases because dramatisation compresses timelines, merges witnesses and flattens objections. Nikam's willingness to be named in the title itself suggests he reviewed the material closely enough to stake his professional reputation on it.

Cast and Launch: The Verifiable Details

Rajkummar Rao heads the cast as Ujjwal Nikam, while Marathi actor Lalit Prabhakar takes on the role of Ajmal Kasab — one of the most heavily scrutinised portrayals an Indian screen actor can attempt. The trailer was launched at Chhatrapati Shivaji Maharaj Terminus by Maharashtra Deputy Chief Minister Devendra Fadnavis, a venue choice that ties the promotion directly to the attacks' geography.

The Countercase: 26/11 as Spectacle

Not everyone shares Nikam's confidence. Rediff's critical piece, headlined "Prahaar: When 26/11 Became A Film Spectacle," argues that converting a massacre into a star vehicle risks converting grief into box-office currency. The counterargument is not that the film is inaccurate, but that accuracy itself can become packaging.

  • Pros: Prosecutor-level endorsement gives the film unusual factual credibility.
  • Pros: Casting a Marathi actor as Kasab signals attention to linguistic and regional texture.
  • Pros: The CSMT launch anchors public memory to the actual site of the attacks.
  • Cons: A title carrying the prosecutor's name can read as hagiography rather than reportage.
  • Cons: Star-casting the lead risks audience sympathy being managed rather than earned.
  • Cons: Survivors' and victims' families have no equivalent voice in the promotional cycle.

How Buyers of This Film Should Watch It

Treat Prahaar as a courtroom reconstruction, not a documentary record. Use Nikam's endorsement as a benchmark for procedural fidelity, then test it against published trial reporting. The film's real value lies in explaining how a terror prosecution actually works — the identification, the confessional evidence, the burden of proof — in a way news coverage rarely sustained for audiences.
