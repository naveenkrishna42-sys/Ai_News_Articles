ISRO faces talent drain to private space firms, NASA's 40-year-old fix offers lessons

📅 2026-07-18 📂 Space Original source ↗
Key points
  • ISRO is experiencing a steady loss of experienced scientists to private space companies like Skyroot and Agnikul.
  • NASA faced a similar talent drain in the 1980s after the Challenger disaster and the rise of the commercial space industry.
  • NASA's solution involved creating a formal commercial crew program, fostering public-private partnerships, and adjusting salary structures.
  • India's space policy 2023 opened the sector to private players, accelerating the demand for ISRO-trained talent.
  • Industry experts suggest ISRO could adopt NASA-like models, including more flexible work arrangements and joint ventures.

The Indian Space Research Organisation (ISRO) is grappling with a problem that has no easy fix in the launchpad or the lab. A growing number of its top scientists and engineers are leaving for the private space sector, drawn by higher pay, faster promotions, and the allure of building rockets for companies like Skyroot Aerospace and Agnikul Cosmos.

This isn't a new phenomenon globally. Forty years ago, NASA faced a similar exodus. The American space agency lost talent to emerging private contractors and startups in the wake of the Challenger disaster and the dawn of the commercial space age. How NASA tackled that crisis offers a blueprint that ISRO might consider today.

The current exodus at ISRO

Senior sources within ISRO confirm that the brain drain has accelerated over the past two years. The space sector in India was opened to private players in 2023 through the Indian Space Policy, creating a sudden demand for experienced rocketeers. Private companies are offering salaries that are often double or triple what ISRO can pay.

One former ISRO scientist who now works at a Bengaluru-based space startup described the shift as inevitable. "At ISRO, you work on a project for a decade. In the private sector, you can build and launch a rocket in three years," he said. The allure of faster career growth and equity stakes is proving hard to resist for younger engineers.

The loss is not just numerical. ISRO is losing mid-career professionals with 10 to 15 years of experience—people who have worked on critical missions like Chandrayaan and Mangalyaan. Their departure creates a knowledge vacuum that fresh hires cannot immediately fill.

How NASA fixed its talent problem 40 years ago

In the 1980s, NASA faced a similar crisis. After the Challenger disaster in 1986, morale plummeted and the agency lost public confidence. At the same time, private companies like McDonnell Douglas and later SpaceX began poaching NASA engineers. The agency was leaking talent at an alarming rate.

NASA's response was not to build walls. Instead, it pivoted to a model of deep public-private partnership. It created the Commercial Crew Program in the 1990s, which allowed private companies to develop spacecraft for NASA missions. This gave NASA engineers a reason to stay—they could work on cutting-edge projects alongside private partners, without losing government benefits.

NASA also introduced more flexible career tracks, including sabbaticals and joint appointments with universities and private firms. It revised its pay structure for critical roles, offering retention bonuses and research grants. The result was a slower, more manageable outflow. Many engineers who left NASA later returned with private-sector experience.

What ISRO can learn from NASA's playbook

Industry insiders say ISRO could adopt several of these measures. One immediate step is to allow ISRO scientists to take sabbaticals to work with private startups, then return without losing seniority. This would turn a loss into a rotation, keeping institutional knowledge intact.

Another lesson is in salary reform. ISRO is a government agency, and its pay scales are tied to central government norms. Experts argue that ISRO needs a separate, more flexible pay structure for its top talent, similar to how NASA was allowed to offer retention bonuses for critical personnel.

There is also a need for more joint projects. ISRO has already begun this with its IN-SPACe initiative, which handholds private companies. But the scale is still small. A formal program that co-develops technology with startups could keep ISRO scientists engaged with the most exciting work in the sector.

The road ahead

ISRO's leadership is aware of the problem. Chairman S. Somanath has publicly acknowledged the challenge and said the organisation is working on new policies to retain talent. But the private sector is moving fast—Skyroot Aerospace alone has hired dozens of former ISRO employees in the last year.

The next few months will be crucial. If ISRO can adapt NASA's 40-year-old solution to India's context, it could turn the brain drain into a brain gain. If not, it risks becoming a training ground for the very companies it helped create.
