Canadian Camper Discovers 390-Million-Year-Old Meteor Crater on Google Maps

📅 2026-07-17 📂 Science Original source ↗
Key points
  • A Canadian man discovered a potential impact crater on Google Maps while planning a camping trip.
  • Scientists confirmed the site is a 390-million-year-old meteor crater, roughly 25 kilometres wide.
  • The crater, named Uhackatik, is located in a rugged, remote region of Quebec, Canada.

A Canadian man planning a camping trip stumbled upon a discovery that has rewritten the geological history of a remote part of Quebec. While scanning Google Maps for a suitable campsite, he noticed a strange, circular depression in the terrain. What he initially thought was a geographical oddity turned out to be a 390-million-year-old meteor impact crater.

The discovery was first reported by PetaPixel and later confirmed by multiple scientific outlets, including Live Science and The Planetary Society. The crater, now named Uhackatik, measures roughly 25 kilometres across and had remained hidden in the rugged Canadian wilderness for millions of years.

The Accidental Discovery

The camper, who has not been publicly identified, was using Google Maps to scout locations for an upcoming trip. He spotted a perfectly circular hole in the satellite imagery of a region known for its dense forests and rocky terrain. Intrigued, he shared the image with local geologists.

Scientists from the University of Quebec and other institutions analysed the site. They confirmed that the depression was not a sinkhole or a volcanic caldera, but a massive impact crater formed by a meteor strike during the Devonian period.

A 390-Million-Year-Old Secret

The crater's age places its formation in the Devonian period, a time when life was just beginning to colonise land. The meteor that created Uhackatik would have been several kilometres wide. Its impact would have caused a cataclysm that reshaped the local landscape and likely triggered a mass extinction event.

Despite its massive size, the crater remained unknown because of the region's inaccessibility. Dense forests and rugged terrain made it invisible to ground surveys. Even aerial surveys had missed it, as the crater's shape is subtle and easily mistaken for natural geological features.

Why It Took So Long to Find

The site is located in a remote part of Quebec, far from major roads and population centres. The area is covered by thick boreal forest, which masks the crater's outline from the ground. Satellite imagery, however, reveals the telltale circular structure that points to an impact origin.

Scientists told Live Science that the crater's preservation is remarkable. Most ancient craters are eroded or buried by sediment over millions of years. Uhackatik's location in a geologically stable region helped it survive intact.

What Makes Uhackatik Unique

At 25 kilometres wide, Uhackatik is now one of the largest known impact craters in Canada. The country is home to several famous craters, including the 1.9-billion-year-old Sudbury Basin, but Uhackatik is among the oldest.

The Planetary Society noted that the crater's discovery highlights the role of citizen science in modern geology. A casual observation on a mapping app led to a major scientific find. Researchers are now planning field expeditions to the site to collect rock samples and study the impact effects in detail.

Officials have not yet confirmed when the first ground survey will take place. The rugged terrain and harsh climate mean expeditions will likely be limited to summer months.

What Happens Next

Geologists are eager to analyse the crater's rock formations. The impact would have created unique minerals and shock features that can reveal details about the meteor's size and speed. The findings could also help scientists understand the frequency of large impacts in Earth's history.

For now, the crater remains a remote curiosity. But thanks to a camper's sharp eye and a free satellite map, a piece of the planet's ancient past has finally come to light. Researchers expect to publish their first peer-reviewed findings within the next year.
