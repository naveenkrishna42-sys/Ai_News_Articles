NATO flags climate change as a security threat, outlines response

📅 2026-07-18 📂 Environment Original source ↗
Key points
  • NATO views climate change as a threat multiplier affecting security.
  • Extreme weather and resource scarcity are cited as direct risks.
  • The alliance is adapting military operations and infrastructure.

NATO has formally recognised climate change and environmental degradation as direct threats to international security, moving beyond traditional military concerns. In a statement released on Thursday, the alliance outlined how rising temperatures, extreme weather, and resource scarcity are reshaping the global security environment.

Climate change as a threat multiplier

NATO officials described climate change as a 'threat multiplier' that exacerbates existing vulnerabilities. Conflicts over water and food, mass displacement, and damage to critical infrastructure are now recurring themes in allied defence planning.

The alliance noted that melting Arctic ice is opening new shipping routes and strategic access points, which could lead to increased military competition. At the same time, desertification and sea-level rise are straining communities in vulnerable regions, creating conditions that could fuel instability.

Operational and infrastructure adaptations

NATO is adjusting its military posture to account for more frequent and severe climate-related events. Training exercises now incorporate scenarios involving extreme heat, floods, and wildfires. Several member states have reported that extreme weather has already disrupted routine operations and damaged bases.

The alliance is also investing in energy efficiency and sustainable fuel alternatives for its forces. A NATO official said that reducing reliance on fossil fuels is not just an environmental goal but a tactical necessity, as fuel convoys remain vulnerable in conflict zones.

Tracking emissions and setting targets

NATO has begun measuring the carbon footprint of its military activities, including those of its command structures and installations. The alliance aims to cut emissions from its non-combat infrastructure by at least 40% by 2030, compared to 2008 levels.

However, emissions from actual combat operations and allied national forces remain outside this target. The alliance has not set a specific goal for reducing overall military emissions, citing operational security and the need for readiness.

Civil-military coordination and early warning

NATO is strengthening its partnerships with civilian agencies to improve disaster response and early warning systems. The alliance has conducted joint exercises with national emergency services to prepare for climate-related catastrophes, such as large-scale floods or wildfires that could affect multiple member states.

Intelligence sharing on climate risks is also being integrated into standard security assessments. NATO's Science for Peace and Security programme now funds research projects focused on climate adaptation and resilience.

Diverging views within the alliance

Not all NATO members view climate action with the same urgency. Some nations, particularly those with large fossil fuel industries, have pushed back against aggressive emission reduction targets for the military. Others argue that climate change must remain a secondary concern amid rising conventional threats from rivals.

The alliance has so far avoided setting binding emission limits on member states' armed forces. The current approach emphasises voluntary adaptation and shared best practices rather than mandates.

NATO is expected to present a more detailed climate action plan at its next summit. Member states will be watched closely for concrete commitments on military decarbonisation and funding for climate resilience projects. The alliance's ability to balance environmental goals with core security requirements remains an open question.
