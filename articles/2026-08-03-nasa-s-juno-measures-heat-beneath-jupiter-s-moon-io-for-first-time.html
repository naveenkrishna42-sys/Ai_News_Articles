NASA's Juno Measures Heat Beneath Jupiter's Moon Io for First Time

📅 2026-08-03 📂 Science Original source ↗
Key points
  • Juno made first direct temperature measurements beneath Io's surface
  • Findings reveal magma heating beneath the most volcanically active world
  • Data offers insights into tidal heating and Io's internal structure

For decades, astronomers have watched Jupiter's moon Io spew fountains of lava and belch volcanic plumes, but they could only guess what was happening beneath its fiery crust. Now, NASA's Juno spacecraft has changed that, delivering the first direct temperature measurements from just below Io's surface.

The findings, announced by scientists from the Southwest Research Institute (SwRI), confirm that Io's interior is far hotter than its exterior, driven by immense tidal forces from Jupiter's gravity. The measurements reveal a hidden layer of heat that fuels the moon's relentless volcanic activity.

Peering Beneath a Fiery Crust

Io, roughly the size of Earth's moon, is the most volcanically active body in the solar system. Its surface is a patchwork of sulfur plains, volcanic pits, and lava lakes, but until now, no spacecraft had directly sampled the temperatures below that crust.

Juno, which has been orbiting Jupiter since 2016, used its microwave radiometer to probe Io's subsurface during close flybys. The instrument measures thermal radiation at multiple wavelengths, allowing scientists to construct a temperature profile at different depths.

According to SwRI scientist Dr. Scott Bolton, the data show a dramatic spike in temperature just a few kilometres beneath the surface, far hotter than the frigid exterior. This heat, he explained, is the engine driving Io's hundreds of active volcanoes.

How Tidal Heating Works

Io's internal furnace is not powered by radioactive decay like Earth's, but by tidal heating. As Io orbits Jupiter, the giant planet's gravity squeezes and stretches the moon, generating friction inside its rocky mantle.

This friction converts mechanical energy into heat, a process that has been theorised for decades but never directly observed. Juno's measurements now provide the first empirical evidence of how this heat is distributed beneath the surface.

The new data suggest that the heat is not evenly spread but concentrated in shallow layers, possibly connected to magma chambers beneath known volcanic hotspots. This could explain why some regions of Io are far more active than others.

A Window into Io's Volcanic Plumbing

The findings have significant implications for understanding Io's internal structure. Scientists have long debated whether the moon has a global magma ocean or a more solid interior with pockets of molten rock.

Juno's temperature readings, combined with gravity data from earlier flybys, point toward a partially molten mantle with localised magma reservoirs. This is consistent with the observation that Io's volcanoes are clustered in specific regions rather than spread uniformly.

The measurements also help refine models of tidal heating, which are crucial for studying other tidally locked worlds, including Europa and Enceladus, both of which harbour subsurface oceans and are prime targets in the search for life.

What the Future Holds

Juno's mission has been extended several times, and the spacecraft continues to make close passes of Io. Each flyby adds more data, sharpening the picture of how heat flows through the moon's interior.

Scientists hope to compare Juno's findings with data from ESA's JUICE mission and NASA's Europa Clipper, both set to explore the Jovian system later this decade. Together, these missions could reveal whether Io's volcanic activity is a unique phenomenon or a common feature of tidally heated moons.

As the analysis continues, researchers are eager to see whether the subsurface heating pattern remains consistent or shifts with Io's volcanic cycles. The next set of Juno flybys could provide the answers.
