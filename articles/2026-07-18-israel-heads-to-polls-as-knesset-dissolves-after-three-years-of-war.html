Israel heads to polls as Knesset dissolves after three years of war

📅 2026-07-18 📂 World Original source ↗
Key points
  • Israel's parliament, the Knesset, has dissolved, triggering a general election on October 27.
  • Prime Minister Benjamin Netanyahu faces voters after three years of ongoing military conflict.
  • The outgoing coalition passed a series of controversial laws widely seen as benefiting Netanyahu's political survival.
  • Netanyahu remains on trial for corruption charges, a key issue in the campaign.

Israel's parliament, the Knesset, officially dissolved on Thursday, setting the stage for a general election on October 27. The vote will be the first since the country entered a prolonged period of war over three years ago, and it pits Prime Minister Benjamin Netanyahu against a fractured opposition.

The dissolution came after the outgoing coalition completed what local media described as a "drastic legislative blitz," passing a raft of bills in its final days. Critics accuse Netanyahu of using these laws to buy political loyalty and shield himself from ongoing corruption trials.

A campaign defined by war and security

The election is being held against the backdrop of a conflict that has reshaped Israeli politics and society. Since October 2023, the country has been locked in a multi-front war involving Hamas in Gaza, Hezbollah in Lebanon, and Iran-backed militias in Syria. Tens of thousands of soldiers remain mobilized, and the economy has struggled under the strain.

Netanyahu's campaign is expected to centre on his experience and security credentials. His Likud party argues that only a strong, right-wing government can see the war through to victory. Opinion polls suggest Likud remains the largest single party, but it is unclear if Netanyahu can muster a majority coalition.

Opposition parties, including the centrist Yesh Atid led by Yair Lapid, are hammering Netanyahu on his handling of the war's early days. The October 7, 2023 attack by Hamas, which killed over 1,200 Israelis, remains a deep national trauma. Lapid has called for a commission of inquiry into the government's failures before that date.

Controversial laws raise concerns

In the weeks before the Knesset dissolved, the coalition passed several pieces of legislation that have drawn sharp criticism. One law reportedly expands government control over judicial appointments, a move opponents say undermines the independence of the courts. Another gives the prime minister greater authority over security appointments.

Human rights groups and legal experts have warned that these changes weaken democratic checks and balances. The United States and European Union have expressed concern, urging Israel to uphold the rule of law. Netanyahu has dismissed the criticism as interference in internal affairs.

The legislative blitz also included measures that appear tailored to specific coalition partners. One bill provides financial benefits to ultra-Orthodox yeshiva students, a key constituency for Netanyahu's religious allies. Another allocates funds to settlements in the occupied West Bank.

Corruption trial looms large

Netanyahu is currently on trial for bribery, fraud, and breach of trust in three separate cases. He denies all charges and claims he is the victim of a political witch hunt. The trial has been a background issue for years, but with the election approaching, it is likely to take centre stage.

Opposition leaders have said they will not serve in a government headed by an indicted prime minister. However, Netanyahu's coalition partners have signalled they would support legislation granting him immunity from prosecution. Such a move would require a majority in the next Knesset.

Legal analysts say the timing of the election could work in Netanyahu's favour. The trial is not expected to reach a verdict for at least another year, meaning he will not face a conviction before the vote.

What comes next

Campaigning will officially begin in the coming weeks, with parties scrambling to finalize their lists and platforms. A key question is whether the centre-left and Arab-majority parties can unite to form a viable alternative to Netanyahu's bloc. In the last five elections, no single bloc has been able to secure a stable majority.

If the race remains tight, Israel could face yet another prolonged political deadlock — or a sixth election in under a decade. All eyes will be on October 27.
