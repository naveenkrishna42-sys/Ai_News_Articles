AI 'shrink ray' miniaturises proteins, unlocking new medical possibilities

📅 2026-07-31 📂 Science Original source ↗
Key points
  • AI tool 'Raygun' can redesign proteins to be smaller while retaining function.
  • Researchers demonstrated the technique on the Botox enzyme, creating a smaller version.
  • Miniaturised proteins could improve drug delivery, reduce side effects, and accelerate research.

Molecular shrink ray

Scientists have developed an AI tool that can miniaturise natural proteins, effectively acting like a molecular 'shrink ray'. The technique, described in the journal Nature, allows researchers to redesign proteins to be smaller while preserving their key functions.

The tool, named Raygun, was created by a team at Duke School of Medicine. It works by taking a natural protein and compressing its structure, much like reducing the size of a machine without losing its core capabilities.

According to the researchers, this approach could have far-reaching implications, from improving drug delivery to enabling new kinds of biological research.

Why shrink a protein?

Proteins are the workhorses of biology, carrying out countless tasks in the body. Their size and shape determine how they interact with other molecules. Smaller versions of natural proteins could be easier to produce, more stable, and better able to penetrate tissues that larger proteins cannot reach.

In medicine, this could mean drugs that are more targeted, with fewer side effects. For researchers, it could simplify experiments that involve studying protein behaviour in cells or animals.

Botox enzyme as a test case

The Duke team put their AI to the test by redesigning the enzyme responsible for Botox's effects. Botox, a neurotoxin used both cosmetically and therapeutically, works by blocking nerve signals. The enzyme is large and complex, making it a challenging candidate for miniaturisation.

Raygun successfully produced a smaller version of the enzyme that still functioned as intended. This demonstration suggests the method could be applied to many other proteins, not just this one.

The idea is not just to shrink proteins, but to do so intelligently. The AI identifies parts of the protein that are essential and parts that can be trimmed without breaking the overall structure.

What this unlocks

Miniaturised proteins could open new avenues in biotechnology. For instance, they might be engineered to target specific cells or to carry therapeutic molecules with greater precision.

Smaller proteins are also easier to synthesise in the lab, which could lower production costs for protein-based medicines. This is especially relevant for treatments that are currently expensive or difficult to manufacture at scale.

Beyond medicine, miniaturised enzymes could be used in industrial processes, such as breaking down pollutants or manufacturing chemicals more efficiently.

Not the first AI protein tool

Raygun is part of a broader wave of AI-driven protein design. Other tools, such as AlphaFold, have already transformed how scientists predict protein structures. Raygun's novelty lies in its ability to redesign proteins for reduced size, a task that has proven difficult with traditional methods.

The researchers note that Raygun is not a universal solution. Some proteins may not be amenable to shrinking, and the technique requires careful validation to ensure the redesigned proteins behave as expected.

Still, the potential is significant. As AI tools become more sophisticated, they are likely to become standard instruments in molecular biology labs.

What's next?

The Duke team plans to test Raygun on a wider range of proteins and to explore its use in creating novel proteins that do not exist in nature. Clinical applications, if any, would take years to develop, but the research marks a clear step forward.

Observers will be watching to see how quickly this technology translates from the lab to practical use, and whether it can be combined with other AI tools to accelerate drug discovery.
