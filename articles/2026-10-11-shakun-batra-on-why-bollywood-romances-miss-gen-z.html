Shakun Batra on why Bollywood romances miss Gen Z

✍️ By TIVRA News Editorial Desk 📅 2026-10-11 📂 Entertainment Original source ↗
Key points
  • Bollywood romances often simplify love into destiny beats, while Gen Z expects emotional realism and lived-in conflict.
  • Younger audiences relate to relationships that mirror contemporary realities—identity, communication, and power dynamics—not just romance arcs.
  • Directorial craft (writing, dialogue, pacing, and character interiority) matters as much as casting and music when connecting with Gen Z.

Shakun Batra’s core critique: love stories that stop short of life

Director Shakun Batra has pointed to a discomfort young viewers feel with the way Bollywood romances are written and staged. The central issue, as echoed across coverage from NDTV and other outlets, is not that audiences dislike love stories. It’s that many depictions fail to carry the emotional complexity of real relationships—where the “soulmate” moment is rarely followed by a trouble-free life.

For Gen Z, romance is less about a single grand feeling and more about how people navigate communication gaps, anxiety, ambition, and changing identities over time. When films collapse these realities into tidy, simplified arcs, the audience reads it as fiction without emotional purchase, even if the setting is familiar.

What younger audiences look for: emotional realism over “destiny packaging”

Bollywood has long relied on romance formulas: fated attraction, song-driven escalation, and cathartic resolutions. Batra’s critique suggests a mismatch between that structure and how younger audiences understand intimacy—through conversations, subtle discomforts, and the friction that tests compatibility.

Gen Z tends to reward specificity. If a film wants to sell the idea that love heals, it has to show how healing actually works: consent, negotiation, repair after conflict, and the long tail of emotional growth. Without that, a romance can feel like an advertisement for an ideal rather than a lived story.

There’s also a tonal expectation shift. Younger viewers often consume content where characters are allowed to be messy—contradictory, insecure, funny, defensive, and still relatable. When a screenplay smooths all rough edges in the service of cinematic romance, characters can become emotionally underwritten.

Where films typically miss the mark: writing, language, and relationship power

Batra’s comments, including the observation that films do a disservice by simplifying love, land on a practical filmmaking truth: romance is a writing problem before it becomes a direction or casting problem. The screenplay decides what characters say, what they don’t say, and what they assume. Gen Z is highly attuned to gaps between what’s felt and what’s communicated—especially in the digital age.

Another recurring failure point is how romance scripts treat agency. Many mainstream love stories still position one partner as the emotional instrument that “completes” the other. Younger audiences increasingly expect mutual growth: both characters should want things, set boundaries, and face consequences. When the relationship becomes one-sided in emotional labour, it stops reading as love and starts reading as wish fulfilment.

Dialogue also matters. Romance that leans on poetic generalities can feel generic to viewers raised on fast, specific, and sometimes awkward exchanges in real-life conversations and online culture. The more a film speaks in slogans instead of subtext, the less believable intimacy feels on screen.

Buyer’s lens: what this means for producers and viewers deciding what to fund or watch

For commercial decision-makers, the takeaway is actionable: romance budgets are being judged not only on star power and songs, but on character credibility. If the target is Gen Z, the “hook” must survive after the trailer fades—meaning scripts must be designed for emotional re-engagement, not just one-time fandom.

Producers should pressure-test romance narratives against a simple buyer reality: would a viewer feel seen, even during conflict? Marketing can capture attention, but writing determines whether the audience stays for the emotional payoff. The relationship has to behave like a story people recognize, not just like a template that looks familiar.

For viewers, Batra’s remarks also explain why some love stories feel dated. You can enjoy romance while still sensing an emotional mismatch. The film’s job is to translate intimacy into believable actions and consequences—especially in moments where love doesn’t magically erase stress, miscommunication, or fear.

  • Pros: Gen Z is receptive to romance when it’s grounded in conflict, repair, and consent-driven emotional growth.
  • Pros: Better dialogue and character interiority can make mainstream romance feel “modern” without abandoning its appeal.
  • Pros: Relationships built on mutual agency travel further through word-of-mouth because they spark discussion.
  • Cons: Over-simplified soulmate arcs can feel manipulative—audiences sense emotional shortcuts.
  • Cons: Generic dialogue and tidy resolutions reduce relatability, even with strong music and aesthetics.

Practical checklist for romance projects targeting Gen Z

Based on Batra’s framing and the patterns that typically cause disconnect, producers and writers can use a simple development checklist. It focuses on what the audience tests most: whether characters behave like real people under emotional pressure.

Relationship pacingDo not rush from attraction to destiny; allow time for doubt, negotiation, and evolving trust.

What “connects” ultimately looks like

Shakun Batra’s argument, as carried by NDTV and reinforced by other reporting, points to a plain expectation: Gen Z wants romance that respects the messiness of being human. Love can be magical, but it doesn’t erase reality. When films treat love as a clean emotional transaction, they risk losing the very audience that came to feel something real.
