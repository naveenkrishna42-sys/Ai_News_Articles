US, Russia, China top list of nations behind tracked space debris

📅 2026-08-05 📂 Science Original source ↗
Key points
  • US, Russia and China account for the majority of tracked space debris.
  • Over a tonne of space debris falls to Earth every week, according to a report.
  • Recent incidents, including a metal ring landing in Kenya, highlight the growing risk.

The growing tally of orbital junk

Space debris has become one of the most pressing challenges for the global space industry, and a new analysis has put numbers to the problem. The United States, Russia and China top the list of countries responsible for the most tracked space debris, according to data compiled from tracking networks and recent reports.

The figures come amid growing concern over the safety of satellites, spacecraft and even people on the ground. With thousands of defunct satellites, spent rocket stages and fragments from collisions now circling the Earth, the risk of uncontrolled reentries is no longer theoretical.

Who is responsible for the debris?

The US leads the tally, largely due to decades of launches by NASA, the Department of Defense and private companies. Russia follows closely, with a legacy of Soviet-era hardware and more recent anti-satellite tests that have added to the cloud of fragments. China's rapid expansion in space, including crewed missions and satellite constellations, has also placed it among the top contributors.

India, while a relatively newer entrant to the space race, also features on the list. The country's growing launch cadence and satellite deployments have added to the global debris population, though its share remains smaller compared to the top three. Other nations with significant space programmes, including Japan and members of the European Space Agency, also contribute to the tracked debris.

A tonne a week falling to Earth

The scale of the problem was underscored in a recent report that claimed over a tonne of debris now falls to Earth every week. Most of this material burns up harmlessly in the atmosphere, but not always. Earlier this year, a metal ring from a rocket stage crashed into a village in Kenya, sparking local alarm and drawing global attention to the issue.

Experts have warned that the risk of injury or death from falling debris is low but not negligible. One analysis suggested that SpaceX debris could hurt or kill a person within the next two years if current trends continue. The probability remains small, but the consequences are severe enough that regulators and operators are being urged to act.

Uncontrolled reentries and big falls

Most debris reentries are uncontrolled, meaning no one can predict exactly where the pieces will land. Historical examples include the 1979 fall of NASA's Skylab, which scattered debris across Western Australia, and the 1991 reentry of the Soviet Salyut 7 station. More recently, China's Long March 5B rocket core stages have made headlines for their large, uncontrolled descents over populated areas.

The 10 biggest uncontrolled reentries in history, tracked by organisations like KeepTrack, show a pattern: large objects tend to survive reentry in pieces, and those pieces can travel hundreds of kilometres from each other. The challenge is that tracking systems can predict a reentry window of only a few hours, leaving little time for ground alerts.

What is being done?

International guidelines recommend that satellites be deorbited within 25 years of mission end, but compliance is patchy. Newer constellations, such as SpaceX's Starlink, have committed to lower orbits that decay faster, reducing long-term debris. Yet the sheer volume of launches means the problem is likely to grow before it shrinks.

Efforts are underway to develop active debris removal technologies, including nets, harpoons and robotic arms. Several missions have demonstrated the ability to capture and deorbit defunct satellites, but none have yet scaled to the level needed to make a dent in the estimated 9,000 tonnes of debris currently in orbit.

As more countries and companies enter space, the list of contributors to orbital junk will only lengthen. The question is whether the international community can agree on binding rules before a major accident forces the issue.
