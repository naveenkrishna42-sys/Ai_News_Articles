Dr. C. Rangarajan Warns Against RRB Mergers, Urges RBI to Support SFBs

✍️ By TIVRA News Markets & Finance Desk 📅 2026-09-07 📂 Banking Original source ↗
Key points
  • Former RBI Governor C. Rangarajan states that reducing the number of RRBs is a step in the wrong direction.
  • Consolidation dilutes the localized focus of rural banks, limiting credit access for marginal farmers.
  • Rangarajan urges the RBI to provide regulatory incentives to Small Finance Banks (SFBs) to bridge the rural credit gap.

Why Fewer RRBs Threaten Grassroots Credit Delivery

The consolidation strategy initiated by the government aimed to improve the financial viability of Regional Rural Banks (RRBs) by merging loss-making entities with stronger counterparties. This consolidation exercise dramatically reduced the total number of RRBs from 196 in the late 1990s to just 43. However, veteran economist and former Reserve Bank of India (RBI) Governor Dr. C. Rangarajan warns that this massive reduction in institutional numbers is a step in the wrong direction.

Dr. Rangarajan argues that the core strength of RRBs lies in their localized character and deep understanding of regional dynamics. When these banks are amalgamated into larger, geographically dispersed entities, they lose their close ties with the local population. This structural shift forces RRBs to adopt administrative practices similar to large scheduled commercial banks, ultimately alienating small and marginal farmers who rely on informal and relations-based credit evaluation.

The dilution of local focus directly impacts credit delivery efficiency. Sponsor banks often impose standardized credit policies on consolidated RRBs, ignoring localized agricultural cycles and unique regional craft industries. Instead of continuing aggressive consolidation, policy focus should prioritize capital recapitalization of individual RRBs while preserving their decentralized operating structures.

The Case for Incentivizing Small Finance Banks (SFBs)

To offset the shrinking footprint of localized RRBs, Dr. Rangarajan advocates for a highly supportive regulatory framework for Small Finance Banks. SFBs have proven to be effective vehicles for financial inclusion, but they face high operational expenses and rigid compliance burdens. By offering specific policy relaxations, the RBI can encourage these institutions to expand deep into unbanked and underbanked territories.

Unlike commercial banks, SFBs operate under a mandate to deploy 75% of their Adjusted Net Bank Credit (ANBC) to priority sector lending. Furthermore, at least 50% of their loan portfolio must consist of small-ticket loans up to ₹25 lakh. These stringent criteria, combined with a high minimum capital requirement of ₹200 crore, place immense pressure on their net interest margins. Targeted incentives, such as lower Cash Reserve Ratio (CRR) mandates for rural branches or dedicated refinancing windows, would enable SFBs to lower borrowing costs for rural enterprises.

RRBs vs. SFBs: Structural and Regulatory Comparison

Evaluating the structural parameters of RRBs and SFBs highlights the operational challenges and regulatory distinctions that define both institutional frameworks in India's banking sector.

Specification / Metric Regional Rural Banks (RRBs) Small Finance Banks (SFBs)
Ownership Structure Central Govt (50%), State Govt (15%), Sponsor Bank (35%) Private promoters, public shareholders, and institutional investors
Minimum Capital Requirement Varies by recapitalization schemes backed by sponsors ₹200 crore minimum paid-up equity capital
Priority Sector Lending Target 75% of total outstanding advances 75% of Adjusted Net Bank Credit (ANBC)
Primary Target Demographics Small farmers, agricultural laborers, rural artisans Small business units, micro industries, unorganized sector entities

Key Policy Insights and Operational Implications

  • Loss of Localized Credit Intelligence: Consolidating RRBs increases their geographical jurisdiction, making it difficult for branch managers to maintain the community relationships necessary to assess informal credit risk without collateral.
  • High Compliance Costs for SFBs: SFBs lack the low-cost deposit franchise enjoyed by public-sector RRBs, facing commercial borrowing rates that elevate the cost of capital for rural micro-loans.
  • Tech Integration Bottlenecks: While SFBs utilize modern digital lending platforms to manage small-ticket portfolios, consolidated RRBs frequently struggle with legacy IT infrastructures across disparate state-level branches.
  • The Need for Hybrid Co-Lending: To sustain credit flows, the RBI must establish seamless co-lending partnerships between technology-driven SFBs and capital-rich commercial sponsor banks.
