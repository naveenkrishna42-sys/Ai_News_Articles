Iran hardliners allege 'coup' after US agreement, deepen leadership rift

📅 2026-07-19 📂 Top Stories Original source ↗
Key points
  • Hardliners allege a 'coup' after the US agreement, accusing leaders of betrayal.
  • Slogans like 'Death to the compromiser' highlight growing public anger.
  • The rift deepens as the US truce faces pressure from within Iran.
  • Analysts warn of potential instability if internal divisions are not resolved.

Hardliners Cry Coup After US Deal

Iran's hardline factions are accusing the government of orchestrating a 'coup' following the recent agreement with the United States. The deal, which was meant to ease tensions, has instead sparked fury among conservatives who see it as a betrayal of revolutionary principles.

Reports from Tehran indicate that protesters have taken to the streets, chanting slogans like 'Death to the compromiser' and 'Us, Blade, Your Throat'. These chants are directed at the leadership that signed the pact, which hardliners claim undermines Iran's sovereignty.

Leadership Under Fire

The agreement, which was brokered after months of negotiations, was intended to lift sanctions in exchange for nuclear concessions. But for hardliners, it represents a surrender to American pressure. They argue that the deal was pushed through without proper consultation, amounting to a 'coup' against the country's foundational ideals.

Senior figures in the Islamic Revolutionary Guard Corps (IRGC) have publicly criticised the government, with some calling for the resignation of key negotiators. 'This is not a deal; it's a dagger aimed at the heart of the revolution,' said one hardline cleric during a Friday sermon in Qom.

Deepening Divisions

The rift within Iran's leadership is now out in the open. Moderate factions, led by President Hassan Rouhani, argue that the deal is necessary for economic survival. But hardliners, backed by the IRGC and conservative religious figures, view it as a capitulation that will only embolden Washington.

This internal conflict has paralysed decision-making in Tehran. Sources close to the Supreme Leader say Ayatollah Khamenei is trying to mediate, but his silence on the matter has only fuelled speculation about where his loyalties lie.

Economic Pressures Mount

Behind the political drama lies a stark economic reality. Iran's economy has been crushed by years of sanctions, with inflation running high and unemployment rising. The government had hoped the US agreement would bring relief, but hardliners argue that the deal fails to guarantee long-term benefits.

Protesters in Tehran's bazaar, a traditional bastion of conservative support, have shut down shops in protest. 'We don't need bread from America; we need dignity,' one shopkeeper told local media. This sentiment resonates with many Iranians who feel the deal compromises national pride.

What Comes Next

The coming weeks will test whether the Iranian leadership can contain the backlash. If hardliners succeed in framing the deal as a 'coup', it could lead to a constitutional crisis or even a change in government policy. For now, the fate of the US agreement hangs in the balance, as does the stability of Iran's political system.
