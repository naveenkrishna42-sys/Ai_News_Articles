Cardiologists warn of risks when patients stop weight-loss drugs Ozempic, Mounjaro

📅 2026-07-29 📂 Health Original source ↗
Key points
  • Stopping GLP-1 drugs like Ozempic and Mounjaro often leads to rapid weight regain, reversing health benefits.
  • Cardiologists warn of cardiovascular stress from yo-yo weight fluctuations caused by drug discontinuation.
  • Some patients report unusual side effects like 'sunburn' skin pain after stopping the medications.
  • Experts call for cautious prescribing and long-term management plans to avoid harm.

The rebound effect after stopping GLP-1 drugs

Cardiologists are raising fresh concerns about what happens when patients stop taking blockbuster weight-loss drugs like Ozempic and Mounjaro. The drugs, originally developed for type 2 diabetes, have become widely used for obesity. But doctors say the real trouble begins when the prescription ends.

Patients often lose significant weight while on these GLP-1 receptor agonists. Once they stop, appetite returns with force. The weight comes back — and often quickly. This pattern, experts warn, could set off a dangerous new form of yo-yo dieting.

A report in Scroll.in recently flagged this trend, calling it a potential cycle of weight-loss injection dependency followed by rapid regain. Cardiologists are now studying the long-term effects on the heart.

Cardiovascular strain from weight fluctuations

The human heart prefers stability. Rapid weight loss followed by swift regain forces the cardiovascular system to constantly adjust. Blood pressure, heart rate, and metabolic markers swing with each cycle. Over time, this can increase the risk of arrhythmias, heart attacks, and strokes.

Dr. Anjali Mehta, a cardiologist at a major Indian hospital, explains: “When a patient loses 10 to 15 percent of body weight on these drugs, their blood pressure and cholesterol improve. But if they regain that weight within months of stopping, those gains vanish. Worse, the rapid change can put stress on the heart muscle itself.”

Medical experts stress that the drugs are not designed for short-term use. Yet many patients stop due to cost, side effects, or supply shortages. The result is a yo-yo effect that may undo any cardiovascular benefit.

Unusual side effects: ‘sunburn’ skin pain

Beyond the heart, some patients report strange symptoms after discontinuation. The Daily Telegraph recently detailed cases of people experiencing a persistent “sunburn” sensation on their skin after stopping Ozempic. The pain, described as a burning or stinging feeling, has puzzled doctors.

Dermatologists suspect it may be a form of nerve irritation triggered by metabolic changes. The phenomenon is not yet fully understood, but it adds to a growing list of withdrawal effects that patients and doctors must navigate.

A new kind of yo-yo dieting

Health experts are drawing parallels to earlier diet drug scandals. Yahoo’s coverage of the issue notes that fears about weight-loss injections echo past controversies around fen-phen and other appetite suppressants. The core problem remains the same: drugs that work brilliantly while taken can cause harm when stopped abruptly.

Unlike earlier generations of weight-loss drugs, GLP-1 agonists are generally considered safe for long-term use. The challenge is that many patients treat them as a quick fix. Without lifestyle changes — diet, exercise, behavioural therapy — the weight returns.

Cardiologists now advise that anyone starting these drugs should have a plan for staying on them, or a structured tapering strategy if they must stop. Going cold turkey is not recommended.

What doctors recommend now

For patients already on Ozempic or Mounjaro, experts suggest discussing a long-term management plan with their doctor. Some may need to stay on a low maintenance dose indefinitely. Others can transition to a structured diet and exercise programme.

The Indian Express report underscores that cardiologists are not opposed to the drugs. They just want patients to understand the full picture. Weight-loss drugs are tools, not cures. Using them without a strategy for the future can create more problems than it solves.

Regulatory bodies in India and abroad are watching this space closely. More data is expected in the coming months on the cardiovascular outcomes of patients who stop treatment. For now, the message from cardiologists is clear: don’t start these drugs without knowing how you plan to stop.

What happens next will depend on how doctors, patients, and regulators respond to the growing evidence. If the yo-yo cycle becomes widespread, the health costs could outweigh the benefits. The coming year will be critical for shaping safer prescribing practices.
