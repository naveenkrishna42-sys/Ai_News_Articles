First Human Gets Experimental Vaccine for Rare Ebola Strain

📅 2026-07-26 📂 Health Original source ↗
Key points
  • First human volunteer injected with experimental Bundibugyo Ebola vaccine
  • Vaccine developed by Oxford University and Serum Institute of India
  • Trial begins amid ongoing Ebola outbreak in Congo

A volunteer in the United Kingdom has become the first person to receive an experimental vaccine targeting a rare strain of the Ebola virus. The shot, developed jointly by the University of Oxford and the Serum Institute of India, is designed to protect against the Bundibugyo strain.

The trial marks a significant step in global efforts to prepare for future outbreaks. Bundibugyo Ebola is less common than the Zaire strain but has caused lethal outbreaks in Africa.

Why a New Vaccine Was Needed

Existing Ebola vaccines primarily target the Zaire strain. They have been deployed effectively during recent outbreaks in the Democratic Republic of Congo and elsewhere. However, those vaccines do not generate a strong immune response against the Bundibugyo strain.

Health experts have long warned that a dedicated vaccine for Bundibugyo is a critical gap. The current trial aims to fill that gap by testing a candidate specifically tailored to the strain's unique viral proteins.

The Trial and the Volunteers

The first volunteer received the injection at a clinical research facility in Oxford. Researchers plan to enroll several dozen healthy adults in the initial phase of the study. The primary goal is to assess safety and the immune response generated by the vaccine.

Scientists will monitor participants for several months. If the results are promising, larger trials could follow in regions where the Bundibugyo strain has historically circulated.

A Collaboration with Indian Expertise

The Serum Institute of India, based in Pune, is a key partner in the development and manufacturing of the experimental vaccine. The institute has extensive experience producing vaccines at scale for global distribution.

Oxford researchers have previously collaborated with Serum Institute on other vaccine projects, including the Oxford-AstraZeneca COVID-19 vaccine. The partnership brings together cutting-edge academic research with large-scale manufacturing capability.

Context of the Ongoing Outbreak

The trial comes at a time when the Democratic Republic of Congo is battling an Ebola outbreak. That outbreak is driven by the Zaire strain, against which existing vaccines are effective. However, the development of a Bundibugyo vaccine is seen as a proactive measure.

Bundibugyo Ebola first emerged in Uganda in 2007. It has a lower fatality rate than the Zaire strain but still poses a significant public health threat. No licensed vaccine currently exists for this strain.

Health officials have not confirmed whether the Congo outbreak involves any Bundibugyo cases. Surveillance efforts remain focused on containment with existing tools.

What Happens Next

The trial is expected to yield initial safety data within a few months. If the vaccine proves safe and immunogenic, discussions will begin about phase 2 and phase 3 trials, likely in African countries at risk of Bundibugyo outbreaks. Researchers and public health agencies will watch closely for any signs of the strain's spread.
