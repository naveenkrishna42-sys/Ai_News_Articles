RBI Draft Rules: External Benchmark Optional for NBFCs

📅 2026-08-13 📂 Banking Original source ↗
Key points
  • RBI draft rules propose external benchmark as optional for NBFCs, not mandatory.
  • Move aims to balance flexibility for NBFCs with improved interest rate transmission.
  • Final norms expected after stakeholder feedback; industry sees it as a pragmatic step.

The Reserve Bank of India (RBI) has released draft rules that make external benchmarking of interest rates optional for non-banking financial companies (NBFCs), a shift from earlier expectations of a mandatory requirement. The draft, published on Wednesday, seeks to streamline how NBFCs set and adjust lending rates, while keeping the framework flexible enough for the sector's diverse business models.

What the draft proposes

Under the proposed norms, NBFCs will not be compelled to link their floating-rate loans to an external benchmark such as the repo rate or Treasury bill yields. Instead, they may choose to adopt such a benchmark voluntarily. This departs from the stricter stance applied to banks, where external benchmarking is compulsory for most floating-rate retail and MSME loans.

The RBI has, however, laid down conditions for NBFCs that opt for external benchmarks. They must ensure transparency in the spread charged over the benchmark, and any changes in the benchmark must be passed on to borrowers without undue delay. The draft also requires NBFCs to disclose the methodology used for rate revision on their websites and in loan agreements.

Why the regulator is taking a softer line

Sources indicate that the RBI's decision to keep the requirement optional stems from the heterogeneity of the NBFC sector. Unlike banks, which have a uniform liability structure, NBFCs raise funds from a mix of bank borrowings, bonds, and deposits, making a one-size-fits-all approach impractical. For many smaller NBFCs, linking loans to an external benchmark may not align with their cost of funds, which often includes a fixed-rate component.

By making it optional, the RBI appears to be nudging the industry towards better rate transmission without imposing a rigid framework. The draft also proposes a review mechanism where the RBI can step in if it finds that an NBFC's internal benchmark is not being applied consistently or is leading to delayed transmission of policy rate changes.

Industry reactions

Banking and NBFC circles have largely welcomed the draft. Industry bodies have long argued that a mandatory external benchmark could squeeze margins for NBFCs, especially those operating in niche segments like microfinance or infrastructure lending. The optionality, they say, provides breathing room while still encouraging adoption of more transparent pricing.

However, some consumer rights groups have expressed disappointment, arguing that the absence of a mandate could leave borrowers at the mercy of opaque internal benchmarks. They point out that banks were initially reluctant to shift to external benchmarks and only complied after the RBI made it compulsory. The regulator has not yet commented on this criticism.

What happens next

The RBI has invited comments on the draft from stakeholders, with a deadline of 30 days from publication. After considering the feedback, the central bank will issue final guidelines. It is expected that the final norms will retain the optional feature, though the RBI may tighten disclosure requirements further.

For now, NBFCs can continue with their existing rate-setting methods. But those that choose to adopt external benchmarks will need to ensure strict compliance with the new rules once they are finalised. The move is being seen as part of the RBI's broader push to make interest rate transmission faster and more predictable across the financial system.

As the consultation period begins, all eyes will be on how NBFCs respond and whether the regulator tweaks the draft in response to feedback. The final guidelines are likely to be issued within the next few months, and they could set the tone for how the sector prices credit in the coming years.
