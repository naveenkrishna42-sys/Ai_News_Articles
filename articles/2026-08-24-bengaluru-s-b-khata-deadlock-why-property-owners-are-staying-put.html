Bengaluru’s B-Khata Deadlock: Why Property Owners Are Staying Put

The Conversion Crisis

Bengaluru’s property market is currently grappling with a severe policy bottleneck. Despite the government’s push to bring B-khata properties into the formal A-khata fold, the response has been lackluster at best. Recent data indicates that a mere 5.5 per cent of eligible property owners have engaged with the conversion process.

This low adoption rate is not merely a sign of administrative apathy. It reflects a deep-seated distrust among homeowners regarding the long-term benefits of the conversion against the immediate, steep financial costs. For many, the B-khata tag has become a permanent, albeit frustrating, fixture of Bengaluru real estate.

Why Owners Are Hesitating

The core issue lies in the financial burden imposed on property owners. Conversion is not just a bureaucratic shift; it requires the payment of significant penalties and betterment charges. In an economic climate where homeowners are already managing rising maintenance and utility costs, the additional lump sum is a deterrent.

Furthermore, the documentation requirements are notoriously opaque. Many owners fear that initiating the process will invite scrutiny on past building violations, potentially leading to demolition notices or further fines rather than the desired khata upgrade. The risk-reward ratio, as viewed by the average owner, is currently skewed toward staying in the status quo.

Key Barriers to Compliance

  • High penalty charges for regularization.
  • Complex documentation causing delays and confusion.
  • Fear of retrospective scrutiny on building plan approvals.
  • Lack of clear incentives for those moving to A-khata.

The Broader Market Impact

The persistent prevalence of B-khata properties creates a dual-tier market in Bengaluru. Properties with A-khata status command a premium and are easily mortgageable. Conversely, B-khata properties often languish in the resale market, frequently restricted to cash transactions and lacking institutional financing support.

By failing to convert, property owners are effectively capping the value of their own assets. However, the government must also shoulder the blame for failing to create a transparent, time-bound, and affordable pathway to compliance. Until the state can guarantee that the conversion process is a one-time settlement that cleans the slate, most owners will prefer the legal gray zone over the uncertainty of the application office.

The current data suggests that the scheme, in its present form, is failing to meet its primary objective of formalizing the city's housing stock. Without a policy pivot that addresses the penalty structure, Bengaluru is likely to remain a city defined by its khata divide for years to come.
