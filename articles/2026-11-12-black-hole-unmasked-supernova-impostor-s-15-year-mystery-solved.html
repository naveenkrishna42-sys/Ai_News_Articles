Unveiling a Cosmic Deception: The Supernova Impostor Mystery

For over 15 years, astronomers were baffled by a distant star, designated AT 2016blu, which repeatedly flared with the intensity of a dying sun. These events, occurring every 113 days, mimicked a rare type of stellar explosion known as a "supernova impostor." Unlike true supernovae, which are catastrophic, singular events marking a star's final moments, impostors are transient outbursts where the star survives.

The persistent, recurring nature of AT 2016blu's eruptions defied conventional explanations for these bright, yet non-terminal, stellar events. Astrophysicists grappled with models that could account for such regular, powerful energy releases without destroying the star itself. The enigma underscored a significant gap in our understanding of extreme stellar behavior.

Chandra's X-Ray Gaze Pinpoints the Culprit

The breakthrough came through meticulous observations by NASA's Chandra X-Ray Observatory. Chandra, renowned for its unparalleled sensitivity to X-ray emissions, turned its focus on AT 2016blu. X-ray astronomy is uniquely suited to detect phenomena involving highly energetic processes, such as matter being heated to millions of degrees as it spirals into a compact object.

The Chandra data revealed a distinct, powerful X-ray source emanating from the vicinity of AT 2016blu. This X-ray signature was not consistent with the star itself, but rather with a compact companion. The evidence strongly pointed towards a hidden black hole, gravitationally bound to AT 2016blu, actively feeding on its stellar partner.

The Mechanism of Recurring Outbursts

The discovery fundamentally recontextualizes AT 2016blu's behavior. Instead of an unstable, solitary star undergoing inexplicable periodic eruptions, the system is now understood as a binary pair: a normal, albeit unstable, star orbiting a black hole. The black hole's immense gravity periodically strips material from its companion star, pulling it into an accretion disk.

As this stellar material spirals towards the black hole, it heats up to extreme temperatures, emitting torrents of X-rays and visible light.
