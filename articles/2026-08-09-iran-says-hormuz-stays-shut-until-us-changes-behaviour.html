Iran says Hormuz stays shut until US changes behaviour

📅 2026-08-09 📂 World Original source ↗
Key points
  • Iran's top security official says Strait of Hormuz will remain closed until US 'corrects its behaviour'.
  • Oman reports positive talks, but Iran warns a deal alone won't reopen the waterway.
  • President Pezeshkian seeks to end 'no war, no peace' situation amid diplomatic efforts.
  • Strait of Hormuz standoff continues as global shipping lanes remain affected.

Iran has made clear that the Strait of Hormuz will not reopen until the United States changes its behaviour, according to the country's top security official. The statement, reported by Iranian media, puts a firm condition on any resolution to the ongoing standoff in the strategic waterway.

The remarks come as diplomatic efforts intensify, with Oman reporting positive signs from recent talks. But Tehran has been quick to dampen expectations, warning that even a deal would not automatically lead to the strait reopening.

Security chief sets red line

The unnamed top security official, quoted by Iran's Tasnim news agency, linked the strait's fate directly to Washington's actions. "The strait will not open until America corrects its behaviour," the official said, according to the report.

This hardline position underscores the deep mistrust between Tehran and Washington. It also signals that Iran sees the waterway as leverage in broader negotiations, not just a shipping lane to be managed.

Pezeshkian pushes for clarity

President Masoud Pezeshkian has separately called for an end to what he describes as a "no war, no peace" situation. His remarks, carried by Al Jazeera, suggest frustration with the current limbo and a desire for a more definitive outcome.

Pezeshkian's comments come amid reports of renewed diplomatic activity around Hormuz. The president appears to be navigating between domestic hardliners and international pressure to resolve the crisis.

Oman sees progress, Iran cautions

Omani officials have described the recent Hormuz talks as positive, according to BBC reporting. The sultanate has historically played mediator between Tehran and Washington, and its optimism has raised hopes of a breakthrough.

Iran, however, has moved to temper that optimism. A warning from Tehran made clear that a deal, even if reached, would not by itself open the strait. The condition remains: US behaviour must change first.

What's at stake in the strait

The Strait of Hormuz is a critical chokepoint for global oil supplies, with a significant share of the world's crude passing through its waters. Any prolonged closure would have severe consequences for energy markets and international shipping.

The Times of India notes that the standoff has drawn international attention, with both Iran and the US laying out competing demands. For Washington, the priority is restoring free navigation; for Tehran, it is about recognition and behaviour change.

The situation remains fluid, with no clear timeline for a resolution. Analysts suggest that the coming weeks will be crucial in determining whether diplomacy can bridge the gap.

As talks continue, the world watches to see if Iran's conditions soften or if the US moves to address Tehran's concerns. The strait's fate now hinges on whether the two sides can find common ground beyond the current standoff.
