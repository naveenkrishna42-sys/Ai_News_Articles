Dr Shriram Nene debunks 4 common monsoon health myths

📅 2026-08-07 📂 Health Original source ↗
Key points
  • Dr Shriram Nene addresses common misconceptions about monsoon health
  • Myths include avoiding bathing, eating fruits, and getting wet in rain
  • Simple preventive measures can significantly reduce waterborne disease risk

Monsoon brings relief from the heat, but it also brings a flood of health advice—some of it more myth than fact. Cardiac surgeon Dr Shriram Nene has stepped in to set the record straight, busting four common monsoon myths that many families still swear by.

In a video that has gone viral with the caption “Send this to the family group,” Dr Nene tackles misconceptions that range from avoiding bathing during fever to skipping fruits in the rainy season. His goal: to replace folklore with science-backed guidance.

The ‘Don’t Bathe in Fever’ Myth

One of the most persistent myths Dr Nene addresses is the belief that bathing during a fever—especially in monsoon—worsens the condition. Many Indian households insist on sponge baths or no baths at all when someone has a temperature.

Dr Nene clarifies that bathing is not only safe but can actually help bring down a fever. A lukewarm bath helps regulate body temperature and keeps the skin clean, reducing the risk of secondary infections. He advises against cold water, which can cause shivering, but stresses that skipping a bath altogether is unnecessary.

Fruits Are Your Friend, Not Foe

Another widespread belief is that eating fruits during the rains invites infections, particularly if they are not cooked. Dr Nene dismisses this, noting that fruits are packed with essential vitamins and antioxidants that strengthen immunity—exactly what the body needs during the monsoon.

He does add a caveat: wash fruits thoroughly and avoid cut fruit from street vendors, where hygiene may be compromised. The key is not to avoid fruits, but to ensure they are clean and fresh.

Getting Wet in Rain Does Not Cause Illness

Many parents scold children for playing in the rain, fearing they will catch a cold or dengue. Dr Nene explains that getting wet itself does not cause disease—it is the exposure to cold and the subsequent drop in body temperature that can make one more susceptible to viral infections.

He recommends drying off promptly and changing into dry clothes, but says there is no reason to panic if someone gets drenched. The real culprits are viruses and bacteria, not the rain itself.

Dengue and Mosquito Myths

Dr Nene also takes on the myth that dengue spreads through direct contact with an infected person. He clarifies that dengue is transmitted only through the bite of an infected Aedes mosquito, not through coughing, sneezing, or touching.

He urges families to focus on eliminating stagnant water around homes—the breeding ground for mosquitoes—rather than isolating a sick family member. Simple steps like covering water containers, cleaning coolers, and using repellents are far more effective than avoiding a patient.

Practical Monsoon Hygiene Tips

Beyond busting myths, Dr Nene emphasises simple habits to prevent waterborne diseases. Drinking boiled or filtered water, eating freshly cooked meals, and washing hands frequently are non-negotiables.

He also advises caution with street food and cut fruits, which are common sources of infections during the rains. For those with children, he stresses that immunisation and proper nutrition are the best defences against seasonal illnesses.

Health experts echo his advice, noting that most monsoon ailments—from typhoid to gastroenteritis—are preventable with basic hygiene. The Indian Express, which first reported the video, highlights that Dr Nene’s approachable style has made him a trusted voice in health communication.

As the monsoon continues, expect more doctors and public health officials to reinforce similar messages, especially in regions prone to flooding and water contamination. The takeaway: follow science, not superstition, and when in doubt, consult a physician.
