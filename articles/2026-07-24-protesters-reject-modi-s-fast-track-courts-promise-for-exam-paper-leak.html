Protesters reject Modi's fast-track courts promise for exam paper leaks

📅 2026-07-24 📂 India Original source ↗
Key points
  • PM Modi promised fast-track courts and a new bill to tackle exam paper leaks amid ongoing protests.
  • Protesters and opposition leaders reject the move as insufficient, demanding accountability from officials.
  • Delhi High Court has designated a special court for criminal cases arising from paper leaks.

Protests intensify despite PM's pledge

Prime Minister Narendra Modi's promise to set up fast-track courts and introduce stricter laws to tackle exam paper leaks has failed to pacify angry protesters across India. Demonstrations, led largely by students, continue in several states, with many calling the government's response a 'band-aid' solution.

In a video message released today, Modi said the government will bring a new Bill to ensure 'more strict actions' against those involved in paper leaks. He also announced the creation of fast-track courts to expedite trials. The announcement came as protests over recurring leaks in competitive exams, including medical and engineering entrance tests, have simmered for weeks.

'Band-aid' ire and demands for accountability

Student groups and opposition parties have dismissed the PM's pledge as inadequate. Critics argue that fast-track courts alone cannot address the systemic failures that allow such leaks to happen repeatedly. 'This is a band-aid on a deep wound. The government must first hold those in power accountable,' said a protest leader in Delhi.

Opposition parties have stepped up their attack, demanding the resignation of Education Minister Dharmendra Pradhan. The Congress party and other opposition groups allege that the leaks stem from a lack of oversight and deliberate negligence. The Chief Justice of India has also weighed in, calling for a thorough investigation into the nexus between exam officials and private coaching centres.

Delhi High Court designates special court

In a parallel development, the Delhi High Court has notified a designated court to exclusively deal with criminal cases arising from exam paper leaks. This move is seen as an attempt to speed up the legal process, but activists say it does little to prevent future leaks.

Several state governments have also launched their own inquiries, but no major arrests have been reported in recent days. The Central Bureau of Investigation is reportedly probing multiple cases, though officials have not confirmed any breakthroughs.

What's at stake

The exam paper leaks have affected lakhs of students, many of whom spent months preparing for competitive tests. For many, these exams are the only route to a government job or a seat in a top college. The repeated breaches have eroded trust in the system, with students demanding not just punishment for culprits but also structural reforms.

As protests show no signs of waning, the government faces mounting pressure to deliver more than just promises. The coming weeks will test whether the fast-track courts and new legislation can restore confidence, or if the anger on the streets will force a deeper political reckoning.
