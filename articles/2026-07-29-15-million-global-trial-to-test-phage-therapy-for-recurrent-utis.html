€15 Million Global Trial to Test Phage Therapy for Recurrent UTIs

📅 2026-07-29 📂 Health Original source ↗
Key points
  • The trial is funded with €15 million and aims to enroll patients across multiple countries.
  • Phage therapy uses viruses that specifically target and kill bacteria, offering a potential alternative to antibiotics.
  • Recurrent UTIs affect millions globally, and antibiotic resistance is a growing concern in treatment.

A global clinical trial backed by €15 million in funding is set to test whether bacteriophage therapy can offer a viable alternative to antibiotics for recurrent urinary tract infections (UTIs). The study, announced this week, aims to tackle the growing crisis of antimicrobial resistance by turning to nature's oldest predators of bacteria: viruses known as phages.

What Phage Therapy Is and How It Works

Bacteriophages, or phages, are viruses that naturally infect and destroy bacteria. Unlike broad-spectrum antibiotics that wipe out both harmful and beneficial microbes, phages are highly specific — they target only the bacterial strain causing the infection.

For patients with recurrent UTIs, this precision could mean fewer side effects and a lower risk of disrupting the gut microbiome. The therapy is not new; it was used in parts of Eastern Europe and the former Soviet Union for decades. But rigorous Western-style clinical trials have been scarce.

The Scope and Scale of the Trial

The trial, funded by a €15 million grant from a consortium of European research bodies and philanthropic foundations, will be one of the largest of its kind. It will enroll patients from hospitals in Europe, North America, and possibly India, though the exact number of participants has not been disclosed yet.

Researchers plan to compare the efficacy of a phage cocktail — a mix of several phages — against standard antibiotics in preventing UTI recurrence over a six-month period. The primary endpoint is the reduction in the number of infection episodes.

Why This Matters for India

Urinary tract infections are among the most common bacterial infections in India, affecting millions — particularly women. The rise of multidrug-resistant bacteria, such as extended-spectrum beta-lactamase (ESBL) producing E. coli, has made many first-line antibiotics ineffective.

Indian hospitals have reported UTI-causing bacteria resistant to nearly all available oral antibiotics. If phage therapy proves successful in this global trial, it could offer a new treatment pathway for Indian patients who currently have few options.

Challenges Ahead

Despite its promise, phage therapy faces significant hurdles. Manufacturing phages at scale is complex, and regulatory frameworks for such biological therapies are still evolving in many countries, including India. The Indian Council of Medical Research has expressed interest in phage research but has yet to approve any commercial phage product.

Another challenge is the need for rapid diagnostic tests to identify the specific bacterial strain causing a UTI, since phages are strain-specific. Without such tests, doctors cannot match the right phage to the infection. The trial will also test a point-of-care diagnostic tool to address this bottleneck.

Officials involved in the trial have not yet confirmed timelines for completion or when results might be published. Given the scale of recruitment and follow-up, the study is expected to run for at least three years.

The success of this trial could reshape how recurrent UTIs — and potentially other bacterial infections — are treated globally. For now, patients and doctors alike will be watching closely.
