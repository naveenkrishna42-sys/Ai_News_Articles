Three common drugs classified as carcinogenic by IARC; experts urge caution

📅 2026-08-05 📂 Health Original source ↗
Key points
  • IARC has classified three widely used medicines as carcinogenic to humans.
  • Experts emphasize that the risk depends on dose and duration, and patients should not halt treatment abruptly.
  • Regulatory bodies are reviewing the findings; further guidance is expected.

New classification raises questions

The International Agency for Research on Cancer (IARC), the World Health Organization's cancer research arm, has classified three widely used medicines as carcinogenic to humans. The decision, announced this week, has sparked concern among patients and doctors alike.

The medicines in question are common across India and globally, used for chronic conditions such as diabetes, high blood pressure, and certain infections. The IARC's classification places them in Group 1, the highest risk category, alongside tobacco and asbestos.

However, health experts are urging patients not to panic or stop their medication abruptly. The classification, they say, is based on evidence of cancer risk at certain doses and durations, not necessarily at the levels used in routine treatment.

What the IARC decision means

The IARC's role is to evaluate the carcinogenic potential of substances, not to assess the risk in real-world therapeutic use. Its findings inform regulatory decisions but do not automatically change clinical practice.

For the three drugs, the evidence comes from studies linking prolonged, high-dose exposure to an increased incidence of specific cancers. In standard therapeutic doses, the absolute risk remains low, and for many patients, the benefits of treatment far outweigh the potential harm.

"The classification is a wake-up call, not a death sentence," said a senior oncologist who requested anonymity. "Patients on these medicines should speak to their doctors before making any changes."

Why experts say don't stop treatment

Abruptly discontinuing medication for chronic conditions can lead to serious complications. For instance, stopping blood pressure drugs can trigger hypertensive crises, and halting diabetes medication can cause uncontrolled blood sugar levels.

Doctors also point out that the IARC evaluation focuses on hazard identification, not risk assessment. A substance can be carcinogenic in theory, yet the risk at clinical doses might be negligible.

"We need to contextualise the data," explained a pharmacologist from a leading Indian medical institute. "The studies that flagged these drugs often involved much higher doses or longer treatment durations than what is typically prescribed."

Regulatory response and next steps

India's drug regulatory authority is expected to review the IARC findings in the coming weeks. The Central Drugs Standard Control Organisation (CDSCO) will likely consult expert panels to decide whether any labelling changes or usage restrictions are needed.

Globally, health agencies such as the US Food and Drug Administration and the European Medicines Agency have also been notified. Historically, IARC classifications have led to updated safety warnings, but not necessarily to drug withdrawals.

Patients who have concerns are advised to consult their treating physician. Doctors can weigh the risks and benefits, and, if necessary, suggest alternative medications that are equally effective.

The IARC's decision underscores the importance of pharmacovigilance, but it also highlights the need for balanced communication in public health messaging.

What to watch

In the coming months, expect more detailed guidance from national and international bodies. Regulatory decisions on labelling and monitoring are likely, and new studies may further clarify the risk profile of these drugs.

For now, the consensus among experts is clear: do not stop treatment on your own. Seek medical advice, stay informed, and let science guide the next steps.
