Study: Balanced Fluids No Better Than Normal Saline For Children With Septic Shock

📅 2026-07-20 📂 Health Original source ↗
Key points
  • The Pragmatic Pediatric Trial compared balanced fluids vs. normal saline in pediatric sepsis.
  • No significant difference in mortality or kidney injury was found between the two fluid types.
  • Findings may prompt changes in emergency and ICU fluid protocols for children.

Large Trial Questions Fluid Superiority in Pediatric Sepsis

A new study has cast doubt on the assumed superiority of balanced crystalloid fluids over normal saline for children suffering from septic shock. The Pragmatic Pediatric Trial of Balanced Versus Normal Saline Fluid in Sepsis, led by Dr. Scott L. Weiss and Dr. Fran Balamuth, found no meaningful difference in outcomes between the two fluid types.

Published this month, the trial enrolled hundreds of children across multiple centres. Researchers tracked mortality rates, need for kidney replacement therapy, and length of hospital stay. The results challenge a growing trend in intensive care to favour balanced solutions like Ringer's lactate or Plasma-Lyte over the cheaper, more widely available normal saline.

No Edge in Survival or Kidney Protection

The primary outcome — death within 30 days — was nearly identical between groups. Rates of acute kidney injury and subsequent dialysis also showed no statistical difference. "Balanced fluids did not reduce mortality or kidney complications compared to normal saline," the authors noted in their analysis.

This matters because many hospitals have shifted protocols toward balanced fluids based on smaller adult studies suggesting potential benefits. The pediatric data now suggests that for children, normal saline may be just as safe and effective. The findings were presented at a critical care conference and published simultaneously in a peer-reviewed journal.

Clinical Practice Implications

For emergency physicians and paediatric intensivists, the trial provides clear guidance. Cost and availability often dictate fluid choice in resource-limited settings. Normal saline costs significantly less and is easier to stock in large volumes. The study supports its continued use as a first-line resuscitation fluid in children with sepsis.

Dr. Weiss and his team emphasised that the choice between balanced and unbalanced fluids should not be a major clinical dilemma. "What matters more is timely administration, appropriate volume, and overall sepsis management," the researchers said. They cautioned against reading the results as an endorsement of normal saline over balanced fluids — rather, the two appear clinically equivalent.

What the Study Does Not Answer

The trial did not include children with severe pre-existing kidney disease or those needing massive fluid resuscitation. It also did not compare hyperchloremic acidosis rates in detail, though no excess harm was seen in the saline group. The researchers called for further analysis of subgroups where a difference might emerge.

Some clinicians argue that balanced fluids still have theoretical advantages — less chloride load, better acid-base balance — but this trial suggests those advantages do not translate into better survival or kidney outcomes in children.

Hospitals that have already switched to balanced fluids are unlikely to reverse course based on one trial. But for institutions still using normal saline, the study offers reassurance. The next step will be to see if larger, real-world registry data confirms these results over a longer follow-up period.
