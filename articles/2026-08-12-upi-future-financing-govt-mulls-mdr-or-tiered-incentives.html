UPI future financing: Govt mulls MDR or tiered incentives

📅 2026-08-12 📂 Business Original source ↗
Key points
  • Government weighs MDR or 'tiered incentives' for UPI sustainability
  • Small traders to be exempt from proposed merchant discount rate
  • Editorial scrutiny calls for thorough review of UPI financing
  • Policy reversal may impact digital payment ecosystem
  • Decision expected after stakeholder consultations

UPI at a crossroads

The government is exploring ways to make the Unified Payments Interface (UPI) financially self-sustaining, with discussions centering on introducing a merchant discount rate (MDR) or a system of 'tiered incentives'. The move marks a potential reversal from the current zero-MDR regime that has underpinned UPI's explosive growth since its launch.

Officials familiar with the deliberations say the aim is to reduce the burden on the National Payments Corporation of India (NPCI) and banks, which have absorbed the costs of processing billions of free transactions. The proposal, however, has sparked concern among fintech firms and consumer groups who fear it could dampen adoption.

Small traders shielded

According to reports, small traders will not be covered under the proposed MDR on digital transactions. This carve-out is intended to protect the millions of kirana stores and street vendors who have come to rely on UPI for daily receipts, and who were central to the government's push for digital payments after demonetisation.

The exemption, while welcome to small businesses, raises questions about how the revenue gap will be bridged. If the smallest merchants are excluded, the cost would fall disproportionately on larger retailers and e-commerce platforms, which could pass on charges to consumers.

Grey areas and scrutiny

An editorial in The Telegraph India has highlighted the grey areas in the proposal, arguing that UPI's future financing requires thorough scrutiny. The piece questions whether a tiered incentive structure could be gamed, and whether the government's push for financial inclusion would be undermined by any fee, however small.

The editorial also points to the lack of transparency in how NPCI and banks calculate their costs, and urges the regulator to publish detailed data before any final decision. "The devil lies in the details," it notes, adding that a poorly designed MDR could hurt the very segments the policy aims to help.

Policy reversal and market impact

The potential shift has been described as a 'policy reversal' by some analysts, given that the government had earlier resisted any charges on UPI to encourage adoption. The Reserve Bank of India had previously capped MDR on UPI at zero, and any change would require amending that framework.

Market watchers are divided. Some argue that a nominal fee is necessary to ensure the system's long-term viability, while others contend that UPI's network effects and low processing costs already make it profitable for banks. The Times of India reports that the proposal is at a preliminary stage, with no timeline for implementation.

What happens next

The government is expected to hold consultations with stakeholders, including banks, fintech companies, and merchant associations, before finalising any framework. A decision is likely in the coming months, and the outcome will shape the future of digital payments in India.

Observers will watch closely whether the final policy strikes a balance between sustainability and inclusiveness, and whether the exemption for small traders holds. For now, UPI users can transact as usual, but the ground beneath India's digital payment revolution may be shifting.
