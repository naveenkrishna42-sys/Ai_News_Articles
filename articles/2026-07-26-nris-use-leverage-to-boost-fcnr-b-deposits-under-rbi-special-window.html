NRIs use leverage to boost FCNR(B) deposits under RBI special window

📅 2026-07-26 📂 Banking Original source ↗
Key points
  • NRIs are using leverage to increase FCNR(B) deposits under RBI's special window
  • Depositors borrow against their own funds or pledge securities to multiply deposit size
  • The strategy aims to benefit from interest rate differentials and stable rupee-dollar exchange rates

How the strategy works

Non-resident Indians (NRIs) are turning to leverage to maximise their Foreign Currency Non-Resident (Bank) deposits under the Reserve Bank of India's special window. The approach involves borrowing money against existing assets or funds to increase the size of the deposit placed in FCNR(B) accounts.

Bankers say this is not a new trend but has gained traction in recent months. The RBI's special window, which allows banks to offer higher interest rates on FCNR(B) deposits for a limited period, has created an opportunity for NRIs to lock in better returns.

Why leverage makes sense now

The logic is straightforward. An NRI with, say, Rs 1 crore in liquid funds can borrow an additional Rs 50 lakh against those funds or other securities. The total Rs 1.5 crore goes into an FCNR(B) deposit, earning interest at the special window rate. The NRI pays interest on the borrowed amount but earns a higher return on the total deposit.

The spread between the deposit rate and the borrowing cost is the key. With the RBI's special window offering rates that are often higher than the cost of borrowing in foreign currencies, the net gain can be attractive. Currency stability also plays a role—if the rupee remains steady against the dollar, the NRI avoids exchange rate losses on the principal.

Risks involved

This strategy is not without risk. If the rupee depreciates sharply against the foreign currency in which the FCNR(B) deposit is held, the NRI could face a significant loss on conversion. Similarly, if interest rates rise and the borrowing cost increases, the margin could shrink or turn negative.

Bankers caution that the strategy works best for those with a clear understanding of currency and interest rate movements. The RBI's special window is time-bound, and the window for such deposits may close before the full benefit is realised.

Regulatory backdrop

The RBI's special window for FCNR(B) deposits was introduced to shore up foreign exchange reserves and stabilise the rupee. Banks are allowed to offer rates up to a certain spread over the London Interbank Offered Rate (LIBOR) or other benchmarks, making these deposits more attractive than regular FCNR(B) accounts.

The central bank has not restricted the use of leverage for these deposits, but it has mandated that banks follow know-your-customer and anti-money laundering norms strictly. Any misuse of the window could invite scrutiny.

Market participants say the leverage-driven deposits are concentrated among high-net-worth NRIs in the Gulf and Singapore. Smaller depositors are less likely to use this strategy due to the complexity and cost.

What to watch

The RBI's special window is expected to remain open for a few more months. The next move will depend on how much foreign exchange flows in and whether the rupee stays stable. If volatility returns, the leverage strategy could unwind quickly, leading to early withdrawals and pressure on bank liquidity.
