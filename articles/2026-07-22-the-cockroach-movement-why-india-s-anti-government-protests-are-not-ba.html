The Cockroach Movement: Why India's anti-government protests are not backing down

📅 2026-07-22 📂 Features
Key points
  • The Cockroach Movement is a student-led protest against the NEET paper leak and the government's handling of education.
  • Union Education Minister Pradhan accused Rahul Gandhi and the Congress of exploiting students for political gain.
  • The protest has drawn Opposition leaders to Jantar Mantar, with the CJP demanding action on the paper leak and student safety.
  • The movement's name refers to protesters' refusal to be crushed, echoing a phrase used by a government critic.

The protest that refuses to be crushed

For the third week running, thousands of students and young activists have occupied Jantar Mantar in central Delhi. They call themselves the ‘Cockroach Movement’. The name is deliberately provocative — a reference to the insect that survives even when you try to stamp it out.

The trigger was the NEET-UG paper leak in May 2026, which affected over 20 lakh medical aspirants. But the protest has swelled into a broader indictment of the Modi government: unemployment, inflation, and what demonstrators call the 'criminal privatisation' of education.

Union Education Minister Dharmendra Pradhan has accused the Congress and Rahul Gandhi of 'exploiting' students. On July 21, he said at a press conference: 'They are using these young minds as cannon fodder for their political ambitions.' The Congress has denied the charge, with Rahul Gandhi tweeting: 'Students don't need protectors. They need justice.'

How a cockroach became a symbol of defiance

The movement’s name traces back to a comment made by a senior government official in early July. During a heated television debate on the NEET leak, the official dismissed student protesters as 'cockroaches that keep coming back no matter how many times you swat them.' The phrase was intended as an insult. It backfired.

Within 48 hours, #CockroachMovement was trending on X (formerly Twitter). Protesters began carrying placards with cartoon cockroaches wearing graduation caps. One popular slogan: 'You can spray poison, but we will multiply.' The movement has since spread to campuses in Mumbai, Bengaluru, Kolkata, and Chennai.

Key organisers include the All India Students’ Association (AISA) and the Students’ Federation of India (SFI), but many participants are unaffiliated first-time protesters. 'I never thought I'd be here,' said a 19-year-old from a coaching hub in Kota, who asked not to be named. 'But my entire future was stolen by a paper leak. Who will give me back that year?'

Key players: From Wangchuk to the Chief Justice

The movement has attracted a cross-section of prominent figures. Ladakhi climate activist Sonam Wangchuk — who was briefly detained by Delhi Police on July 18 — has emerged as an unlikely mascot. A Delhi court ordered his shifting to a hospital of his choice, citing 'protection of his fundamental rights.' Wangchuk has been on a hunger strike for 12 days, demanding action on the paper leak and a freeze on NEET results until a CBI probe is complete.

Opposition leaders have made the Jantar Mantar site a regular stop. Congress president Mallikarjun Kharge visited on July 20, promising a 'full-fledged debate in Parliament.' AAP’s Arvind Kejriwal and Trinamool Congress’s Mamata Banerjee have also addressed the crowd. Police have not intervened, but the Delhi government has imposed Section 144 around the area, citing 'security concerns.'

Meanwhile, Chief Justice of India Surya Kant has faced sharp criticism over a 2024 ruling that allowed the Delhi Metro to operate without a full environmental clearance. Student leaders have drawn a direct line: 'If the judiciary can bend rules for a metro, why can't it bend them to protect our exams?'

The NEET paper leak: What we know so far

The leak first came to light in late May when question papers for the NEET-UG exam were found circulating on WhatsApp groups hours before the test. The Central Bureau of Investigation (CBI) has arrested 23 people so far, including four officials from the National Testing Agency (NTA). The agency has since cancelled results for 1,563 candidates, but thousands more remain in limbo.

Prime Minister Narendra Modi, in a speech on July 19, called the leak 'ghor paap' (a grave sin) and vowed 'strictest action' against the culprits. But protesters say his words ring hollow. 'We have heard promises before,' said a student leader at the protest site. 'We want resignations. We want a new exam conducted by a new agency.'

The government has not yet announced a re-test date. On July 21, the Ministry of Education said it was 'evaluating the legal implications' of conducting a fresh exam. Students say that delay is costing them a year of their lives.

What it means for ordinary readers

For the average Indian family, the NEET paper leak is not just a scandal — it is a crisis of trust. Every year, lakhs of students spend crores on coaching and tuition, often taking loans that their parents will spend a lifetime repaying. The exam is supposed to be the one fair gate to a medical seat. When that gate is broken, the entire system is questioned.

The Cockroach Movement taps into a deeper anger. It is not just about one exam. It is about the feeling that the system is rigged for the powerful. The protesters point to the Assam floods, which have displaced 2.5 lakh people this year — the Brahmaputra’s fury is natural, but the lack of drainage and embankment maintenance is political. They point to the tunnel collapse at a hydropower project in Uttarakhand that killed 10 workers and left 17 missing — a disaster that safety audits had warned about for years.

And they point to the recent news that Pakistan approached Canada for support in restoring the Indus Waters Treaty, only to be rebuffed. 'The government talks about national pride,' a protester said. 'But it cannot even secure our exams or our rivers.'

The international dimension: US strikes on Iran

While India’s domestic crisis simmers, global events are adding pressure. The US military has launched its 11th consecutive night of strikes on Iranian military installations, escalating a conflict that began after a drone attack on a US base in Iraq. Oil prices have risen 12 percent in the past week, hitting Indian consumers directly. Petrol in Delhi now costs ₹108 per litre — a record high.

The government has not yet commented on the impact of the US-Iran strikes on India’s energy security. But analysts say the timing could not be worse. 'India imports 85 percent of its oil. Every dollar rise in crude prices adds to the fiscal deficit,' said an economist at the National Institute of Public Finance and Policy, who declined to be named.

On the political front, the UK has a new Prime Minister: Andy Burnham, the former Manchester mayor who styled himself as the 'King in the North.' His Labour Party won a landslide in the July 4 general election. Burnham has already signalled a tougher stance on trade negotiations with India, demanding 'reciprocal access' for British professionals. That could complicate Indian IT firms' access to the UK market.

The opposition’s gamble

For the Congress and other opposition parties, the Cockroach Movement is both an opportunity and a risk. Aligning with students could revive their sagging electoral fortunes. But if the protests turn violent — as they did briefly on July 20 when a scuffle broke out between protesters and police — the government could use that as a pretext for a crackdown.

Rahul Gandhi has walked a careful line. He has visited the protest site twice but has not addressed the crowd directly from the stage. Instead, he met with student leaders in a private room at a nearby café. 'He does not want to be seen as hijacking the movement,' said a Congress strategist. 'But he also cannot afford to be absent.'

The BJP has responded by accusing the opposition of staging a 'drama.' On July 21, Home Minister Amit Shah said in Parliament: 'The NEET leak is a crime, and we are investigating it. But these so-called movements are nothing but a coordinated attempt to defame the government.'

What happens next

The Cockroach Movement shows no sign of dissipating. Organisers have announced a 'Delhi Chalo' march on August 1, calling on students from all states to converge on the capital. The government has not yet indicated whether it will allow the march. Delhi Police has said it will 'assess the situation.'

For now, the protesters remain at Jantar Mantar, sleeping on cardboard sheets and sharing food from langars. They chant slogans, hold up their cockroach placards, and wait. The monsoon rains have started, but they have not packed up. As one student put it: 'Cockroaches don't care about the weather.'

What to watch: The CBI’s final report on the NEET leak, expected by mid-August, could either defuse the movement or pour fuel on the fire. If the report names high-ranking officials, the protests could spread to other cities. If it does not, the students may turn their anger towards the judiciary — and the Chief Justice’s 2024 Metro ruling will be cited again.
