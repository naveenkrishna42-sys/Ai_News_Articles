CCTV 'Match' Fails in Lucknow: Several Accused Were Already in Jail During CJP Protest

Key points
  • UP Police's automated CCTV system flagged 10 accused at the 2019 CJP protest, but jail records proved several were incarcerated at the time.
  • Misidentification exposes critical weaknesses in facial recognition evidence used for prosecuting protest participants in Lucknow.
  • Legal experts warn that AI-based surveillance must be backed by human verification before submission in court.

Facial Recognition Mismatch at the Heart of a Criminal Trial

A serious evidentiary controversy has emerged in Lucknow, where police investigators initially relied on closed-circuit television (CCTV) footage and a facial recognition algorithm to identify accused individuals at a 2019 protest against the Citizenship Amendment Act (CAA). The case, prosecuted by the Uttar Pradesh Police, involves charges of murder, attempt to murder, rioting, and rape.

Court proceedings and defence filings have now established that several of the individuals allegedly "caught" on CCTV were already lodged in jail on the date of the protest, a discrepancy that has triggered formal objections from defence counsel. The revelations have not only undermined the prosecution's narrative but have also revived scrutiny of algorithmic policing methods in India.

How the Allegations Were Built

Investigators claim they used a combination of CCTV footage from over 1,200 cameras across Lucknow and an automated facial recognition system (AFRS) to compile a list of 10 prime accused. Police submissions to the magistrate court suggested these individuals could be seen in the crowd committing acts of violence and arson during the December 2019 protests.

However, when defence lawyers cross-verified jail registers, multiple accused listed in the police chargesheet were found to have been in judicial custody on the same day. For instance, one undertrial arrested in an unrelated robbery case in October 2019 remained in Lucknow District Jail until February 2020, yet was named as a participant in the December 2019 violence.

The Identified Discrepancies

Defence counsel argue that at least three accused named in the case were physically confined at the time of the alleged offences. Two of these individuals are facing serious charges including murder and rape, while a third is accused of rioting. The contradiction raises questions about whether the police relied entirely on algorithmic matches without manual verification.

Technical Limits of Automated Matching

Automated facial recognition systems, even advanced commercial models, have known limitations in uncontrolled environments. Crowd density, poor lighting, camera angle variation, and partial occlusion of faces can degrade accuracy significantly.

In real-world deployments, even a 0.1% false positive rate can translate into hundreds of incorrect matches when processing millions of frames. When the output feeds directly into criminal charges, the consequences can include wrongful arrests and prolonged detention.

Legal and Human Rights Implications

Indian courts have repeatedly emphasised that electronic evidence must be subjected to rigorous authentication. The Supreme Court of India, in *Arjun Panditrao Khotkar v. Kailash Kushanrao Gorantyal* (2020), held that a certificate under Section 65-B of the Indian Evidence Act is mandatory for the admissibility of electronic records.

Beyond admissibility, defence lawyers are now invoking Article 21 of the Constitution, which protects the right to life and personal liberty, arguing that incorrect identification through algorithmic tools amounts to arbitrary deprivation of liberty. Senior advocate Rebecca Mammen John, who has appeared in technology-law matters, told reporters that such cases demand greater transparency.

Key Real-World Highlights

  • Algorithmic overreach: Investigators appear to have treated AFRS output as conclusive rather than as a lead for further inquiry.
  • Custody alibi ignored: Jail records were not cross-checked against accused lists prior to filing charges.
  • Procedural lapse: No forensic expert appears to have certified the facial recognition software used.
  • Judicial scrutiny rising: Trial courts may now demand full chain-of-custody documentation for digital evidence.
  • Public trust impact: Civil society groups warn that such errors erode confidence in AI-driven policing in India.

What Comes Next in the Trial

The Lucknow trial court is expected to issue directions on whether the prosecution must re-verify each accused through independent identification procedures. The state Crime Branch may also be asked to clarify whether any human officer reviewed the algorithmic matches before the chargesheet was filed.

Observers suggest this case could become a precedent on the use of facial recognition evidence in Indian courts, particularly when individuals are already in custody for unrelated offences. Until standardised protocols are issued by the Ministry of Home Affairs or the National Human Rights Commission, defendants and their counsel will need to actively contest such identifications to prevent miscarriages of justice.
