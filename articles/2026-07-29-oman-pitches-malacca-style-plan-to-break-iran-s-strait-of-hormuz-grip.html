Oman Pitches Malacca-Style Plan to Break Iran's Strait of Hormuz Grip

📅 2026-07-29 📂 World Original source ↗
Key points
  • Oman has presented a proposal for a shared management system at the Strait of Hormuz, modeled on the Malacca Strait.
  • The plan involves voluntary fees from Gulf states to fund maritime security and navigation services.
  • Iran and Saudi Arabia are engaged in talks, with Oman mediating, as the US signals support for dialogue.

Oman's Strategic Gambit at Hormuz

Oman has put forward a proposal to restructure control of the Strait of Hormuz, aiming to reduce Iran's dominant influence over the critical waterway. The plan, backed by several Gulf states, seeks to introduce a Malacca-style management system where transit fees are collected voluntarily from users. These funds would then support maritime security and navigational services.

The strait sees about a fifth of the world's oil supply pass through its narrow channel. Iran has long wielded de facto control, often threatening to block the passage during geopolitical tensions. Oman's initiative is a direct attempt to create a multilateral framework that dilutes Tehran's sole grip.

Gulf States Rally Behind Voluntary Fees

Under the Omani proposal, Gulf Cooperation Council (GCC) members would pay voluntary fees to maintain a shared maritime oversight mechanism. The model draws inspiration from the Malacca Strait, where littoral states coordinate traffic management and security without ceding sovereignty.

Sources indicate that Saudi Arabia and the United Arab Emirates are supportive of the plan. They see it as a way to ensure stable oil exports without being held hostage to Iranian threats. Oman, which shares maritime borders with Iran and has historically acted as a neutral broker, is positioning itself as the mediator.

Iran Hosts Talks Amid US Encouragement

Iran has hosted recent rounds of talks with Saudi Arabia and Oman, with Tehran presenting its own counter-proposals. The discussions come as former US President Donald Trump, who is reportedly in touch with regional leaders, has hailed what he calls 'good talks' between the parties.

Iran's primary demand remains recognition of its right to manage the strait, given its long coastline along it. However, the Omani plan offers a face-saving alternative: Tehran could participate in a collective management body rather than being sidelined. The US has not officially endorsed any plan but has signaled openness to dialogue over confrontation.

What Happens Next

The coming weeks will see continued shuttle diplomacy between Muscat, Tehran, and Riyadh. If a consensus emerges, the Strait of Hormuz could see its first formal multilateral management framework in decades. Failure to agree, however, risks a return to unilateral threats and potential naval standoffs.
