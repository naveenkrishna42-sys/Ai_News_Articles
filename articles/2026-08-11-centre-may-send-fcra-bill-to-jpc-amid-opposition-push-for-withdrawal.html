Centre may send FCRA Bill to JPC amid Opposition push for withdrawal

📅 2026-08-11 📂 Top Stories Original source ↗
Key points
  • Centre signals willingness to send FCRA Amendment Bill to a Joint Parliamentary Committee.
  • Opposition parties, backed by protests in Mizoram, are demanding full withdrawal of the Bill.
  • Nagaland Congress alleges the Bill could be used as a political weapon against Christian institutions.

The Union government is exploring a middle path on the contentious Foreign Contribution (Regulation) Amendment Bill, 2026, with indications that it may refer the legislation to a Joint Parliamentary Committee (JPC) for further scrutiny. The move comes as Opposition parties, church bodies and civil society groups step up demands for a complete withdrawal of the proposed law.

Sources familiar with the development said the government is keen to build a consensus, especially after protests erupted in Mizoram and criticism mounted from the northeastern states. A final decision on the Bill’s fate is expected in the coming days, though officials have not yet confirmed the timeline for a formal announcement.

What the Bill proposes

The FCRA Amendment Bill seeks to tighten rules governing foreign funding received by non-governmental organisations, trusts and religious institutions. It introduces stricter reporting norms, limits on administrative expenses and greater government oversight over fund utilisation.

Supporters argue the changes are needed to ensure transparency and prevent misuse of foreign money. Critics, however, contend that the provisions are disproportionately aimed at minority-run institutions, particularly Christian missionary organisations that run schools, hospitals and social welfare projects across the country.

Opposition and church bodies cry foul

The Opposition has rejected the JPC route as inadequate. Several parties have insisted that the Bill be scrapped entirely, calling it an infringement on the autonomy of voluntary organisations. In Nagaland, the Congress unit has gone a step further, alleging that the FCRA could become a political weapon against Christian institutions.

"The Bill cannot become a tool to target churches and their charitable work," a Nagaland Congress spokesperson said, echoing concerns raised by church leaders in the Northeast. Protests in Mizoram have drawn thousands, with demonstrators holding prayers and rallies against the proposed law.

A delicate regional balance

The Bill’s fate is particularly sensitive in the Northeast, where Christian missionaries have historically played a key role in education and healthcare. State governments in the region, including those ruled by the BJP and its allies, have urged the Centre to tread carefully.

Meghalaya and Nagaland have already passed resolutions against the Bill, while Mizoram’s assembly has unanimously opposed it. The government’s willingness to consider a JPC could be an attempt to defuse the situation without a full climbdown.

What a JPC would mean

A Joint Parliamentary Committee would allow for detailed clause-by-clause examination, with representations from stakeholders, including church groups and NGO networks. Such a panel typically has members from both Houses and across party lines, giving the Opposition a platform to press for changes.

However, a JPC is not a veto. It can recommend amendments, but the final say rests with Parliament. Political observers say the government may be using the JPC offer as a tactical concession, hoping to blunt the sharpest criticism while retaining the Bill’s core provisions.

The debate is far from settled. With Parliament’s monsoon session under way, the government must decide whether to push the Bill through or defer it. Any move to send it to a JPC will likely be welcomed by some, but the Opposition’s demand for withdrawal is unlikely to fade.

All eyes are now on the government’s next move — whether it will announce the JPC reference or hold further consultations with regional leaders. The coming weeks will reveal if the Centre is willing to dilute the Bill or stand firm.
