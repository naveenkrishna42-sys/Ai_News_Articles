Arunachal is integral part of India, nothing can change this: MEA on China's objections

📅 2026-08-11 📂 India Original source ↗
Key points
  • MEA firmly states Arunachal Pradesh is an integral part of India and no action can alter this reality.
  • India has formally named 27 places in Arunachal Pradesh, drawing protests from China.
  • India warns that the state of the border will reflect the state of bilateral ties with China.

New Delhi: India has delivered a sharp rebuke to China over its fresh objections to the naming of 27 places in Arunachal Pradesh, asserting that the northeastern state is an integral part of the country and no action can change this indisputable reality.

MEA's firm response

The Ministry of External Affairs (MEA) responded on Tuesday to Beijing's protest against India's recent move to formally name 27 locations in the state. The ministry's statement left little room for ambiguity.

"Arunachal Pradesh has always been, is, and will remain an integral part of India. Nothing can change this fact," an official said, rejecting China's claims. The response underscores New Delhi's resolve to counter any attempt to question its sovereignty over the region.

India names 27 places, China protests

The trigger for the latest diplomatic friction was India's decision to officially designate 27 places in Arunachal Pradesh. The move, seen as a consolidation of administrative control, drew immediate protest from Beijing, which reasserted its long-standing claim over the state.

China has repeatedly objected to India's administrative actions in Arunachal Pradesh, which Beijing refers to as "Zangnan". However, New Delhi has consistently maintained that the state is an inalienable part of Indian territory. The formal naming of these places is being viewed as a strategic step to reinforce India's position on the ground.

Border ties reflect overall relations

In a pointed warning to Beijing, India made it clear that the state of the border will reflect the state of bilateral ties. The message, delivered through the MEA, signals that continued friction along the frontier could have wider implications for the relationship between the two Asian giants.

The statement comes amid ongoing military standoffs at several points along the Line of Actual Control (LAC). While both sides have engaged in diplomatic and military dialogues to resolve tensions, the latest naming exercise adds a new layer of complexity to the already delicate equation.

Officials have not yet confirmed what specific places have been named, but reports suggest the move is part of a broader effort to assert administrative presence in remote areas. The timing of the announcement, coming just days after recent border talks, suggests New Delhi is unwilling to yield ground on its territorial claims.

A history of friction

India and China have a long history of border disputes, with the 1962 war remaining a defining episode in bilateral ties. In recent years, clashes such as the Galwan Valley incident in 2020 have kept tensions simmering, even as both sides work to maintain peace along the frontier.

Beijing's repeated objections to India's actions in Arunachal Pradesh have become a recurring theme in the relationship. However, New Delhi's response this time has been notably firm, with the MEA choosing words that leave no room for negotiation on sovereignty.

The naming of 27 places is also being seen as a counter to China's own practice of assigning Chinese names to locations in the region, a move India has consistently protested. By formalising its own designations, India is signalling that it will not cede ground in the cartographic battle.

What to watch

As both countries continue their diplomatic engagement, the focus will be on whether this latest exchange escalates or remains a rhetorical tussle. With military talks scheduled in the coming months, the onus is on Beijing to respond to India's firm stance without further aggravating the situation. The coming weeks will reveal whether this naming exercise leads to a new round of negotiations or deeper friction along the border.
