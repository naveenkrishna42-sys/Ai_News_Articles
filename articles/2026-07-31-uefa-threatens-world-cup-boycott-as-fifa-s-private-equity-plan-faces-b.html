UEFA threatens World Cup boycott as FIFA's private equity plan faces backlash

📅 2026-07-31 📂 Sports Original source ↗
Key points
  • UEFA and its 55 member associations threaten to boycott the World Cup over FIFA's private equity proposal.
  • Concacaf and AFC join opposition, leaving FIFA isolated among major confederations.
  • Carlos Cordeiro, Gianni Infantino's senior adviser, resigns amid fallout over the plan.

Boycott threat over FIFA's private equity plan

UEFA has vowed to boycott the World Cup if FIFA proceeds with its contentious private equity plan, escalating a bitter power struggle within global football's governing body. The European body, representing 55 national associations, issued a sharply worded statement on Friday, warning that the proposal threatens the sport's integrity and governance.

Concacaf, the governing body for North and Central America and the Caribbean, has also rejected the plan, joining UEFA in open defiance. The AFC, Asia's football confederation, has now followed suit, according to reports, leaving FIFA increasingly isolated among its member associations.

Resignation of senior adviser Carlos Cordeiro

The fallout has already claimed a high-profile casualty. Carlos Cordeiro, a senior adviser to FIFA President Gianni Infantino, resigned on Friday, according to multiple reports. Cordeiro, a former president of the US Soccer Federation, was seen as a key architect of the private equity proposal, which would involve selling a stake in FIFA's commercial operations.

His resignation signals deepening turmoil within FIFA's leadership as Infantino faces mounting opposition from confederations that fear the plan would concentrate power and money in Zurich at the expense of regional bodies.

What the private equity plan entails

FIFA's proposal, first floated earlier this year, would bring in outside investors to buy a minority stake in a new entity holding FIFA's commercial rights. Proponents argue the influx of capital could fund development programmes and boost prizes at tournaments like the World Cup.

Critics, however, contend that selling a stake to private equity firms could compromise FIFA's non-profit ethos and give investors undue influence over decisions, including host-city selections and competition formats. UEFA's statement called the plan "a fundamental threat to football's governance model," though it stopped short of detailing what a boycott would involve.

Confederations close ranks

UEFA's threat is not an empty one. European nations have dominated recent World Cups, and a boycott without them would strip the tournament of its biggest television audiences and most marketable teams. Concacaf's rejection adds weight to the opposition, as does the AFC's reported stance, creating a formidable bloc of more than 100 national associations.

The standoff echoes past clashes between Infantino and UEFA president Aleksander Ceferin, who has repeatedly criticised FIFA's centralising tendencies. With the next World Cup set for North America in 2026, the stakes are high for all parties to find common ground.

What happens next

FIFA has not yet responded publicly to the boycott threat or Cordeiro's resignation. All eyes will now turn to an emergency meeting of FIFA's council, expected in the coming weeks, where Infantino may be forced to shelve or amend the private equity plan. For now, the world's most popular sport is navigating its gravest governance crisis in years.
