Jharkhand job stir: 14th day, hunger strike, and a 'non-political' protest

📅 2026-08-07 📂 Top Stories Original source ↗
Key points
  • Protest over Jharkhand job exams enters its 14th day
  • Eight protesters are on hunger strike; one has been hospitalised
  • Organisers emphasise the movement is 'non-political'
  • A 'Vidhan Sabha March' is planned as the stir escalates
  • Support for the protest comes from figures like Abhijeet Dipke

What began as a demand for transparency in Jharkhand's recruitment exams has now stretched into a 14-day sit-in, with protesters refusing to blink. The agitation, which has drawn hundreds to the streets, is being staged on two separate platforms — a physical occupation of public space and a symbolic hunger strike that has now claimed the health of at least one participant.

Eight protesters are currently on an indefinite fast. On Wednesday, one of them had to be rushed to a hospital as their condition deteriorated, according to officials familiar with the matter. The identity of the hospitalised protester has not been officially disclosed, and medical bulletins have been sparse.

Two stages, one demand

The protest's architecture is deliberate. One stage serves as the nerve centre for daily speeches, crowd coordination, and media interaction. The second, smaller stage is reserved for the hunger strikers — a cordoned-off area where supporters gather in silence, many holding placards that read 'Justice for Job Aspirants' and 'Stop the Paper Leaks'.

Organisers say the two-stage setup is symbolic: one represents the voice of the many, the other the sacrifice of the few. The demand itself is straightforward — a re-evaluation of the exam process and action against those responsible for alleged irregularities in state recruitment tests.

The 'non-political' question

What makes this stir different from the usual run of job protests is the organisers' insistence on a 'non-political' identity. They have refused party flags, turned away leaders offering solidarity, and asked politicians to keep their distance. "We are not here for photo-ops," one organiser told reporters, requesting anonymity. "We are here for jobs."

That stance has drawn cautious praise from unexpected quarters. CJP's Abhijeet Dipke, in a statement to the media, said, "It isn't necessary for me to be at the forefront" — a rare admission from a political figure that some movements are better left to the protesters themselves. His comments have been interpreted as a subtle endorsement, though he has stopped short of formally joining the stir.

What happens next

The protest is set to escalate. Organisers have announced a 'Vidhan Sabha March' in the coming days, intending to take their demand directly to the state legislature. Police permissions are yet to be confirmed, and the administration has not indicated whether it will allow the march to proceed.

Meanwhile, the hunger strike continues to weigh on the minds of the protesters' families. With one striker already hospitalised, the question of how long the fast can sustain itself looms large. Doctors on site have advised against prolonged fasting, but the strikers have remained resolute.

The coming week will test both the protesters' resolve and the state government's patience. If the Vidhan Sabha march is blocked, the movement may lose its momentum. If it goes ahead, it could force a political response that has so far been carefully avoided.

For now, the two stages stand — one loud, one quiet — united by a single demand that has yet to be met.
