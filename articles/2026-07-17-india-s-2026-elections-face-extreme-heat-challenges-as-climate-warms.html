India's 2026 elections face extreme heat challenges as climate warms

📅 2026-07-17 📂 Politics Original source ↗
Key points
  • India's 2026 elections are being held during extreme heatwaves, with temperatures exceeding 45°C in several states
  • Election Commission has extended polling hours and introduced cooling measures at booths
  • Climate scientists warn that such heat events could become more frequent, threatening voter turnout and safety

Polls under a scorching sun

As India heads to the polls in 2026, voters in at least seven states are casting ballots in temperatures that have crossed 45°C. The ongoing heatwave has forced the Election Commission to revise protocols, pushing polling start times earlier and extending hours to avoid the hottest part of the day.

In parts of Uttar Pradesh, Bihar, and Rajasthan, polling booths are now equipped with shaded waiting areas, drinking water, and in some places, misting fans. Officials say the measures are aimed at preventing heat-related casualties among voters and polling staff alike.

Logistical adjustments on the ground

The Election Commission has directed all state chief electoral officers to ensure that every booth has at least two cooling points. In districts with temperatures forecast above 44°C, polling parties are being deployed with extra water and oral rehydration salts.

Several states have also shifted the timing of the voting window. In Odisha and Jharkhand, for instance, polling now starts at 6 AM instead of 7 AM, with breaks during peak afternoon heat. Some booths in Chhattisgarh and Madhya Pradesh are using portable air coolers powered by generators.

Despite these steps, reports of voters collapsing at queues have emerged from at least three constituencies. Election officials have not yet confirmed any fatalities linked to the heat.

Climate experts warn of a recurring crisis

Climate scientists point out that this is not an isolated event. A recent study by the Indian Institute of Tropical Meteorology found that the frequency of heatwave days during April-June has increased by nearly 40% in the last two decades. With India's general elections always held in the summer months, the overlap between voting and extreme heat is becoming a structural challenge.

"We are seeing a pattern where the hottest days now coincide with the peak of campaign rallies and polling days," said a senior climatologist at the Indian Meteorological Department, speaking on condition of anonymity. "Without long-term planning, this will repeat."

Political parties too are adapting. Several have cancelled afternoon rallies and moved public meetings to early mornings or evenings. The use of indoor venues with air conditioning has risen, but smaller parties often lack the resources to make such changes.

Long-term implications for democratic participation

Voter turnout in heatwave-affected areas has already dipped by an estimated 5-8% in the first two phases of polling, according to preliminary data from the Election Commission. If this trend continues, it could affect the representativeness of results in states like Rajasthan and Gujarat, where summer temperatures routinely exceed 40°C.

Some civil society groups are urging the Election Commission to consider a constitutional amendment to shift the election schedule to cooler months, such as October or November. However, the Commission has not publicly endorsed this idea, citing the logistical complexity of aligning state and national elections.

For now, voters are left to brave the heat. At a polling booth in Nim ka Thana, Rajasthan, a 68-year-old farmer told reporters, "I walked two kilometres to vote. The water at the booth was warm, but I am not going to miss this chance just because of the sun."

As India's climate continues to warm, the challenge of conducting free and fair elections in extreme heat will only grow. Whether through technological fixes, schedule changes, or both, the Election Commission will need to act before the mercury rises further.
