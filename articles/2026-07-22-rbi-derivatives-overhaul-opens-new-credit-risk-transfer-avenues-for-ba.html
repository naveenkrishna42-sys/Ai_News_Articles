RBI Derivatives Overhaul Opens New Credit Risk Transfer Avenues for Banks, NBFCs

📅 2026-07-22 📂 Banking Original source ↗
Key points
  • RBI proposes expanding credit default swaps to include NBFCs as reference entities.
  • New instruments like index CDS and securitisation-based risk transfer are being considered.
  • Move aims to free up bank capital and deepen the corporate bond market.

The Reserve Bank of India (RBI) is set to widen the toolkit for credit risk transfer, giving banks and non-banking financial companies (NBFCs) more flexibility to manage their loan books. The central bank’s proposed overhaul of the derivatives market will allow lenders to offload credit risk using a broader set of instruments, including credit default swaps (CDS) tied to NBFCs and new structured products.

The move, outlined in a draft framework released earlier this week, is aimed at deepening the corporate bond market and freeing up capital for fresh lending. Industry experts believe it will also help smaller NBFCs access cheaper funding by making their debt more attractive to investors.

Expanding the Credit Default Swap Universe

Currently, credit default swaps in India are largely restricted to bonds issued by a handful of large corporates and public sector entities. The RBI now wants to include NBFCs as eligible reference entities, allowing investors to buy protection against their default. This is a significant shift, as NBFCs have become a critical source of credit in sectors like housing, microfinance, and small business lending.

The draft also proposes introducing index-based CDS, where investors can trade a basket of credits rather than a single entity. Such instruments are common in developed markets and allow for more efficient hedging of portfolio risk. Banks that hold large exposures to multiple NBFCs could use these to reduce concentration risk.

New Instruments: Securitisation and Beyond

Beyond plain-vanilla CDS, the RBI is exploring the use of securitisation-based credit risk transfer. This would allow banks to bundle a pool of loans, transfer the risk to special purpose vehicles, and sell securities to investors. The regulator has indicated that synthetic securitisation—where the underlying loans remain on the bank’s books but the credit risk is transferred via derivatives—may also be permitted.

Bankers say this could be a game-changer for managing stressed assets. Instead of selling bad loans to asset reconstruction companies at steep discounts, banks could insure them through the derivatives market. However, they caution that pricing and liquidity will need to develop before the market becomes meaningful.

Capital Relief and Market Depth

For banks, the primary benefit is capital relief. Under Basel III norms, loans to NBFCs attract a risk weight of 100% or more. By transferring the credit risk to a third party—say, an insurance company or a pension fund—the bank can reduce the capital it must set aside against that exposure. This frees up capital for new lending, which could help boost credit growth in a slowing economy.

For NBFCs, the change could lower borrowing costs. If investors can hedge their risk using CDS, they may demand a lower yield on NBFC bonds. The RBI is also expected to relax norms for foreign portfolio investors, who have been keen on Indian credit markets but were constrained by hedging restrictions.

Regulatory Safeguards and Implementation

The RBI has built in several safeguards. Only entities with a minimum credit rating—likely investment grade—will be eligible as reference entities. Trades must be cleared through a recognised central counterparty to reduce counterparty risk. Additionally, the central bank has proposed a phased rollout, starting with a pilot involving a few large banks and NBFCs before expanding to the broader market.

Market participants have until August to submit comments on the draft. The final framework is expected by the end of the year. Some bankers worry about the lack of a robust bankruptcy code for NBFCs, which could complicate settlement in case of default. The RBI has acknowledged these concerns and is working with the Insolvency and Bankruptcy Board to address them.

What happens next? Once the framework is finalised, banks and NBFCs will begin setting up the necessary infrastructure—legal documentation, risk models, and trading desks. If liquidity picks up, India could see a more vibrant credit derivatives market within the next 18 months.
