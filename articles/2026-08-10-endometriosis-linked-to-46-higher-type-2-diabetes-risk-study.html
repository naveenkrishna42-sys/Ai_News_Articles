Endometriosis linked to 46% higher type 2 diabetes risk: study

📅 2026-08-10 📂 Health Original source ↗
Key points
  • Study links endometriosis to a 46% increased risk of type 2 diabetes.
  • Findings suggest shared inflammatory and hormonal pathways.
  • Experts call for regular diabetes screening in endometriosis patients.

New study links endometriosis to higher diabetes risk

Women with endometriosis may face a significantly elevated risk of developing type 2 diabetes, according to a new study. The research, published recently, found that affected women have a 46% higher likelihood of being diagnosed with the metabolic condition compared to those without the disorder.

The findings add to a growing body of evidence that endometriosis—a condition where tissue similar to the uterine lining grows outside the uterus—has systemic effects beyond reproductive health. Researchers analyzed health data from a large cohort, adjusting for factors like age, body mass index, and lifestyle habits.

Why the link exists

The exact biological mechanism remains unclear, but scientists point to chronic inflammation and hormonal imbalances common to both conditions. Endometriosis is characterized by persistent pelvic inflammation, which can impair insulin sensitivity over time. Estrogen dominance, a hallmark of the disease, may also interfere with glucose metabolism.

"This is a wake-up call for clinicians," said one of the study's authors. "We need to look beyond the pelvis when managing endometriosis patients." The study's lead researcher emphasized that the association held even after excluding women who developed diabetes within a few years of their endometriosis diagnosis, reducing the chance of reverse causation.

Implications for patient care

The findings suggest that women with endometriosis should be considered a higher-risk group for type 2 diabetes. This means regular blood sugar screening and early lifestyle interventions could be beneficial. Doctors may need to incorporate metabolic assessments into routine gynecological care for these patients.

Currently, there are no specific guidelines for diabetes screening in endometriosis patients. The study's authors hope their work will prompt updates to clinical recommendations. They also call for more research into whether treating endometriosis—through surgery or hormonal therapy—can lower diabetes risk.

What this means for Indian women

In India, endometriosis affects an estimated 25 million women, though many remain undiagnosed due to lack of awareness. The study's findings are particularly relevant given the country's rising diabetes burden. With over 77 million adults living with diabetes, identifying additional risk factors could help target prevention efforts.

Indian women with endometriosis often face delayed diagnosis, sometimes by years. This delay may compound their metabolic risks, as untreated inflammation persists longer. Public health experts say integrating endometriosis awareness into diabetes prevention programs could be a cost-effective strategy.

Looking ahead

The study opens the door for more focused research on how endometriosis treatments affect diabetes risk. Future studies could explore whether early intervention in endometriosis—such as laparoscopic surgery or progesterone-based therapies—reduces the likelihood of developing type 2 diabetes. For now, the takeaway for patients and doctors is clear: endometriosis is not just a reproductive disorder, and its metabolic consequences deserve attention.

As the scientific community digests these findings, attention will turn to updating clinical guidelines and raising awareness among both gynecologists and endocrinologists. For millions of women, this research may be the nudge needed to monitor their blood sugar more closely.
