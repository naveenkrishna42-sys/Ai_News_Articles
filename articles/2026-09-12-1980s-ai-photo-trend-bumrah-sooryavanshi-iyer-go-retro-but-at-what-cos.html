The 1980s AI Photo Trend Explained

A new viral trend has taken over social media: AI-generated 1980s-style portraits. Cricketers Jasprit Bumrah, Vaibhav Sooryavanshi, and Shreyas Iyer are the latest to share their retro makeovers, complete with big hair, neon colours, and grainy film effects. The trend uses AI image generation tools to transform modern selfies into vintage-looking photos reminiscent of the 1980s. While it's all in good fun, the trend raises serious questions about data privacy, deepfake technology, and environmental impact.

How It Works: The Tech Behind the Retro Look

Most of these apps use generative adversarial networks (GANs) or diffusion models to apply style transfer. Users upload a photo, and the AI analyses facial features, then re-renders them with 1980s aesthetics: soft focus, warm tones, and period-appropriate clothing and hairstyles. The processing typically happens on cloud servers, meaning your photo leaves your device. Some apps offer on-device processing, but many rely on remote servers, which raises privacy concerns.

Privacy and Security Risks You Can't Ignore

The cybersecurity bureau has warned against fake '1980s retro photo' apps that may steal personal data. When you upload a selfie, you're handing over biometric data. Malicious apps can use these images for identity theft or to train facial recognition systems without consent. Even legitimate apps often share data with third parties. The Indian Express reports that your '80s style AI photo is temporary on the app, but what happens to the images you upload? Many are stored on servers, sometimes indefinitely, and could be leaked in a breach.

Deepfake Potential

AI-generated images can be misused to create deepfakes. A single photo can be manipulated to place you in compromising situations. While the current trend is harmless, the technology behind it is the same used for malicious deepfakes. Users must be cautious about which apps they trust.

The Hidden Climate Cost of Viral AI Images

Every AI-generated image consumes energy. Data centres running AI models require significant electricity, often from non-renewable sources. Moneycontrol reports that your '80s AI selfie isn't free: the hidden climate cost of viral AI images adds up. With millions of users generating multiple images, the carbon footprint is substantial. One study estimates that generating 1,000 images emits as much CO2 as driving 4 miles. While individual impact is small, the aggregate effect of viral trends is significant.

Real-World Buyer Implications

If you're considering using these apps, weigh the fun against the risks. Free apps often monetise through ads and data collection. Paid apps may offer better privacy but still process your data remotely. For cricket fans wanting to mimic Bumrah or Iyer, the allure is strong, but don't upload sensitive photos. Use a photo you're comfortable sharing publicly. Check app permissions: access to contacts, location, or microphone is unnecessary for a photo filter.

Pros and Cons of Using 1980s AI Photo Apps

  • Pros: Fun, creative way to engage with trends; easy to use; free options available; can produce shareable content.
  • Cons: Privacy risks; potential data misuse; environmental impact; deepfake potential; hidden costs (subscriptions, ads).

What Happens to Your Uploaded Images?

Most apps claim to delete images after processing, but policies vary. Some retain images to improve AI models. Read the privacy policy: look for clauses about data retention, third-party sharing, and user rights. If an app doesn't have a clear policy, avoid it. For cricketers like Bumrah, Sooryavanshi, and Iyer, their images are public anyway, but for everyday users, caution is key.

Safer Alternatives and Best Practices

Use apps that process images on-device, like some open-source options. Avoid apps that require account creation or access to your gallery. If you must use a cloud-based app, use a disposable email and a photo you don't mind losing. Remember, once uploaded, you lose control. For the trend, consider using a cartoon avatar instead of a real photo. The 1980s AI trend is a passing fad, but your digital footprint lasts forever.
