Jurassic Insect Calls Recreated: AI Decodes 165M-Year-Old Wings

  • AI models were trained on modern insect acoustics to simulate 165-million-year-old insect calls

How Scientists Brought Jurassic Forests Back to Life Through Sound

For decades, paleontology focused on what ancient creatures looked like, but rarely what they sounded like. A new interdisciplinary study has changed that by reconstructing the calls of insects from 165 million years ago using a combination of fossilized wing analysis, laser scanning technology, and modern machine learning algorithms. This research offers the first scientifically grounded approximation of a Jurassic forest soundscape, transforming how we understand the sensory ecology of the Mesozoic Era.

The Methodology: From Stone to Sound

Researchers used laser-based 3D surface scanning on exceptionally preserved insect wing fossils to capture microscopic morphological details. These scans revealed wing curvature, venation patterns, and structural asymmetries that determine how an insect generates sound through stridulation or wing-flicking. The scanning precision reached the sub-millimeter scale, allowing scientists to infer biomechanical properties of the now-mineralized tissues.

Once the wing geometry was captured, the structural data was fed into AI models trained on modern insect acoustic libraries. These models mapped ancient wing morphology against the flight and call patterns of extant relatives, generating synthetic calls grounded in biomechanical plausibility rather than artistic interpretation.

Why This Research Matters

The implications of this study extend well beyond novelty soundscapes. By reconstructing how insects communicated, scientists can now make data-driven inferences about predator-prey dynamics, mating behavior, and nocturnal activity cycles in Jurassic ecosystems. Sound plays a critical role in mating, territorial defense, and predator avoidance. Understanding these acoustic environments helps reconstruct broader behavioral and ecological relationships across the Jurassic food web.

Key Research Insights

  • Insect wing morphology correlates strongly with call frequency and amplitude, enabling acoustic reconstruction from fossils alone.
  • AI serves as a translation engine between fragmented fossil morphology and biomechanically plausible sound.
  • Jurassic forests were likely as acoustically dense as modern rainforests, driven by high insect biodiversity.
  • This methodology can be extended to other fossil groups, including early birds and non-insect arthropods.

Limitations and the Path Ahead

While the resulting soundscapes are scientifically grounded, they remain approximations. Soft tissue properties, flight muscle mechanics, and environmental acoustics of the Jurassic period cannot be directly observed. Researchers acknowledge that frequency estimates carry inherent uncertainty, and ambient forest noise reconstructions rely heavily on inference rather than direct evidence.

The next phase of research will focus on expanding the fossil dataset and refining AI training models with more diverse modern insect species. If successful, these models could eventually simulate entire Mesozoic ecosystems spanning multiple sensory dimensions. This project represents the convergence of paleontology, optics, and artificial intelligence, delivering an unprecedented sensory reconstruction of our planet's deep past.
