What Nomura’s preference means for bank watchers

Nomura’s stated preference for HDFC Bank, Kotak Mahindra Bank and ICICI Bank is a classic sell-side signal: the analyst team sees relative operating strength and a better fit for the current macro environment. In practical terms, investors tend to read such prefences as an expectation of steadier earnings quality—especially when margins, asset quality and cost discipline become the deciding factors.

For consumers, the link to credit cards and cashback is indirect but real. Banks that sustain stronger operating profitability are usually better positioned to fund rewards programmes without rapid repricing or abrupt benefit rollbacks. That does not guarantee better offers for every cardholder, but it does tend to support more consistent product roadmaps.

Understanding “core PPOP” and why 17% matters

Core PPOP (pre-provision operating profit) strips out provisioning effects and focuses on the bank’s core earning engine—net interest income, fee income, operating expenses and other recurring drivers. When a brokerage expects core PPOP growth of 17% in Q2, it points to improved operating leverage: banks earning more from their existing base while controlling costs.

From a buyer’s perspective, this is where the signal becomes useful. For investors, sustained core PPOP strength can translate into stronger confidence on dividend capacity and earnings resilience. For customers, higher operating strength often supports smoother technology investments (faster servicing, fraud controls) and more predictable loyalty/rewards economics for card portfolios.

Key operating drivers to track alongside the growth call

Buyers and analysts typically triangulate core PPOP expectations with three observable metrics: net interest margin trends, credit cost/provisioning guidance, and expense discipline (cost-to-income trajectory). Even if revenue grows, weak expense control can blunt profitability; conversely, disciplined costs can amplify the impact of stable demand.

When Nomura highlights operating growth prospects, it implicitly suggests expectations on one or more of these levers improving in the quarter.

What this could mean for credit-card and cashback users

Credit cards are not just “marketing offers”; they are unit-economics products. Rewards, cashback and milestone benefits depend on interchange, backend costs, fraud losses, and how the bank manages credit risk. When core operating profit growth looks healthy, it generally improves the bank’s ability to keep rewards structures sustainable over time.

However, consumers should not over-interpret a brokerage call as a guarantee of better cashback this quarter. Rewards can still be rebalanced based on category-level profitability (for example, travel vs groceries), partner economics, and bank-wide risk appetite.

Practical buyer benchmarks: how to judge whether your card is getting value

Instead of relying on generic “bank is strong” narratives, track the specific offer economics in your use case. A simple approach is to compute effective cashback/points value over three months, using actual spend categories where you will redeem.

  • Redemption clarity: Prefer programmes with low redemption friction and stable conversion rules.
  • Category concentration: If you spend heavily in one category, ensure the card’s bonus rate in that category is genuinely competitive versus alternatives.
  • Annual fee realism: Compare rewards earned on your actual spend to the annual fee to estimate break-even.
  • Offer stability: Look for sustained value rather than one-off promotional bursts.
