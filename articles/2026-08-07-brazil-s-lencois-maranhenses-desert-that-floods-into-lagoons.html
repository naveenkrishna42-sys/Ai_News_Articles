Brazil's Lencois Maranhenses: Desert That Floods Into Lagoons

📅 2026-08-07 📂 Science Original source ↗
Key points
  • Lencois Maranhenses National Park covers 1,565 square kilometres in Brazil
  • Freshwater lagoons form between white sand dunes during the rainy season
  • The phenomenon creates a unique landscape that draws tourists and scientists alike

A Landscape That Defies Labels

Every year, a vast expanse of white sand in northeastern Brazil undergoes a dramatic transformation that seems to defy the laws of nature. The Lencois Maranhenses National Park, spanning 1,565 square kilometres, is often described as a desert. But unlike true deserts, this region receives enough rainfall to create a spectacle that has no parallel anywhere else on Earth.

When the rainy season arrives, between roughly January and June, the depressions between the towering dunes fill with crystal-clear freshwater. The result is a surreal mosaic of turquoise and emerald lagoons nestled amid endless waves of sand. For a few months, the landscape becomes a playground of contrasting elements — arid dunes and vibrant swimming holes coexisting in perfect harmony.

Scientists classify the area as a restinga, a type of coastal ecosystem that is shaped by both marine and terrestrial forces. The name Lencois, which means 'bedsheets' in Portuguese, comes from the way the white dunes resemble crumpled linen spread across the landscape.

The Mechanics Behind the Miracle

The phenomenon is driven by a combination of geography and climate. The park sits along Brazil's northern coast, where trade winds blow sand inland from the Atlantic Ocean, forming dunes that can reach heights of up to 40 metres. But the region also receives a staggering amount of rainfall — around 1,200 millimetres annually — concentrated in the first half of the year.

The rain collects in the low-lying areas between the dunes because an impermeable layer of rock beneath the sand prevents the water from seeping away. This natural sealant turns thousands of sandy hollows into lagoons, some of which stretch for hundreds of metres. The water remains surprisingly clear, thanks to the lack of organic matter that would normally cloud freshwater bodies.

As the dry season sets in, the lagoons gradually evaporate, and the landscape reverts to its stark, sandy form. By October, most of the water is gone, leaving only the undulating dunes behind. The cycle repeats itself faithfully every year, a rhythm that has continued for millennia.

Wildlife and Visitors

During the wet months, the lagoons become a temporary haven for a surprising array of life. Fish, turtles, and even crabs somehow find their way into these isolated pools, likely transported by migratory birds that stop over in the region. The park is also home to a variety of endemic species that have adapted to the extreme seasonal shifts.

Tourists flock to the area during the lagoon season, often visiting between June and September when water levels are at their peak. Guided tours take visitors through the dunes on 4x4 vehicles, with stops at the most spectacular lagoons for swimming and photography. The park's remote location, about 250 kilometres from the city of Sao Luis, adds to its appeal as an off-the-beaten-path destination.

Local communities have long relied on the seasonal waters for fishing and recreation. In recent years, however, the growing tourism industry has brought both economic opportunities and concerns about environmental pressure on the fragile ecosystem.

A Site of Global Significance

In 2023, the United Nations Educational, Scientific and Cultural Organization (UNESCO) designated Lencois Maranhenses as a World Heritage Site, recognising its outstanding universal value. The designation has helped draw international attention to the park's unique geological and ecological features.

Conservationists, however, warn that climate change could alter the delicate balance that creates the lagoons. Shifts in rainfall patterns or rising sea levels could threaten the very phenomenon that makes the area so special. Researchers are closely monitoring the region's hydrological cycles to better understand how the ecosystem might respond to changing conditions.

For now, the spectacle continues — a reminder that nature's most extraordinary creations often arise from the most unexpected combinations.

Officials have not yet announced any new conservation measures, but visitors are encouraged to follow park guidelines to minimise their impact. The next rainy season is expected to bring the lagoons back to life, offering another chance to witness this remarkable natural wonder.
