Jakarta Airport Halts Flights as Anak Krakatau Erupts: What Travelers and Businesses Must Know

Key points
  • Ash cloud from Anak Krakatau forces immediate flight suspensions at Soekarno‑Hatta International Airport.
  • Airlines face costly re‑routing, while cargo and tourism sectors brace for revenue losses.
  • Authorities outline short‑term safety measures and long‑term volcanic monitoring upgrades.

Volcanic Activity Triggers Immediate Airport Shutdown

On 6 September 2026, the Anak Krakatau volcano erupted, spewing ash to an altitude of roughly 12 km. Within minutes, the Ministry of Transportation issued a NOTAM that barred all take‑offs and landings at Soekarno‑Hatta International Airport (CGK), Indonesia’s busiest gateway.

Air traffic controllers activated the standard volcanic ash avoidance protocol, which mandates a 0‑visibility ceiling for jet engines. The decision aligns with International Civil Aviation Organization (ICAO) guidelines that prioritize passenger safety over schedule adherence.

Operational Fallout for Airlines and Passengers

Major carriers, including Garuda Indonesia, Lion Air, and AirAsia, reported over 350 flight cancellations within the first two hours. The ripple effect extended to regional airports, where connecting flights were delayed or diverted to alternate hubs such as Kuala Lumpur International.

Travelers faced a cascade of re‑booking fees, accommodation costs, and visa‑extension hassles. Airlines activated their contingency funds, but the immediate financial hit is estimated at US$12‑15 million in lost revenue and compensation.

Economic and Social Ripple Effects

Beyond aviation, the ash plume disrupted ground transportation, halted school sessions in nearby districts, and forced a temporary closure of fishing ports along the Sunda Strait. Local businesses near the airport reported a 20 % dip in foot traffic on the day of the eruption.

Tourism operators warned of a potential 5‑7 % dip in international arrivals for the quarter, as travelers reroute to alternative destinations like Bali or Singapore. The broader supply chain—especially perishable goods shipped by air—faces spoilage risks, compounding the economic toll.

Mitigation Strategies and Future Preparedness

Indonesia’s volcanic monitoring agency, PVMBG, has pledged to upgrade its real‑time ash‑cloud tracking radar, integrating satellite data from the European Space Agency’s Copernicus program. The Ministry also plans to establish a dedicated “Ash‑Cloud Response Unit” at CGK to streamline communication between meteorologists, air traffic control, and airline operators.

Airlines are encouraged to revise their contingency manuals, incorporating flexible crew rostering and dynamic fuel‑load calculations to mitigate future ash‑related disruptions.
