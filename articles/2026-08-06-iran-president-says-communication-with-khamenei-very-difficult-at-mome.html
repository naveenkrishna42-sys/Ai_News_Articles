Iran president says communication with Khamenei 'very difficult at moment'

📅 2026-08-06 📂 World Original source ↗
Key points
  • President Pezeshkian described communication with Supreme Leader Khamenei as 'very difficult at the moment'.
  • Reports of Pezeshkian's resignation threat during Hormuz talks have been denied by Iranian state media.
  • Mojtaba Khamenei has reportedly issued a 'final ultimatum' to the president, signaling deepening political rifts.

Iranian President Masoud Pezeshkian has acknowledged that communication with the country's supreme leader, Ayatollah Ali Khamenei, is currently "very difficult at the moment." The remark, made during a meeting with domestic officials, was reported by multiple outlets on Wednesday, though the exact context of the conversation was not fully disclosed.

The admission comes amid swirling reports of political turbulence inside Iran's leadership, including claims that Pezeshkian threatened to resign during sensitive negotiations over the Strait of Hormuz. Those reports have been swiftly refuted by Iranian state media, including the Tasnim news agency, which called them baseless.

Strained ties at the top

Pezeshkian's candid comment signals an unusual public acknowledgment of friction at the highest level of Iran's dual power structure. Under the Islamic Republic's constitution, the supreme leader holds ultimate authority over state affairs, while the president manages the day-to-day government. Any public admission of difficulty in communicating with Khamenei is rare and noteworthy.

Political observers have noted that the relationship between the president and the supreme leader's office has grown increasingly tense in recent weeks. The primary flashpoint appears to be the handling of the Strait of Hormuz, a critical waterway through which about a fifth of the world's oil passes.

Hormuz tensions and resignations claim

Reports originating from regional media suggested that Pezeshkian had threatened to step down during internal debates over how to respond to recent provocations in the strait. According to the Guardian, the claim has been denied by Iranian officials, who described it as a distortion of routine internal consultations.

The denial, however, has not fully quelled speculation. Political analysts point out that the very existence of such rumours indicates a level of discord that Iran's leadership would normally prefer to keep under wraps. The state's quick response suggests sensitivity over any appearance of a split between the elected government and the clerical establishment.

Mojtaba's 'final ultimatum'

Adding to the narrative of internal strain, NDTV reported that Mojtaba Khamenei, the supreme leader's son and a powerful figure in his own right, has issued what it described as a "final ultimatum" to President Pezeshkian. The nature of the ultimatum was not specified in the reports, but its existence suggests a hardening of positions within the ruling elite.

Mojtaba Khamenei has long been seen as a potential successor to his father, though his formal role in governance remains unofficial. His reported move against the president would mark a significant escalation in the power struggle that has simmered since Pezeshkian took office in 2024 promising reform and engagement with the West.

What's at stake

Iran's internal political dynamics are being watched closely by foreign capitals, particularly Washington and Tel Aviv. The United States has maintained a policy of maximum pressure on Iran, while Israel has repeatedly threatened military action against Iranian nuclear facilities if diplomacy fails.

Domestically, the situation is complicated by economic hardship. Inflation remains high, and ordinary Iranians have grown weary of international sanctions that show no sign of being lifted. A leadership vacuum or prolonged infighting could further destabilise an already fragile situation.

The exact nature of the communication difficulties between Pezeshkian and the supreme leader remains unclear. What is clear is that the public facade of unity that Iran's leadership typically projects has begun to crack, offering a rare glimpse into the tensions that lie beneath.

In the coming days, analysts will be watching for any further statements from the president's office or the supreme leader's circle that could clarify the situation. For now, the world can only speculate about the true state of Iran's leadership, even as the country navigates one of its most challenging periods in recent memory.

Whether Pezeshkian can mend fences with the supreme leader's office or is forced to navigate an increasingly hostile political environment remains to be seen. The coming weeks are likely to provide an answer.
