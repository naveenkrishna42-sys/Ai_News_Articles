NISAR satellite detects 60 cm ground shift in Venezuela quake

📅 2026-07-26 📂 Space Original source ↗
Key points
  • NISAR satellite detected ground displacement of up to 60 cm in Venezuela earthquake
  • Joint ISRO-NASA mission uses advanced radar to map surface deformation
  • Data helps scientists understand fault mechanics and earthquake dynamics

The ground beneath Venezuela shifted by as much as 60 centimetres during the country's recent earthquake, according to fresh data from the NISAR satellite. The joint ISRO-NASA mission, launched in 2024, captured the deformation using synthetic aperture radar.

The satellite's precise measurements offer scientists an unprecedented look at how the Earth's crust moved during the seismic event. The earthquake struck Venezuela earlier this year, causing widespread damage and casualties.

How NISAR tracks ground movement

NISAR carries two radar systems — an L-band radar from NASA and an S-band radar from ISRO. This dual-frequency setup allows it to see through clouds and vegetation, mapping the ground with centimetre-level accuracy.

The satellite compares radar images taken before and after an earthquake. By measuring the difference in the radar signal's return time, scientists calculate exactly how much the surface shifted. In this case, the displacement reached 60 cm in some areas.

Such data is crucial for understanding fault behaviour. It helps researchers identify which fault segments ruptured, how stress was transferred, and where future quakes might occur.

Implications for earthquake science

The Venezuela data is already being studied by seismologists at NASA's Jet Propulsion Laboratory and ISRO's Space Applications Centre. They are mapping the full extent of the deformation field around the epicentre.

This kind of measurement was difficult before NISAR. Ground-based instruments are sparse in many regions, and older satellites had lower resolution or longer revisit times. NISAR can image any point on Earth every 12 days.

The satellite's ability to detect even subtle shifts helps scientists refine models of earthquake physics. It also aids disaster response by showing which areas experienced the most ground movement.

What the data reveals about the Venezuela quake

The 60 cm shift suggests a significant release of strain along the fault. Preliminary analysis indicates the rupture may have propagated over a length of several tens of kilometres.

Officials have not yet confirmed the exact magnitude of the earthquake, but ground deformation of this scale typically corresponds to a quake of magnitude 7 or higher. The displacement pattern will help determine whether the fault was strike-slip or thrust type.

NISAR's data also shows variations in displacement across the region. Some areas moved more than others, reflecting differences in rock type, fault geometry, and local geology.

Next steps for researchers

ISRO and NASA scientists are now cross-referencing the NISAR data with ground observations from GPS stations and seismometers. This will validate the satellite measurements and improve their accuracy.

The findings will eventually be published in peer-reviewed journals. They will also feed into global seismic hazard models, which are used to estimate earthquake risk in regions like the Himalayas, the Andes, and the Pacific Ring of Fire.

NISAR continues to orbit, collecting data on earthquakes, volcanoes, landslides, and ice sheets. Each pass adds to a growing archive of how our planet's surface changes over time.

For now, the Venezuela earthquake data offers a stark reminder of the forces beneath our feet — and a new tool to measure them.
