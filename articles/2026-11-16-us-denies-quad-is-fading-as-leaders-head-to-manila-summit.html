US Denies Quad Is Fading as Leaders Head to Manila Summit

Key points
  • US officials have publicly pushed back on reports that the Quad has stalled under the current administration, ahead of a leaders' meeting in Manila.
  • The Quad has no treaty, no secretariat and no mutual-defence clause, so its durability depends entirely on political will in four capitals.
  • Deliverables remain concentrated in maritime domain awareness, critical minerals, technology standards and coast guard coordination rather than hard security guarantees.

US officials have moved to shut down suggestions that the Quad — the four-nation grouping of the United States, Japan, India and Australia — is drifting under the current administration, days before leaders are expected to gather in Manila. The pushback follows a wave of commentary, including a critical assessment in Politico, arguing that the grouping has lost momentum precisely when the Indo-Pacific needs it most. Japanese and Indian outlets have simultaneously reported that the summit schedule itself was uncertain until recently.

Washington's Denial and the Sceptics' Case

The American position is straightforward: the Quad is functioning, its working groups are active, and the Manila meeting is proof of continuity rather than a rescue operation. Officials point to ongoing coordination on maritime domain awareness, undersea cable security and critical minerals as evidence that the machinery has not seized up.

Sceptics counter that continuity of process is not the same as continuity of purpose. A grouping that once framed itself as the spine of a rules-based Indo-Pacific now sits awkwardly alongside tariff actions, transactional burden-sharing demands and a visible presidential preference for bilateral deals over multilateral formats.

The Politico Argument

The core critique is that the Quad's value was always aspirational — it promised to convert four disparate democracies into a predictable caucus. When the largest member signals ambivalence, the other three hedge, and the caucus becomes a calendar entry rather than a coalition.

Why Structure Cuts Both Ways

The Quad has no treaty, no secretariat and no mutual-defence clause. That design kept India inside the tent by avoiding a formal alliance. It also means nothing survives a change of mood in any one capital. Manila, a non-member host, is itself a signal: the grouping is trying to widen its footprint without deepening its commitments.

What the Quad Actually Delivers

Member / MetricPractical Contribution
United StatesMaritime domain awareness funding, technology standards, naval interoperability
JapanInfrastructure finance, coast guard capacity building, supply-chain resilience
IndiaIndian Ocean surveillance, humanitarian assistance, digital public infrastructure
AustraliaPacific engagement, critical minerals, undersea cable and telecom security
FormatLeaders' summit plus foreign ministers, working groups; no binding obligations

For buyers of the grouping's rhetoric — governments, insurers, shipping lines and semiconductor supply-chain planners — the relevant question is not whether the Quad survives, but which of these workstreams produce bankable outputs. Maritime domain awareness has produced shared tracking of dark vessels. Critical minerals coordination has produced early-stage financing conversations. Neither has produced a security guarantee, and neither will.

India's Balancing Act

New Delhi carries the heaviest diplomatic load in Manila. It needs the Quad as a technology and maritime platform while protecting its strategic autonomy and its still-substantial defence relationship with Moscow. Indian officials have consistently framed the grouping as a public-goods mechanism, not an Asian NATO — a framing that becomes harder to sustain if Washington's commitment reads as conditional.

Key Real-World Highlights

  • Continuity, not deepening: expect communiqué language on cables, minerals and coast guards rather than new security architecture.
  • No binding commitments: the Quad's voluntary structure is its diplomatic strength and its operational weakness.
  • Tariff friction is the real test: trade disputes between members will shape perceptions of the grouping more than any joint statement.
  • Manila as signal: hosting a non-member suggests outreach to Southeast Asia, but without institutional follow-through.
  • Watch the sherpa track: if working-group meetings slip in 2027, the sceptics' case is effectively proven.

What to Watch in Manila

Three deliverables matter. First, whether the leaders endorse a concrete maritime domain awareness expansion with named funding. Second, whether critical minerals coordination moves from dialogue to offtake agreements. Third, whether the joint statement avoids language that India would find binding.

The US denial is credible on process and thinner on ambition. The Quad will almost certainly survive Manila. Whether it remains a decision-making body or becomes a recurring photo opportunity depends on decisions taken in Washington, not in the Philippine capital.
