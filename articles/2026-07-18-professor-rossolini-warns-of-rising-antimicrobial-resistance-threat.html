Professor Rossolini Warns of Rising Antimicrobial Resistance Threat

📅 2026-07-18 📂 Health Original source ↗
Key points
  • Antimicrobial resistance is rising globally, making common medicines less effective.
  • Professor Rossolini highlights the risk to routine surgeries and medical procedures.
  • The future of clinical microbiology is critical in combating AMR.
  • World AMR Awareness Week 2026 underscores the urgency of the issue.

The Growing Crisis of Antimicrobial Resistance

Professor Gian Maria Rossolini, a leading expert in clinical microbiology, has issued a stark warning about the global threat of antimicrobial resistance (AMR). In a recent discussion, he explained why common medicines are increasingly failing to treat infections that were once easily curable.

Antibiotic resistance is not a future problem—it is happening now. Rossolini stressed that the overuse and misuse of antibiotics in humans, animals, and agriculture are accelerating the crisis. The result: once-reliable drugs are losing their power, leaving doctors with fewer options to treat bacterial infections.

Impact on Safe Surgery and Routine Care

The threat extends beyond treating common infections. Rossolini pointed out that AMR is undermining the safety of routine surgeries, including caesarean sections, joint replacements, and chemotherapy. Without effective antibiotics to prevent infections during and after these procedures, patients face higher risks.

Organisations like the World Health Organization have flagged this as a major concern. World AMR Awareness Week 2026, observed this week, aims to educate the public and policymakers about the scale of the problem. The theme this year focuses on preserving antibiotics for future generations.

What the Numbers Say

While exact figures vary by region, experts estimate that drug-resistant infections already cause more than a million deaths annually worldwide. In India, the burden is particularly heavy due to high rates of antibiotic consumption and limited infection control measures in some healthcare settings.

Rossolini emphasised that without urgent action, even minor infections could become life-threatening. He called for stronger surveillance systems, better infection prevention, and more research into new antibiotics and alternative treatments.

The Role of Clinical Microbiology

The future of clinical microbiology is central to the fight against AMR. Rossolini explained that rapid diagnostic tests can help doctors prescribe the right antibiotic at the right dose, reducing unnecessary use. Laboratories must also improve their ability to detect and track resistant strains.

He noted that investment in microbiology is not just about technology—it is about people. Training more specialists and ensuring labs in low-resource settings have basic equipment are critical steps. Without these, the world will remain blind to the spread of resistance.

Global Coordination Needed

No single country can solve AMR alone. Rossolini highlighted the need for international cooperation on data sharing, antibiotic stewardship, and the development of new drugs. He pointed to initiatives like the Global Antimicrobial Resistance and Use Surveillance System as positive steps, but said much more is needed.

In India, the government has launched a national action plan on AMR, but implementation remains uneven. Experts argue that stricter regulations on over-the-counter antibiotic sales and better public awareness are essential.

What comes next? The coming years will test whether governments, pharmaceutical companies, and healthcare systems can act with the urgency the crisis demands. Without coordinated action, the era of miracle drugs may soon come to an end.
