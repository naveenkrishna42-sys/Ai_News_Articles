AI Redesigns Botox Enzyme, Shrinks Protein Without Losing Function

📅 2026-07-30 📂 Science Original source ↗
Key points
  • AI tool redesigned the Botox enzyme, reducing its size by 30% without losing function.
  • Lab experiments confirmed the shrunk enzyme retained its biological activity.
  • Method combines AI design with directed evolution to push proteins beyond natural limits.

Molecular Shrink Ray

Scientists have used artificial intelligence to shrink a key enzyme used in Botox treatments by nearly a third, without destroying its function. The breakthrough, reported by researchers from the University of Washington and collaborating institutions, demonstrates how AI can redesign proteins to be smaller, more stable, and potentially more useful for medicine and industry.

The enzyme in question is botulinum neurotoxin, the active ingredient in Botox. Its natural form is large and complex, which makes it difficult to manufacture and deliver. The AI tool, called ProteinMPNN, was tasked with redesigning the enzyme to fold into a smaller structure while preserving its ability to cleave specific proteins — its core biological job.

AI Design Meets Lab Evolution

The team combined computational protein design with directed evolution, a lab technique that mimics natural selection. First, the AI generated thousands of potential smaller versions of the enzyme. Then, researchers screened these designs in the lab, testing which ones still worked. The best candidates were further mutated and tested in cycles, pushing the enzyme beyond what evolution alone had achieved.

“We essentially used AI to propose a completely new protein backbone that is much smaller,” said one of the lead researchers in a statement. “Then we let evolution fine-tune it.” The final product was a functional enzyme roughly 30% smaller than the original, yet it retained its target specificity and catalytic activity.

Why Size Matters

Smaller proteins have several advantages. They are easier to produce in large quantities, more stable at room temperature, and can penetrate tissues more effectively. For Botox, a smaller enzyme could mean lower doses, fewer side effects, and cheaper manufacturing. Beyond cosmetics, botulinum toxin is used to treat muscle spasms, chronic migraines, and overactive bladder.

The approach is not limited to Botox. The same AI-driven workflow could be applied to other therapeutic enzymes, industrial catalysts, or even proteins used in lab diagnostics. “This is a general method to make any protein smaller without losing its function,” the researchers noted.

Limits of Natural Evolution

Natural evolution rarely produces the smallest possible version of a protein. Evolution optimises for survival, not for minimal size. The AI tool breaks that constraint by exploring structural possibilities that nature never tried. Directed evolution then ensures the redesigned protein actually works in the real world.

Critics caution that not all proteins will shrink gracefully. Some functions rely on large structural domains that cannot be compressed. But for many enzymes, the method appears robust. The team has already tested it on several other proteins with similar success.

What Comes Next

The researchers plan to test the shrunk Botox enzyme in animal models to see if it works as well as the natural version. If it does, clinical trials could follow. Meanwhile, the AI tool is being made available to other labs, accelerating the hunt for smaller, better proteins across biotechnology.

The next step is scaling up: can AI redesign entire metabolic pathways, not just single enzymes? That question will keep the field busy for years.
