The NLSIU Screening Controversy at a Glance

The Akhil Bharatiya Vidyarthi Parishad (ABVP) has formally urged the National Law School of India University (NLSIU), Bengaluru, to cancel a scheduled screening of a documentary centred on former JNU student leader Umar Khalid. The demand, reported by The Hindu, places one of India's most prestigious law campuses at the centre of a familiar national argument over political speech, campus autonomy and student safety.

Khalid has been in custody since 2020 in connection with the larger Delhi riots conspiracy case, and proceedings remain sub judice; this article references only the publicly known status of the matter and does not assert any finding of guilt. The documentary in question is a film, not a verdict, and the dispute is about whether a law school is the appropriate venue for it.

What ABVP Has Demanded and Why

ABVP's position rests on three pillars. First, the organisation argues that hosting a film centred on an accused in a pending trial amounts to political advocacy rather than legal education. Second, it claims the event could provoke confrontation between student groups, raising campus security concerns. Third, it contends that NLSIU, as a publicly funded institution, should avoid programming that aligns the university with one side of a live criminal proceeding.

The demand follows a pattern seen across Indian campuses in recent years, where student wings of national parties contest the framing of events rather than merely their logistics. The substantive question for NLSIU's administration is procedural: who decides what counts as academic content, and on what written criteria?

The Legal and Institutional Stakes for NLSIU

NLSIU operates as an autonomous institution under the National Law School of India University Act, Karnataka. Its event approval process typically runs through student bodies and the administration, with freedom-of-expression protections under Article 19(1)(a) of the Constitution applying to campus speech subject to reasonable restrictions on public order.

For a law university, the reputational calculus cuts both ways. Cancelling an approved event invites allegations of capitulation to political pressure. Proceeding without robust security planning risks disorder. Either outcome will be cited by other institutions facing similar requests.

Real-World Implications for Students and Institutions

For NLSIU students, the practical stakes are immediate: event permissions, security deployment and the reputational signal sent to future speakers. A cancellation could chill invitations to politically sensitive figures across the academic calendar. A forced go-ahead without consensus could push disagreements from debate into disruption.

  • Precedent risk: Every decision on contested events becomes a template for the next demand.
  • Security cost: Polarising screenings typically need additional marshals, access control and police coordination.
  • Academic framing: Events with structured legal discussion, faculty moderation and reading material are harder to characterise as pure advocacy.
  • Student body autonomy: The row tests how much programming discretion NLSIU's student bodies genuinely retain.
  • Public perception: Alumni, recruiters and ranking bodies watch how law campuses handle free-expression disputes.

How Comparable Campus Disputes Have Played Out

Indian universities have handled similar demands in three broad ways: outright cancellation, conditional permission with enhanced security, or relocation to a closed academic setting. Conditional permission has generally proven the least damaging option, because it preserves the event while addressing the stated safety concern in writing.

For NLSIU, that middle path would require the organisers to publish a session framework, include a faculty respondent, and accept a code of conduct for attendees. Such conditions are administratively routine and legally defensible, and they deny both sides the narrative of outright suppression or unchecked provocation.

The Bottom Line

The ABVP demand at NLSIU is less about one documentary and more about who controls the campus calendar. The university's written response, expected through official channels, will define its stance for the academic year. Students, faculty and observers should track three things: the formal grounds cited for any decision, the security plan disclosed, and whether the approval process is applied consistently to events of all political hues.
