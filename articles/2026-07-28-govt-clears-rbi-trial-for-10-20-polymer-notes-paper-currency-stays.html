Govt clears RBI trial for ₹10, ₹20 polymer notes, paper currency stays

📅 2026-07-28 📂 Banking Original source ↗
Key points
  • The finance ministry has given the green light for limited trials of polymer notes in ₹10 and ₹20 denominations.
  • The RBI will conduct field tests to assess durability, hygiene, and counterfeiting resistance.
  • The government has categorically stated that paper currency will not be replaced.
  • Polymer notes are expected to last longer and stay cleaner than paper notes.
  • The pilot will be conducted in select cities before any wider rollout is considered.

The government has approved the Reserve Bank of India's proposal to conduct field trials of polymer banknotes in ₹10 and ₹20 denominations, according to official sources. The move is part of a long-standing exploration into alternative currency materials, but authorities were quick to clarify that there is no plan to phase out paper currency.

Pilot project details

The RBI will now begin limited circulation of polymer notes in select cities to test their performance under real-world conditions. The central bank will evaluate how these notes hold up in terms of wear and tear, hygiene, and resistance to counterfeiting.

Polymer notes are made from a thin, flexible plastic film. They are known to last two to three times longer than paper notes and do not absorb moisture or dirt as easily. Several countries, including Australia, Canada, and the United Kingdom, have already switched to polymer or hybrid notes for at least some denominations.

No replacement of paper currency

Senior officials in the finance ministry have stressed that this is strictly a pilot project. “There is no plan to replace the existing paper currency system,” a ministry spokesperson said. The government is merely testing whether polymer notes can coexist with paper notes or offer advantages in specific use cases.

The ₹10 and ₹20 notes were chosen for the trial because they are among the most commonly used denominations in daily transactions. Higher-value notes, such as ₹50, ₹100, ₹200, and ₹500, are not part of the current pilot.

Why polymer notes?

The RBI has been studying polymer currency for years. In 2017, it had floated a tender for the supply of polymer substrate for a proposed trial of ₹10 notes. That plan was later shelved. The renewed interest comes as the central bank looks for ways to reduce the cost of printing and replacing soiled notes.

Paper notes, especially in smaller denominations, wear out quickly in India's humid climate. A typical ₹10 paper note lasts about 10 to 12 months before it becomes unfit for circulation. Polymer notes could extend that lifespan significantly, potentially saving the government crores of rupees in printing costs annually.

Counterfeiting and environmental concerns

Polymer notes are also harder to counterfeit. They can incorporate transparent windows, holograms, and other security features that are difficult to replicate with conventional paper. However, they are not without drawbacks. Environmental groups have raised concerns about the plastic content and the challenges of recycling polymer notes.

The RBI is expected to address these issues during the trial period. The central bank will also study how the notes behave in automatic teller machines and vending machines, which often have trouble handling plastic currency.

What happens next

The pilot is expected to run for several months in a few identified cities. The RBI will then submit a detailed report to the finance ministry on the feasibility of wider adoption. No timeline has been set for a potential national rollout, and the government has made it clear that a final decision will depend on the trial results.

For now, Indians will continue to use paper notes for most transactions. The polymer trial is a cautious first step, not a currency revolution.
