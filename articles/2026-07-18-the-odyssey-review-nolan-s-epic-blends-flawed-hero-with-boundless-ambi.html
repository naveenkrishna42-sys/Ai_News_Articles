The Odyssey review: Nolan’s epic blends flawed hero with boundless ambition

📅 2026-07-18 📂 Movies Original source ↗
Key points
  • Christopher Nolan’s The Odyssey is a visually stunning but flawed adaptation of Homer’s epic.
  • The film focuses on a complex, imperfect hero rather than a traditional mythic figure.
  • Nolan’s signature ambition and technical prowess are on full display, though the narrative sometimes struggles.
  • Critics praise the performances and scale but note pacing issues in the middle act.

A Hero for Our Time

Christopher Nolan’s The Odyssey is not your grandfather’s epic. The director, known for bending time and space in films like Inception and Interstellar, now turns his lens on Homer’s ancient tale of homecoming. The result is a film that is as ambitious as it is flawed — much like its protagonist.

Nolan’s Odysseus is no flawless demigod. He is a man haunted by the Trojan War, plagued by pride and doubt. This version of the hero struggles with his own humanity, making his decade-long journey back to Ithaca a battle against both external monsters and internal demons. It is a refreshingly modern take, one that prioritises character depth over mythic grandeur.

Visual Splendour and Technical Marvels

Visually, The Odyssey is a feast. Nolan has once again pushed the boundaries of practical effects and IMAX cinematography. The Cyclops is a towering, terrifying presence, brought to life without excessive CGI. The storm scenes at sea are visceral, with waves that feel like they could swallow the audience whole.

Hoyte van Hoytema’s camera work is breathtaking. From the sun-bleached shores of Aeaea to the dark, claustrophobic caves of the Laestrygonians, every frame looks like a painting. Hans Zimmer’s score, a mix of pounding percussion and haunting strings, amplifies the tension.

The Acting and Character Work

The cast delivers strong performances. The actor playing Odysseus brings a raw vulnerability to the role, making his cunning and bravery feel earned. Penelope, played with quiet strength, is given more agency than in traditional adaptations. She is not just waiting; she is scheming, holding the kingdom together with wit and will.

But not every character gets their due. Some of the crew members are thinly sketched, serving more as plot devices than people. When they die — and they do, often — the emotional weight is not always there. The film’s length, over two and a half hours, means some subplots feel rushed.

Ambition vs. Execution

Nolan’s ambition is both the film’s greatest strength and its occasional weakness. He tries to weave together multiple timelines, flashbacks to Troy, and the gods’ interventions. The result is a dense narrative that rewards attention but can confuse casual viewers.

The middle act drags. The encounters with Circe and the Sirens are visually arresting but lack narrative momentum. The film seems to pause, waiting for the next big set piece. The final act, however, is gripping. The bow contest and the slaughter of the suitors are staged with brutal, balletic precision.

Critics have noted that the film’s emotional core sometimes gets lost in the spectacle. The reunion between Odysseus and Penelope is poignant but feels rushed. After ten years of separation, a few minutes of screen time seem insufficient.

A Flawed but Essential Epic

The Odyssey is not a perfect film. It is messy, overstuffed, and occasionally self-indulgent. But it is also bold, beautiful, and deeply human. Nolan has crafted a blockbuster that refuses to talk down to its audience. It asks big questions about fate, free will, and what it means to come home.

For fans of Nolan and epic cinema, this is a must-watch. For others, it may be a test of patience. Either way, it is a film that will be discussed for years.

What comes next for Nolan is unclear, but with The Odyssey, he has proven that even a flawed hero can deliver a memorable journey. The box office and awards season will tell if this gamble pays off.
