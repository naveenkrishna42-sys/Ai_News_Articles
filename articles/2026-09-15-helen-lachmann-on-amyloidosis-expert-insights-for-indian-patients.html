The Growing Burden of Amyloidosis in India

Amyloidosis, a rare group of disorders caused by misfolded proteins depositing in organs, is gaining attention in India as diagnostic capabilities improve. Prof. Helen Lachmann, a world-renowned expert from the UK National Amyloidosis Centre, recently shared insights on transforming care pathways. For Indian patients, the challenge is twofold: awareness and affordability. With an estimated 5,000-7,000 new cases annually in India, most present late with advanced organ damage.

Lachmann emphasises that early suspicion by primary care physicians can drastically alter outcomes. Unlike Western cohorts where AL amyloidosis dominates, Indian data suggests a higher proportion of AA amyloidosis linked to chronic infections like tuberculosis. This epidemiological difference demands tailored screening protocols.

Diagnostic Advances and Real-World Benchmarks

Confirming amyloidosis requires tissue biopsy with Congo red staining, followed by typing via mass spectrometry or immunohistochemistry. In India, access to these tests is limited to tertiary centres. Lachmann advocates for non-invasive techniques like cardiac MRI and scintigraphy using 99mTc-DPD, which can diagnose ATTR amyloidosis without biopsy. However, these scans cost ₹15,000-25,000 and are not widely reimbursed.

Genetic testing for hereditary ATTR (hATTR) is crucial, especially in endemic regions like Kerala. A 2025 study from CMC Vellore found a 12% prevalence of the V122I mutation among heart failure patients with thickened ventricles. Lachmann stresses that cascade screening of family members can identify carriers decades before symptoms.

Treatment Access and Value Justification

For ATTR amyloidosis, tafamidis stabilises the TTR tetramer and slows neuropathy and cardiomyopathy. The ATTR-ACT trial showed a 30% reduction in mortality at 30 months. Yet at ₹18-22 lakh per year, it remains out of reach for most Indians. Lachmann notes that the UK's NICE initially rejected tafamidis on cost-effectiveness grounds, but real-world data led to a managed access agreement. India needs similar risk-sharing models.

For AL amyloidosis, autologous stem cell transplant (ASCT) remains the gold standard for eligible patients, costing ₹15-25 lakh. Bortezomib-based regimens are cheaper (₹2-4 lakh per cycle) but require 4-6 cycles. Lachmann highlights that daratumumab, recently approved in India, improves haematologic response but adds ₹1.5 lakh per infusion.

Key Real-World Highlights and Practical Takeaways

  • Early referral saves organs: Patients with unexplained proteinuria, heart failure with preserved ejection fraction, or peripheral neuropathy should be screened for amyloidosis.
  • Genetic testing is underutilised: In Kerala and Tamil Nadu, hATTR clusters warrant proactive family screening.
  • Cost barriers are surmountable: Patient assistance programmes and crowdfunding are bridging gaps, but policy-level solutions are needed.
  • Telemedicine expands expertise: Lachmann's centre offers remote second opinions; Indian doctors can replicate this via platforms like eSanjeevani.
  • Clinical trials offer hope: Several siRNA and CRISPR-based therapies are in phase 3; Indian sites are participating in global studies.

What Lies Ahead for Indian Amyloidosis Care

Lachmann advocates for a national registry to capture real-world data and guide resource allocation. She also calls for subsidised tafamidis through the Jan Aushadhi scheme once generics launch. For now, awareness campaigns targeting cardiologists, nephrologists, and neurologists are paramount. As India's elderly population grows, amyloidosis will emerge from obscurity—and with experts like Lachmann guiding the way, the healthcare system can prepare.
