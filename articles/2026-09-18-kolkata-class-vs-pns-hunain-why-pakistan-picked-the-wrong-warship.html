Kolkata-Class vs PNS Hunain: Why Pakistan Picked the Wrong Warship

Key points
  • The Kolkata-class destroyer outguns and outranges PNS Hunain, a Chinese-built Type 054A/P frigate, in every major warfare domain.
  • India's rejection of Pakistan's 'usual prevarication' signals a deliberate grey-zone provocation that New Delhi will not accept.
  • The 1991 India-Pakistan Agreement on Prevention of Incidents at Sea is now under stress, raising escalation risks in the Arabian Sea.

What Happened in the Arabian Sea?

On 16 September 2026, an Indian Navy Kolkata-class destroyer and Pakistan Navy's PNS Hunain collided in the Arabian Sea, triggering a sharp diplomatic row. Pakistan alleged the Indian warship violated maritime norms; India rejected what it called Pakistan's 'usual prevarication' and summoned the Pakistani envoy. The incident is the first direct India-Pakistan naval collision in decades and has revived scrutiny of the 1991 Agreement on Prevention of Incidents at Sea.

Platform Comparison: Kolkata-Class vs PNS Hunain

The Kolkata-class is a 7,400-tonne guided-missile destroyer built by Mazagon Dock Shipbuilders, carrying 32 Barak-8 surface-to-air missiles, 16 BrahMos supersonic cruise missiles and a 76mm main gun. PNS Hunain is a 4,000-tonne Type 054A/P frigate built by China State Shipbuilding Corporation, with 32 HQ-16 SAMs, eight C-802 anti-ship missiles and a 76mm gun. The Kolkata displaces nearly twice as much, carries longer-range BrahMos (450+ km) versus C-802 (180 km), and fields a more capable radar and combat suite.

Grey-Zone Tactics and the 1991 Agreement

Pakistan's use of a smaller, cheaper frigate in a deliberate collision fits a classic grey-zone playbook: provoke without crossing the threshold of full-scale war. The 1991 bilateral agreement mandates safe manoeuvring and forbids simulated attacks, but it lacks an enforcement mechanism. India's sharp rebuttal signals that it will not accept such tactics, and any repeat could force New Delhi to raise the cost of provocation through aggressive naval patrolling or economic measures.

Buyer Implications: What This Means for Defence Procurement

For Indian taxpayers and procurement planners, the Kolkata-class proves that indigenous shipbuilding can deliver world-class warships. The follow-on Visakhapatnam-class and Project 18 destroyers will carry even more capable missiles and sensors. Pakistan's reliance on Chinese exports, by contrast, locks it into a lower-tier capability with shorter-range weapons and less robust logistics. The collision also highlights the importance of investing in naval domain awareness, satellite surveillance and secure communications to avoid accidental escalation.

Key Takeaways

  • Firepower: Kolkata-class carries BrahMos with a 450+ km range versus Hunain's 180 km C-802, giving India a decisive first-strike advantage.
  • Survivability: Barak-8's 100 km intercept range outmatches HQ-16's 40 km, providing a wider defensive bubble.
  • Cost: India's ₹11,000 crore per destroyer buys twice the displacement and superior weaponry compared to Pakistan's ₹3,500 crore frigate.
  • Risk: The 1991 agreement is toothless; India must enforce red lines through operational deterrence, not just diplomacy.

Pakistan's decision to deploy a smaller frigate against a Kolkata-class destroyer was militarily irrational and diplomatically costly. India's rejection of Pakistan's claims underscores a simple truth: in any naval confrontation, capability matters. The Kolkata-class remains the wrong warship for Pakistan to mess with.
