Mumbai Police Bust ₹24 Crore Cyber Fraud Racket, Seize 2,898 SIM Cards

Key points
  • Mumbai police arrested three accused in a cyber-fraud network linked to ₹24 crore in illegal transactions and 2,898 seized SIM cards.
  • The operation points to bulk SIM procurement and mule-account abuse as the backbone of organised digital fraud in India.
  • Readers should treat unsolicited KYC, loan, and job-offer calls as high-risk vectors and report fraud within the golden 24-hour window.

The Arrest and What Investigators Seized

Mumbai police have arrested three men accused of running a cyber-fraud network tied to illegal transactions worth roughly ₹24 crore, alongside the seizure of 2,898 SIM cards. The scale of the SIM haul is the headline number here. Bulk quantities of pre-activated connections are the raw material of organised digital fraud, and recovering them in one operation suggests a distribution hub rather than a handful of independent callers.

The case was first reported by The Hindu. Details on the exact modus operandi remain limited, so buyers and banking customers should read this as a structural warning about how fraud supply chains operate, not as a closed case.

How a 2,898-SIM Network Actually Works

Fraud operations rarely rely on one person making calls. They rely on volume: hundreds of numbers cycling through victims, each discarded before a complaint is traced. SIM cards obtained in bulk, often through forged documents or complicit retail points, feed this churn.

Money moves separately. Fraudsters route stolen funds through mule bank accounts, layered across UPI handles, wallets, and shell entities, before converting to cash or crypto. A ₹24 crore figure is consistent with a network that ran for months, not weeks.

Why the SIM Count Matters More Than the Rupee Figure

₹24 crore is recoverable in theory. Nearly 3,000 active SIMs in criminal hands represent future fraud capacity that would have been used against thousands of households. Every seized card is a call centre seat that never went live.

Practical Implications for Everyday Users

Most people assume fraud requires technical sophistication. It does not. The common entry points remain unsolicited calls about KYC expiry, loan approvals, job offers, or courier disputes. A large SIM inventory makes such calls cheaper and harder to trace.

  • Key insight: Bulk SIM seizure indicates an organised distribution layer, not isolated scammers.
  • Risk factor: UPI and wallet mule accounts remain the fastest route for laundering stolen funds.
  • Actionable step: Report fraud to 1930 and the cybercrime portal within 24 hours, when fund recall is most likely.
  • Limitation: Police have not disclosed the full method or victim count, so loss recovery figures remain unverified.

What This Means for India's Digital Payment Ecosystem

Cases of this size expose a persistent gap: SIM verification and bank onboarding are separate processes, and fraudsters exploit the seams. Telecom reforms and stricter KYC norms have helped, yet retail-level lapses continue to supply the market.

For consumers, the practical takeaway is simple. Never share OTPs, never install remote-access apps on a caller's instruction, and verify any loan or KYC claim through official bank channels. For regulators, the priority is tighter bulk-SIM monitoring and faster mule-account freezing.

Bottom Line

A ₹24 crore fraud network and 2,898 SIM cards are symptoms of an ecosystem problem, not a one-off crime. The Mumbai arrests disrupt a node, but the demand for cheap, untraceable connections will persist until verification systems close the gap.
