Nisar satellite sends first images of India from space, begins science ops

📅 2026-07-19 📂 Space Original source ↗
Key points
  • Nisar satellite has transmitted its first images of India from space.
  • The joint NASA-ISRO mission has officially commenced science operations.
  • Images show high-resolution details of terrain and infrastructure.

The Nisar satellite, a joint Earth observation mission between NASA and ISRO, has sent back its first striking images of India from space. The pictures, released on Friday, mark the formal start of science operations for the ambitious project.

First glimpse of India from orbit

The initial images capture parts of the Indian subcontinent with remarkable clarity. They reveal detailed terrain features, urban sprawl, and agricultural patterns across several states. Scientists involved with the mission described the pictures as exceptional in quality.

The satellite's synthetic aperture radar (SAR) can see through clouds and darkness, making it a powerful tool for continuous monitoring. The first data sets confirm that all onboard instruments are functioning as intended after the launch and orbital insertion earlier this year.

Science operations now live

With the successful transmission of these initial images, Nisar has moved from its commissioning phase into active science operations. The satellite will now begin its primary task: mapping the Earth's land and ice surfaces every 12 days.

ISRO officials stated that the data will support a wide range of applications, from disaster management to agricultural monitoring. The high revisit rate and all-weather imaging capability are expected to provide unprecedented insights into dynamic processes on the ground.

What Nisar will track

The mission is designed to observe changes in ecosystems, ice sheets, and natural hazards. It will measure ground movements with centimetre-level precision, helping scientists study earthquakes, landslides, and volcanic activity.

In India, the data is expected to aid in crop yield estimation, forest cover assessment, and water resource management. The collaboration combines NASA's expertise in SAR technology with ISRO's experience in satellite building and launch operations.

Global collaboration in action

Nisar represents one of the most significant joint space projects between the two countries. The satellite carries two different radar systems: an L-band radar from NASA and an S-band radar from ISRO. This dual-frequency approach allows for richer data collection.

The mission was launched from India's Satish Dhawan Space Centre in early 2025. It has since completed its orbit-raising manoeuvres and instrument calibration. The first images confirm that the partnership is delivering on its technical promises.

What happens next

Scientists will now process the initial data streams and begin distributing them to research institutions across the globe. The first full mapping cycle is expected to be completed within the next few weeks.

As Nisar settles into its operational rhythm, the focus will shift to analysing the continuous flow of information. The mission is designed to last at least three years, with the potential for extension. The world will be watching what this satellite reveals about our changing planet.
