Ashish Yadav's journey from dry pond training to U20 Worlds javelin silver

📅 2026-08-08 📂 Sports Original source ↗
Key points
  • Ashish Yadav clinched silver in men's javelin at the World Athletics U20 Championships.
  • He trained with wooden javelins in dry ponds due to lack of facilities.
  • His achievement comes 10 years after Neeraj Chopra's U20 Worlds gold.
  • Pooja Singh also qualified for the high jump final at the same event.

New Delhi: Ashish Yadav's silver medal at the World Athletics U20 Championships is a story of grit, scarce resources, and an unshakable belief. Ten years after Neeraj Chopra's historic gold at the same meet, Yadav has put Indian javelin throwing back in the global spotlight — but his path was anything but conventional.

Yadav, who hails from a modest background, did not have access to modern training facilities or high-end equipment. Instead, he trained in dry ponds, hurling a wooden javelin — a far cry from the carbon-fibre spears used by his competitors on the world stage.

From dry ponds to the world podium

In many parts of rural India, aspiring athletes often make do with whatever is available. For Yadav, that meant practicing in parched water bodies, their hard, uneven beds serving as his throwing sector. The wooden javelin, heavy and unbalanced compared to standard implements, forced him to focus intensely on technique and strength.

That unconventional preparation evidently paid off. At the U20 World Athletics Championships, Yadav produced a series of throws good enough to secure the silver medal, marking one of India's standout performances at the meet.

A decade after Neeraj Chopra

The significance of Yadav's achievement is amplified by its timing. It has been exactly ten years since Neeraj Chopra won gold at the same championships — a result that announced India's arrival in global javelin throwing. Chopra would later go on to win Olympic gold, becoming a national icon.

Yadav's silver, while a step below Chopra's gold, is being seen as evidence that the javelin pipeline in India is producing talent beyond just a solitary superstar. His success, built on minimal infrastructure, speaks to the raw potential waiting to be tapped in smaller towns and villages.

More Indian success at the championships

Yadav was not the only Indian to shine. Pooja Singh qualified for the high jump final, adding to the country's medal hopes. Her performance, alongside Yadav's podium finish, suggests a broader upswing in Indian athletics at the junior level.

While the spotlight naturally falls on javelin — given the sport's recent history in India — Singh's qualification underscores the growing depth across multiple disciplines. Athletics officials have not yet commented on the overall medal tally or future training plans for these athletes.

What this means for Indian athletics

Yadav's story resonates because it mirrors the journeys of many Indian athletes who succeed despite systemic gaps. The wooden javelin and dry ponds are not just details; they are symbols of a system that often demands extraordinary effort to overcome basic hurdles.

His silver medal will likely prompt renewed calls for better grassroots infrastructure, especially in javelin throwing, which has now produced two world-level medallists in a decade. Whether authorities act on that remains to be seen.

For now, Yadav's achievement stands on its own — a testament to what determination can accomplish, even without the tools most would deem essential. As he returns home, the focus will shift to how his career is nurtured from here, and whether India can convert such junior success into consistent senior-level performances.

The coming months will reveal if Yadav gets the support and equipment he lacked during his formative years — and whether his wooden-javelin days are truly behind him.
