A Brief History of Vaccination: From Smallpox to COVID-19

📅 2026-07-22 📂 Inventions Original source ↗
Key points
  • Vaccination began with Edward Jenner's smallpox vaccine in 1796.
  • Louis Pasteur developed vaccines for rabies and anthrax in the 1880s.
  • The WHO launched the Expanded Programme on Immunization in 1974.
  • Modern vaccines include mRNA technology used for COVID-19.

The Origins of Vaccination

The story of vaccination starts in the late 18th century. In 1796, English physician Edward Jenner noticed that milkmaids who caught cowpox did not get smallpox, a deadly disease. He scraped pus from a cowpox blister and inoculated an eight-year-old boy.

The boy developed a mild fever but recovered. When Jenner later exposed him to smallpox, the boy showed no symptoms. Jenner called his method 'vaccination', from the Latin word 'vacca', meaning cow.

Pasteur and the Germ Theory

Nearly a century later, French scientist Louis Pasteur built on Jenner's work. Pasteur developed vaccines for rabies and anthrax in the 1880s. His experiments proved that weakened forms of germs could trigger immunity.

Pasteur's rabies vaccine saved a nine-year-old boy bitten by a rabid dog in 1885. That success launched modern laboratory-based vaccine development. By the early 20th century, vaccines for cholera, typhoid, and plague had been created.

The Age of Mass Immunization

The mid-20th century brought a wave of new vaccines. Jonas Salk introduced the polio vaccine in 1955, and Albert Sabin's oral version followed in 1961. Measles, mumps, and rubella vaccines became routine in the 1960s and 1970s.

The World Health Organization launched the Expanded Programme on Immunization in 1974. The goal was to vaccinate children worldwide against diphtheria, tetanus, pertussis, polio, measles, and tuberculosis. By 1980, the WHO declared smallpox eradicated—the first human disease wiped out by vaccines.

Recent Breakthroughs

In the 1990s and 2000s, vaccines for hepatitis B, rotavirus, and human papillomavirus (HPV) became available. The HPV vaccine, first licensed in 2006, prevents cervical cancer. Researchers also developed vaccines for Ebola during the 2014 outbreak.

The COVID-19 pandemic accelerated vaccine technology. In 2020, mRNA vaccines from Pfizer-BioNTech and Moderna were approved in under a year. These vaccines use genetic instructions to trigger immunity, a technique now being studied for flu, HIV, and cancer.

Vaccines remain one of the most cost-effective public health tools. The WHO estimates they prevent 2 to 3 million deaths every year.

Looking ahead, scientists are developing vaccines for malaria, tuberculosis, and HIV. The challenge is to make them affordable and accessible in low-income countries. The next chapter of vaccination history is still being written.
