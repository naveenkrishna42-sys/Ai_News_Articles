The #ThatGirl trend: Inside Gen Z's quest for a perfect daily routine

📅 2026-08-09 📂 Lifestyle Original source ↗
Key points
  • The #ThatGirl trend promotes an idealized morning routine involving early wake-ups, exercise, and journaling.
  • Critics say the trend fosters toxic perfectionism and may harm mental health.
  • The trend reflects a broader cultural shift toward self-optimization among Gen Z.

On any given morning, thousands of young women across India wake up before sunrise, reach for their yoga mats, and brew a green smoothie. They are part of a global movement that has taken over social media feeds: #ThatGirl. But what began as a hashtag promoting wellness has quickly become a blueprint for a 'perfect' life, one that experts say may be more stress-inducing than stress-relieving.

What is #ThatGirl?

The trend features young women—often influencers—documenting their idealized morning routines. The formula is nearly identical: wake up at 5 a.m., hydrate with lemon water, meditate, journal, and complete a workout before starting the day. Videos are set to calm lo-fi music, showing neatly arranged desks and color-coordinated planners.

These posts are designed to inspire, but they also carry an unspoken benchmark: if you aren't doing all this, you're falling behind. The hashtag has amassed billions of views on platforms like Instagram and TikTok, and Indian Gen Z users have adopted the trend with equal fervour.

The appeal of discipline

For many, #ThatGirl is less about aesthetics and more about regaining control in a chaotic world. After two years of pandemic-induced uncertainty, young people are seeking structure. A fixed routine offers a sense of agency, a way to feel productive when so much else feels out of hands.

"It's not about being perfect," says one Delhi-based college student who follows the trend, though she admits she sometimes feels pressured to keep up. "It's about showing up for yourself." She wakes at 6 a.m., does yoga, and writes three gratitude points in a journal—practices she says have improved her mental health.

When aspiration becomes anxiety

But the flip side is equally visible. Psychologists warn that the trend romanticises a level of discipline that is unsustainable for most people, especially students and young professionals juggling deadlines, commutes, and family responsibilities. "It creates a binary: you're either a productive 'That Girl' or a failure," says a Mumbai-based therapist who has seen clients internalise this pressure.

The aesthetic itself is a privilege. Lavish homes, expensive yoga classes, and time for elaborate breakfasts are not accessible to everyone. Yet the content rarely acknowledges this gap, leaving many viewers feeling inadequate rather than motivated.

Beyond the routine

Some influencers have started countering the trend with #NotThatGirl, posting realistic mornings that include snoozed alarms, unmade beds, and leftover dinner for breakfast. This backlash is gaining traction, underscoring a growing fatigue with perfectionism.

Still, the core appeal of #ThatGirl remains. It taps into a universal desire to be better, to start fresh, to believe that a few small habits can transform a life. That hope is not harmful in itself—the harm lies in measuring one's worth against a filtered highlight reel.

As the trend evolves, the conversation is shifting toward self-compassion over self-optimization. The next iteration of #ThatGirl may well celebrate the messy, imperfect, and very human process of building a routine that actually sticks—not one that looks good on camera but leaves you exhausted in real life.

What to watch

Expect brands and wellness apps to capitalise on this moment, offering journals and courses that promise a 'That Girl' lifestyle. But the real test is whether Gen Z will continue to chase perfection or embrace a more forgiving version of self-improvement.
