Perseid meteors and solar eclipse: Will shooting stars appear on Aug 12?

📅 2026-08-11 📂 Science Original source ↗
Key points
  • The Perseid meteor shower peaks around Aug. 12, the same day as a total solar eclipse.
  • Daylight and the moon's glare may obscure meteors during the eclipse.
  • Astronomers advise focusing on the eclipse and catching the Perseids on surrounding nights.

A Rare Celestial Double Feature

On August 12, 2026, skywatchers across parts of the globe will be treated to a rare celestial event: a total solar eclipse coinciding with the peak of the Perseid meteor shower. The question on many minds is whether shooting stars will streak across the darkened sky during the eclipse. The short answer, according to astronomers, is complicated.

The Perseids, one of the most anticipated meteor showers of the year, typically produce up to 100 meteors per hour at their peak. But their visibility depends on several factors, including the timing of the eclipse and the phase of the moon. This year, the moon will be near full, which could wash out fainter meteors.

During a total solar eclipse, the sky darkens dramatically for a few minutes, creating conditions that might allow brighter meteors to be seen. However, the eclipse's path of totality—where the sun is fully blocked—will be limited to specific regions. Observers outside this path will see only a partial eclipse, with little darkening effect.

The Perseid Peak and Eclipse Timing

The Perseid meteor shower is active from mid-July to late August, with its peak occurring around August 12-13. This year, the peak falls on the same day as the solar eclipse, a convergence that has generated significant excitement among astronomy enthusiasts. But the timing of the peak is not exact; meteors can be seen for several days before and after.

During totality, the sky can become dark enough to spot stars and possibly a few bright meteors. Yet, the duration of totality—typically a few minutes—limits the window for spotting shooting stars. Moreover, the sun's corona, which is visible during totality, may draw the eye away from other parts of the sky.

Experts suggest that the best chance of seeing Perseids during the eclipse would be in areas where totality occurs shortly after midnight, when the meteor shower is most active. However, most eclipse paths occur during daytime hours, making meteor observation difficult.

What Astronomers Recommend

Astronomers advise that skywatchers should not pin their hopes on seeing meteors during the eclipse. The primary focus should be on the eclipse itself, which is a spectacular event in its own right. For those eager to catch the Perseids, the nights before and after the eclipse will offer better conditions, especially after midnight when the radiant point is high in the sky.

The moon's phase is another hurdle. A nearly full moon will brighten the sky throughout the night, reducing the visibility of fainter meteors. Observers in rural areas with clear skies and minimal light pollution will have the best chance of seeing the brighter Perseids.

Regional Visibility and Expectations

In India, the eclipse will be visible as a partial solar eclipse, with the moon covering a portion of the sun. The Perseids, however, will be less prominent due to the moon's glare and the fact that the shower's radiant is in the northern sky. Indian skywatchers may see a few bright meteors in the pre-dawn hours, but the show will not be as spectacular as in darker, clearer conditions.

Reports suggest that regions like Utah in the United States might miss the "biggest stargazing night of 2026" due to cloud cover or other factors. Similarly, other locations along the eclipse path could face weather challenges, which could further reduce the chances of seeing meteors.

Despite the uncertainties, the convergence of these two events offers a unique opportunity for photographers and astronomers to capture both phenomena. Some may attempt to photograph meteors during totality, though the short duration and the need for specialized equipment make this a challenging endeavor.

Looking Ahead

As August 12 approaches, skywatchers are advised to check local weather forecasts and plan their viewing locations carefully. While the Perseids may not be visible during the eclipse for most, the surrounding nights promise good meteor activity, weather permitting. The eclipse, meanwhile, will be a major event for those in its path, with many planning to travel to experience totality.

For those who miss the eclipse, the next total solar eclipse visible from India is expected in the coming years, but the combination of a major meteor shower and an eclipse is a rare treat. Astronomers encourage everyone to step outside, look up, and enjoy whatever the sky offers—whether it's a fleeting meteor or the awe-inspiring sight of the sun's corona.

As the date draws near, updates from meteorological and astronomical agencies will provide clearer guidance on visibility conditions. Until then, keep your eyes on the skies and your cameras ready.
