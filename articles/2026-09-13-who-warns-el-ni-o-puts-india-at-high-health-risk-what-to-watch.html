WHO Warns: El Niño Puts India at High Health Risk — What to Watch

✍️ By TIVRA News Science & Health Desk 📅 2026-09-13 📂 Health Original source ↗
Key points
  • WHO classifies India among high-risk countries as El Niño conditions strengthen, warning of dengue surges and heat-related illness.
  • El Niño typically suppresses monsoon rainfall while spiking pre-monsoon and post-monsoon temperatures, expanding the Aedes aegypti mosquito's breeding window.
  • States including Kerala, Maharashtra, Tamil Nadu, West Bengal and Jammu & Kashmir are already on alert, with early-September heat recorded in Karnataka.
  • Vector control, ORS availability, heat action plans and district-level surveillance are the most cost-effective defences, say public health experts.
  • Vulnerable groups — children under 5, pregnant women, the elderly and outdoor workers — face the sharpest risk and need targeted intervention.

The World Health Organization has flagged India among the countries facing a high health risk as El Niño conditions strengthen across the equatorial Pacific. The warning, carried by News18 and echoed by NDTV, Greater Kashmir and Deccan Herald, points to a familiar but dangerous chain: a warming ocean shifts monsoon patterns, extends the mosquito breeding season, and pushes heat stress into months that were once relatively mild.

What El Niño Does to India's Weather

El Niño is a periodic warming of sea-surface temperatures in the central and eastern Pacific. For India, its most reliable signature is a weakened southwest monsoon, with rainfall distribution turning erratic rather than uniformly deficient. Some districts get cloudbursts; others get multi-week dry spells. Both extremes create public health problems.

This year's signal is arriving alongside an unusually hot September. Deccan Herald reported early-September heat in Tumakuru, Karnataka — the kind of anomaly that shortens the gap between monsoon and post-monsoon mosquito seasons.

Why Dengue Becomes the Primary Threat

The Aedes aegypti mosquito breeds in clean, stagnant water and thrives when temperatures sit between 25°C and 32°C. A dry spell followed by intermittent rain is close to ideal for it: stored water accumulates in tanks, tyres and construction sites, and warmer ambient temperatures accelerate the virus's incubation inside the mosquito.

Public health experts therefore expect a longer dengue transmission window, stretching from August into November in peninsular India and into December in the northeast. Severe dengue — with plasma leakage, bleeding and organ impairment — is the outcome that overwhelms district hospitals.

States Already on Alert

Jammu & Kashmir has issued an advisory, according to Greater Kashmir, even though the Himalayan region traditionally sees limited dengue. Kerala, Maharashtra, Tamil Nadu, West Bengal and Uttar Pradesh remain the highest-burden states in most years. The WHO's risk framing matters because it pushes state health departments to pre-position testing kits, platelets and vector-control teams before case counts spike.

Heat Illness: The Underrated Risk

El Niño years are associated with above-normal pre-monsoon and post-monsoon temperatures. Heat stroke, heat exhaustion and dehydration-related kidney injury rise sharply among outdoor workers — construction labourers, farmers, delivery riders and traffic police. The economic cost is real: a single heatstroke ICU admission can run into several lakh rupees, and lost wages hit daily-wage households hardest.

Practical Preparedness: What Actually Works

  • Vector control: Source reduction — emptying water containers weekly — remains more effective than fogging alone, which only kills adult mosquitoes temporarily.
  • Heat action plans: Cities with functioning heat action plans report measurably lower heat mortality. Ensure local plans are activated, not just filed.
  • ORS and hydration: Oral rehydration salts cost a few rupees per sachet and prevent most dehydration admissions. Stock them at primary health centres and Anganwadi centres.
  • Early testing: NS1 antigen tests within the first five days of fever improve dengue detection and reduce severe-case progression.
  • Workplace protection: Shift outdoor work to early mornings, provide shade and drinking water, and train supervisors to recognise heat exhaustion.

Pros and Cons of the Current Response

  • Pros: Early WHO warning gives states lead time; existing national vector-borne disease and heat action frameworks can be scaled quickly; surveillance systems already track dengue serotypes.
  • Cons: El Niño forecasts carry uncertainty, so complacency is a risk; district-level data lags by weeks; many states lack dedicated entomologists; public awareness campaigns typically start only after the first deaths.

What Buyers and Households Should Do

For families, the practical spend is small: mosquito repellents, window screens, covered water storage, and a basic thermometer and ORS stock. For employers, the calculus is sharper — a heat-illness incident costs far more in lost productivity and liability than preventive measures. Municipal corporations should treat drain clearing and larvicide spraying as a fixed pre-monsoon calendar item, not an emergency response.

The WHO's warning is a forecast, not a verdict. India's dengue and heat outcomes will depend less on the Pacific Ocean and more on whether district administrations act in September rather than November.
