Microsoft unveils Majorana 1 chip, claims breakthrough in quantum computing

📅 2026-07-18 📂 Inventions Original source ↗
Key points
  • Microsoft announces Majorana 1 chip, its first quantum processor based on topological qubits.
  • The chip uses a new state of matter called a topological superconductor to improve qubit stability.
  • Majorana 1 is designed to scale to a million qubits, a key milestone for practical quantum computing.

Microsoft enters quantum race with topological qubit chip

Microsoft has unveiled the Majorana 1 chip, its first quantum processor built around a new type of qubit that the company says could leapfrog existing approaches. The chip, announced on Wednesday, is the result of nearly two decades of research into topological qubits — a method that promises greater stability and scalability than current quantum systems.

Unlike conventional quantum processors that use superconducting loops or trapped ions, Microsoft’s design relies on a particle called the Majorana fermion. The company claims to have created a new state of matter — a topological superconductor — that can host these particles and make them usable for computation.

How Majorana 1 differs from existing quantum chips

Most quantum computers today are limited by noise and errors. Qubits, the basic units of quantum information, are fragile and require extreme cooling and error correction. Microsoft’s topological approach aims to build qubits that are inherently protected from environmental disturbances.

The Majorana 1 chip uses a superconducting nanowire made of indium arsenide and aluminum. When cooled to near absolute zero, the wire can be tuned to create Majorana zero modes — particles that store quantum information in a way that is resistant to decoherence.

Microsoft researchers say this design reduces the overhead needed for error correction, which currently consumes most of the computational power in competing systems. The company believes this could eventually allow a single chip to scale to one million qubits, a threshold widely seen as necessary for solving real-world problems like drug discovery and materials science.

Years of research, a single chip

Microsoft has been working on topological quantum computing since 2005. The effort has seen several setbacks, including a 2021 retraction of a Nature paper that claimed to have observed Majorana particles. The company says the latest results, published in Nature on Wednesday, are based on more rigorous measurements and a different experimental setup.

The chip itself is small — about the size of a fingernail — but Microsoft says its architecture is designed to be modular. Multiple Majorana 1 chips could be linked together to form a larger quantum processor. The company has not released a timeline for when such a system might be available commercially.

Competitors like Google, IBM and IonQ have already demonstrated quantum processors with dozens or hundreds of qubits, but they have struggled to reduce error rates. Microsoft’s approach, if validated, could offer a different path — one that prioritises qubit quality over quantity.

What happens next

The Majorana 1 chip is still a research device. Microsoft says it is now working on building a complete quantum computer that uses the new qubits. Independent researchers will need to replicate the results before the broader scientific community accepts the claim. For now, the chip marks a significant milestone — but the road to a million-qubit machine remains long.
