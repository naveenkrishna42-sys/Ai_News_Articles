Nikhil Gupta Gets 235 Months in US Court for Pannun Murder Plot

A 235-Month Sentence Handed Down in New York

Nikhil Gupta, an Indian national, has been sentenced to 235 months — just under 20 years — in a US federal prison for his role in a foiled plot to assassinate Gurpatwant Singh Pannun, a Sikh separatist leader and American citizen based in New York. The sentence was delivered in the Southern District of New York, closing a prosecution that has run since Gupta's arrest in Prague in June 2023 and has shadowed India–US diplomacy for more than three years.

Court records describe the case as a murder-for-hire conspiracy, not a politically motivated prosecution of speech or activism. Pannun leads Sikhs for Justice, an organisation India declared unlawful in 2020 and whose founder it separately designated a terrorist under the Unlawful Activities (Prevention) Act.

How the Plot Unravelled

Federal prosecutors alleged that Gupta negotiated a $100,000 contract to have Pannun killed and advanced a $15,000 cash payment. The alleged intermediary Gupta approached to arrange the hit turned out to be a confidential source working with US drug enforcement investigators, which allowed agents to record the discussions and build the case before any harm occurred.

Gupta was detained in Prague in June 2023 on a US warrant. Czech authorities cleared his extradition after a contested legal process, and he was transferred to American custody in June 2025. He subsequently pleaded guilty, which materially reduced his sentencing exposure compared with a full trial conviction.

The Alleged Role of "CC-1"

The indictment describes Gupta as acting at the direction of an Indian government employee identified only as "CC-1", who allegedly recruited him and coordinated logistics from India. Indian officials have denied any involvement in an assassination attempt, stated that such action would be contrary to government policy, and constituted a high-level committee to examine the inputs shared by Washington.

Why This Sentence Matters Beyond the Courtroom

The judgment is the first custodial sentence of its kind tied to allegations of state-linked targeting on US soil, and Washington will treat it as a deterrent signal. American prosecutors framed the case narrowly around contract killing, which insulated the proceedings from arguments about political speech or separatist advocacy.

For New Delhi, the practical cost is reputational and diplomatic rather than legal. The case sits alongside Canada's separate allegations concerning the killing of Hardeep Singh Nijjar in Surrey, British Columbia, in June 2023 — a matter that already triggered a sharp downturn in India–Canada ties. Washington has handled its own dispute more quietly, but the sentencing gives opposition voices and human-rights monitors a durable reference point when questioning India's commitments on transnational conduct.

Trade, defence procurement and technology cooperation between the two countries have continued through the dispute. Officials on both sides have consistently separated the criminal case from the broader partnership, and no sanctions or restrictions on Indian entities have followed from the proceedings.

Key Implications

  • Deterrence precedent: A near-20-year term sets a benchmark for future prosecutions involving alleged foreign-directed plots on US territory.
  • Guilty plea discount: Gupta's cooperation and plea likely spared him a substantially longer exposure had the matter gone to trial.
  • Unresolved question: The alleged role of "CC-1" remains the central unanswered element, and no Indian official has been charged in a US court.
  • Diplomatic insulation: Both governments have kept trade and defence engagement outside the case's perimeter, limiting economic fallout.
  • Civil liberties angle: Sikh advocacy groups in North America are likely to cite the ruling in fresh lobbying and legal filings.

Gupta's legal team retains limited post-sentencing options, and any appeal would focus on evidentiary and sentencing-procedure grounds rather than the underlying facts he has already admitted.
