Mumbai's potholes filled, but killer bumps remain on roads

📅 2026-07-28 📂 Mumbai Original source ↗
Key points
  • Pothole-free roads in Mumbai reveal new danger: uneven patches and badly designed bumps.
  • Fatal accidents reported due to sudden dips and raised sections on otherwise smooth roads.
  • Civic authorities yet to address the hazard or assign responsibility for road repairs.

From potholes to perilous bumps

Mumbai's roads have improved. The gaping potholes that once swallowed tyres and tempers are largely gone. But a new threat has emerged. Drivers and motorcyclists now face sudden, jarring bumps—irregular patches of asphalt, sharp ridges, and poorly levelled sections. These are not the speed breakers meant to slow traffic. They are unintended, uneven surfaces left behind after patchwork repairs.

These bumps have turned deadly. At least three fatal accidents were reported in the past month alone, according to local police records. In each case, a two-wheeler hit a raised section at speed, lost control, and crashed. Victims were thrown off their vehicles. Two died on the spot. One succumbed in hospital.

The hidden danger of smooth roads

Ironically, the danger spikes because the roads are otherwise smooth. Motorists accelerate, expecting a consistent surface. Then they hit a bump. The sudden jolt can cause loss of steering, especially on bikes and scooters. Car drivers face damage to axles and tyres. But the human cost is higher.

Residents in areas like Andheri, Bandra, and Kurla have flagged multiple spots. A stretch on the Western Express Highway near Vile Parle has a particularly nasty dip followed by a raised patch. Commuters describe it as a 'roller coaster' that has caused several near-misses.

Who is responsible?

The Brihanmumbai Municipal Corporation (BMC) has not yet assigned blame. Officials say the bumps are a result of incomplete road-laying and utility cuts. Water board, electricity, and telecom agencies dig up roads for repairs. They refill the trenches, but the surface rarely matches the original level. Over time, these patches settle unevenly.

“There is no co-ordination,” said a traffic police officer on condition of anonymity. “One agency digs, another fills, and nobody checks the final surface. We keep reporting these spots, but the response is slow.”

Residents have started filing RTI applications to find out which agency last dug at each accident site. But the process is cumbersome. Meanwhile, the bumps remain.

Fatal bumps: not a new problem

Road safety experts point out that this is not a Mumbai-specific issue. Cities across India have seen similar accidents after pothole-filling drives. The focus is always on filling holes, not on restoring a smooth, safe surface. The result is a patchwork of hazards.

“We celebrate pothole-free roads without checking what replaced them,” said a transport researcher at a Mumbai-based institute. “These bumps can be as dangerous as potholes, especially at night or in rain.”

In many cases, the bumps are only a few inches high. But at 60 km/h, that is enough to throw a rider off balance. Motorcyclists are particularly vulnerable. No helmet can protect against a fall caused by a poorly patched road.

What comes next

The BMC has promised to survey major roads and identify problematic bumps. But no timeline has been given. Activists plan to file a public interest litigation if action is not taken soon.

For now, Mumbai's commuters are learning to slow down even on smooth roads—because the next bump could be fatal.
