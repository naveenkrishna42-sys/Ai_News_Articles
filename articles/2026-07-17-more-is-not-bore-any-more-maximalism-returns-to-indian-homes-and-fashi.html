More is not bore any more: Maximalism returns to Indian homes and fashion

📅 2026-07-17 📂 Chennai Original source ↗
Key points
  • Designers and stylists in Chennai report a clear shift from minimalist aesthetics to maximalist styles in homes and wardrobes.
  • The trend is driven by a post-pandemic desire for joy, colour, and cultural reclamation.
  • Local artisans and traditional crafts are seeing renewed demand as part of this maximalist movement.

Chennai's design sensibilities are undergoing a dramatic shift. After years of clean lines, neutral palettes and 'less is more' mantras, the city is embracing its inner maximalist. From living rooms layered with Kanchipuram silks to wardrobes exploding with colour, the old fear of 'too much' is fading.

Interior designers across the State report a surge in requests for bold patterns, mixed textures and statement pieces that refuse to blend into the background. The minimalist era, it appears, is making room for something far more exuberant.

The shift from restraint to expression

For nearly a decade, Indian urban homes followed a global script: white walls, sparse furniture, and a single piece of art. That script is being rewritten. Homeowners are now asking for vibrant wallpapers, ornate furniture, and spaces that tell a story rather than whisper a design philosophy.

"People want their homes to feel alive," says a Chennai-based interior stylist. "After two years of being confined to the same four walls, the desire for visual stimulation is natural. Minimalism started feeling sterile."

The change is not limited to interiors. Wedding planners, fashion designers, and even event curators are noticing a preference for more — more colour, more texture, more detail.

Fashion embraces the bold

On the fashion front, Chennai's boutiques are stocking up on heavy embellishments, mixed prints, and oversized accessories. The understated elegance that once defined South Indian style is now competing with a bolder, more-is-more aesthetic.

Designers point to a generation that is unafraid of clashing colours or traditional motifs reimagined in contemporary cuts. "The younger crowd is not interested in fading into the background. They want to be seen, and they want their clothes to reflect their personality," notes a textile designer from the city.

This shift is also reviving interest in handloom weaves and indigenous crafts. The maximalist trend, unlike its Western counterpart, is deeply rooted in local traditions of layering and ornamentation.

Cultural reclamation through design

Maximalism in Chennai is not just about aesthetics; it is also about identity. There is a conscious move away from Western minimalism toward a celebration of Indian visual heritage. Grandmother's carved wooden chest, the family's collection of Tanjore paintings, and vintage temple jewellery are no longer seen as clutter but as curated statements.

Art galleries in the city report increased footfall for works that feature dense compositions and rich colours. Buyers are drawn to pieces that demand attention and spark conversation. The quiet, monochrome abstract is losing ground to figurative, narrative-driven art.

This trend aligns with a broader global resurgence of maximalism. But in Chennai, it carries a distinctly local flavour — one that merges contemporary design with centuries-old craft traditions.

What is driving the change?

Industry observers attribute the shift to several factors. The post-pandemic desire for comfort and joy plays a significant role. After months of uncertainty, people are seeking spaces and objects that feel celebratory. Social media, particularly Instagram, has also fuelled the trend by showcasing maximalist interiors and styling.

Additionally, a growing economic confidence among younger professionals is enabling bolder spending on home decor and fashion. They are willing to invest in statement pieces that reflect their journey and tastes.

"For a long time, the fear of making a mistake kept people playing safe," says a city-based lifestyle blogger. "Now, the fear is of being boring. That has changed everything."

What started as a quiet rebellion against minimalism is now a full-fledged movement. Chennai's homes, wardrobes, and public spaces are becoming braver, more colourful, and unapologetically excessive. The question is not whether maximalism will stay, but how far the city will go in embracing the beauty of more.
