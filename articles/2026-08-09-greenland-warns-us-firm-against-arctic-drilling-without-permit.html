Greenland warns US firm against Arctic drilling without permit

📅 2026-08-09 📂 World Original source ↗
Key points
  • Greenland's government has issued a stern warning to a US oil firm linked to Donald Trump over planned Arctic drilling.
  • The Texas-based company reportedly intends to proceed without obtaining the necessary permits from Greenlandic authorities.
  • The move has intensified a broader geopolitical and environmental dispute over Arctic resources.
  • Greenland has never produced oil commercially, making the project a high-stakes bet for the company.

Greenland's government has issued a sharply worded warning to a Texas-based oil company with ties to former US president Donald Trump, which is preparing to drill for oil in the Arctic without seeking the necessary permission from local authorities.

The dispute marks an escalation in a standoff over sovereignty, resources, and the rapidly changing geopolitics of the Arctic region. Greenlandic officials have made it clear that any drilling activity without explicit consent would be a violation of their laws and a direct challenge to their authority.

A billion-dollar bet under the ice

The company, which has never produced a barrel of oil in Greenland, is reportedly investing heavily in exploration beneath the ice sheet. This is a high-stakes gamble: Greenland remains one of the few places on Earth where vast oil reserves are still untapped.

For decades, the territory has resisted commercial drilling, citing environmental concerns and the fragility of its ecosystems. Now, a single US firm appears willing to bypass those barriers entirely, betting billions on the reserves that lie beneath the frozen surface.

The project has drawn attention not just for its scale, but for its political undertones. The firm's connection to Trump has added a layer of controversy, with critics suggesting the push is as much about political signalling as it is about energy exploration.

Greenland's tough stance

Greenland's government has responded with a "strong warning," according to reports, making clear that it will not tolerate unauthorised drilling. Officials have stressed that any operation must comply with local laws, which require permits and environmental assessments before work can begin.

The warning is not just about legal procedure; it is about control over a territory that is increasingly coveted by global powers. With melting ice opening new shipping routes and resource opportunities, Greenland has become a focal point for international interest, particularly from the United States, China, and Russia.

By taking a hard line, Greenland is signalling that it will not be sidelined in decisions about its own land and waters.

A history of no oil

Despite decades of geological surveys and exploratory interest, Greenland has never produced a single barrel of oil. Several international companies have attempted to drill in the past, but high costs, harsh conditions, and environmental opposition have consistently pushed them back.

The current Texas firm is undeterred, however, and appears ready to proceed regardless of the local government's objections. This has raised questions about what enforcement measures Greenland can actually take, given its limited military and economic resources and its reliance on Denmark for defence and foreign affairs.

The situation is further complicated by the strategic interests of larger powers. The United States has long viewed Greenland as a critical piece of its Arctic strategy, particularly through its Thule Air Base, and Trump himself has previously expressed interest in acquiring the island.

Environmental and geopolitical stakes

Environmental groups have condemned the planned drilling, warning that any accident in the Arctic could be catastrophic, with oil spills in icy waters being notoriously difficult to clean up. The region is home to unique wildlife, including polar bears, whales, and seals, and any industrial activity risks causing long-term damage.

Geopolitically, the situation is equally fraught. Greenland's government has been seeking to diversify its economy away from fishing and Danish subsidies, and oil revenues could transform its finances. But it also wants to maintain control over its own development, without being seen as a pawn in larger power games.

The firm's decision to push ahead without permission could force Greenland to take a firmer stance, potentially involving Danish authorities or international courts to resolve the dispute.

What happens next

With the company reportedly preparing to move ahead despite the warning, the coming weeks will be critical. Greenland is likely to seek legal and diplomatic support to enforce its position, while the firm may try to use political connections to gain leverage.

Observers will be watching closely to see whether this becomes a test of wills between a small Arctic territory and a powerful foreign company with political backing, and what it means for the future of Arctic drilling.
