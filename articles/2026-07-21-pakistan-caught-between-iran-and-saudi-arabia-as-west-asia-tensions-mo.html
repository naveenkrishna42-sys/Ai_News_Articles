Pakistan caught between Iran and Saudi Arabia as West Asia tensions mount

📅 2026-07-21 📂 World Original source ↗
Key points
  • Pakistan faces pressure to choose sides between Iran and Saudi Arabia amid Houthi naval blockade
  • Chinese oil tanker U-turns in Red Sea after Houthi attack threat, highlighting escalation
  • Islamabad's balancing act grows harder as regional proxy conflicts intensify

Pakistan finds itself in an increasingly tight spot as West Asia's fault lines deepen. Caught between its long-standing ties with Saudi Arabia and a delicate relationship with Iran, Islamabad is being forced to walk a tightrope that grows narrower by the day.

The immediate trigger is a series of escalations around the Red Sea. Yemen's Houthi rebels, backed by Iran, have declared a naval blockade of Saudi Arabia. A Chinese oil tanker recently performed a U-turn in the Red Sea after receiving threats of a Houthi attack. The blockade threatens to choke one of the world's busiest shipping lanes.

The Houthi blockade and its ripple effects

The Houthi move is not just a military provocation. It is a direct challenge to Saudi Arabia's regional standing and its ability to secure vital trade routes. The Bab el Mandeb strait, a narrow chokepoint between Yemen and Djibouti, is now a potential flashpoint for a wider conflict.

Oil prices are already feeling the heat. Analysts at Reuters note that a sustained Houthi blockade would lift prices, though workarounds could limit the impact. But the immediate danger is military: any Saudi retaliatory strike risks drawing in Iran, and by extension, Pakistan.

For Pakistan, the nightmare scenario is having to choose. Riyadh has in the past called on Islamabad for military support. Iran, meanwhile, shares a long and porous border with Pakistan and holds leverage through Shia populations in Balochistan and beyond.

Pakistan's balancing act

Islamabad has long styled itself as a mediator in West Asia. It maintains strong military and economic ties with Saudi Arabia. Thousands of Pakistanis work in the Kingdom. Remittances from Saudi Arabia are a lifeline for Pakistan's struggling economy.

But Iran is not a neighbour Pakistan can afford to alienate. The two countries have clashed over border security and smuggling, but also cooperate on energy and regional diplomacy. A complete break is unthinkable.

ThePrint's analysis highlights that Pakistan's "mediator who cannot choose" dilemma is not new. But the Houthi blockade has raised the stakes. Islamabad now faces the prospect of being pulled into a conflict it cannot control and cannot escape.

Chinese oil tanker U-turn underscores risks

The incident involving the Chinese oil tanker is a stark reminder of how quickly tensions can escalate. The vessel, reportedly carrying crude from the Middle East, reversed course in the Red Sea after receiving what sources described as a credible threat from Houthi forces.

China, a major buyer of Middle Eastern oil, has so far avoided direct involvement. But disruptions to tanker traffic could force Beijing to take a harder stance, further complicating the regional picture.

For Pakistan, the Chinese angle adds another layer of complexity. Beijing is Islamabad's closest ally and largest investor. Any instability that threatens Chinese oil supplies could strain that relationship.

What comes next

The Houthi blockade is unlikely to be lifted soon. Saudi Arabia will not back down easily, and Iran sees the Houthis as a valuable proxy. Pakistan's diplomatic options are limited. It can push for de-escalation, but its leverage over either side is thin.

Officials in Islamabad have not yet commented publicly on the latest developments. But behind the scenes, the pressure is mounting. The coming weeks will test whether Pakistan can maintain its role as a mediator or whether it will be forced to finally pick a side.
