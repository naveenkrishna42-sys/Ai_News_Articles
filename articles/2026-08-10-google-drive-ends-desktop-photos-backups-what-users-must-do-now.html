Google Drive ends desktop Photos backups: What users must do now

📅 2026-08-10 📂 Technology Original source ↗
Key points
  • Google Drive desktop app no longer backs up photos automatically from August 10.
  • Users need to manually back up photos to Google Photos or use alternative cloud services.
  • Existing backups in Google Drive remain accessible but no new uploads will occur.

End of an era for Google Drive desktop backups

Google has officially pulled the plug on the desktop app's Photos backup feature, effective August 10. The change, which has been rolling out to users globally, means that any photos and videos in your PC's designated folders will no longer sync automatically to Google Photos via Drive for Desktop.

The move has caught many users off guard, especially those who relied on the desktop app to offload camera rolls and screenshots without manual intervention. For years, the feature acted as a bridge between local storage and the cloud, letting users set and forget their photo backups. That convenience is now gone.

What exactly has changed?

Starting August 10, the Google Drive desktop application will stop uploading new photos and videos to Google Photos. Existing files already backed up remain safe and accessible in your Google account, but any new media added to your local folders will no longer be picked up automatically.

Users who attempt to adjust settings in the app will find the Photos backup toggle removed or disabled. Google has not yet confirmed whether this is a temporary measure or a permanent feature removal, but the language in official help pages suggests it is a permanent shift in strategy.

Why is Google doing this?

Google has been streamlining its storage services for years, pushing users toward Google Photos as the standalone destination for media. The desktop app's backup function often created confusion, with users unsure whether files were in Drive or Photos, leading to duplicate uploads and storage headaches.

By cutting the feature, Google simplifies its ecosystem and nudges users to adopt Google Photos' dedicated backup tools, including the web uploader and mobile app. The company has not issued a detailed public statement about the rationale, but the move aligns with its broader push to unify cloud storage under clearer product lines.

What should you do now?

If you have been relying on Drive for Desktop to back up your photos, you are not entirely out of luck. Here are a few practical steps to keep your memories safe:

  • Use Google Photos' web uploader: You can manually drag and drop photos into photos.google.com from your browser. It is straightforward but requires you to do it periodically.
  • Switch to the Google Photos desktop app: While there is no dedicated Windows or macOS app, Google Photos integrates with the Files app on ChromeOS and offers a web-based backup flow that can handle large batches.
  • Try alternative cloud services: Services like OneDrive, Dropbox, or iCloud offer desktop folder syncing that can include photo folders. Some even have auto-upload features for camera folders.
  • Use external drives: For those wary of cloud dependence, an external hard drive or a NAS device remains a reliable offline backup option.

If you want to keep using Google Drive for other file types, the desktop app continues to work normally for documents, spreadsheets, and other non-photo content. Only the Photos backup pipeline has been severed.

A word on storage management

Users who had enabled the backup feature may notice that their Google Drive storage quota is now less cluttered. Photos and videos previously counted toward Drive storage if they were uploaded via the desktop app, whereas uploads through Google Photos' own channels counted separately under the same 15GB free tier but with different management tools.

This change effectively forces users to choose a single workflow, reducing accidental duplication. However, it also means that anyone who relied on the desktop app to automatically free up phone storage by moving photos to the cloud will need to reassess their routine.

Tech forums are already buzzing with workarounds, including scripts that monitor folders and upload via the Google Photos API, but these are not officially supported and may break with future updates. For most users, the manual web upload or a third-party sync tool is the safest bet.

What happens next?

Google has not announced any replacement feature for desktop photo backups, and there is no word on whether a future update to Drive for Desktop will bring back the functionality. Users should monitor official Google Workspace updates for any announcements.

In the meantime, the onus is on individuals to adapt their backup habits. With cloud storage becoming increasingly fragmented, this move could push more users toward dedicated photo management tools or local backups. For now, the simplest advice is: if you value your photos, do not leave them sitting only on your PC's hard drive—take action today.
