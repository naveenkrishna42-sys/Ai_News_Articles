Indonesia ends 2025 with more disasters, stalled green reforms

📅 2026-08-07 📂 Environment Original source ↗
Key points
  • Increase in hydrometeorological disasters across Indonesia in 2025
  • Environmental reform efforts stalled amid regulatory and enforcement challenges
  • Experts call for stronger climate adaptation and policy action

Indonesia is ending 2025 on a sombre note, with a sharp uptick in natural disasters and little progress on the environmental reforms that were meant to curb them. From flash floods in Sumatra to landslides in Java, the year has underscored the country's vulnerability to climate-driven extremes.

The disaster spike has been driven largely by hydrometeorological events — floods, landslides, and extreme weather — which together account for the bulk of incidents reported across the archipelago. Local authorities have been stretched thin, with emergency responses often reactive rather than preventive.

A year of compounded crises

Throughout 2025, disaster management agencies logged dozens of significant events, many of which displaced thousands of residents and damaged critical infrastructure. In several districts, repeated flooding left communities in a cycle of recovery, with little time to rebuild before the next deluge.

The human toll has been significant, though officials have not yet released consolidated figures. What is clear is that the frequency and intensity of these events are outpacing the government's adaptation measures.

Experts point to deforestation, poor land-use planning, and inadequate drainage systems as aggravating factors. These are not new problems, but they remain largely unaddressed.

Reforms stuck in the pipeline

On the policy front, 2025 was supposed to be a turning point. Several environmental bills and regulatory updates were tabled, ranging from stricter emissions controls to better protection for peatlands and mangroves. But most have stalled in legislative limbo.

Industry lobbying and bureaucratic inertia have slowed momentum. Enforcement of existing rules, meanwhile, remains patchy, with illegal logging and land conversion continuing in many regions.

The gap between rhetoric and action has frustrated civil society groups, which had hoped for accelerated progress after the previous year's commitments. Instead, they say, the reform agenda has been diluted.

The cost of delay

The consequences of stalled reform are now visible in real time. Disaster response budgets are being consumed by repeated emergencies, leaving less room for long-term resilience projects.

Climate scientists have warned that without systemic changes, the frequency of such disasters will only rise. Indonesia, with its long coastline and mountainous terrain, is among the most exposed countries in the region.

There have been some bright spots — pilot projects on mangrove restoration and community-based early warning systems have shown promise. But these remain small-scale and underfunded.

What to watch in 2026

As the new year begins, the pressure is on President Prabowo Subianto's administration to translate environmental pledges into concrete action. Key legislative items, including a revised environmental protection bill, are expected to be revisited.

Whether they pass — and whether they are enforced — will determine if Indonesia can break the cycle of disaster and delay. For now, the outlook remains cautious, with the coming rainy season likely to test the country's preparedness once again.
