How stablecoins could reshape finance in the Global South

📅 2026-08-12 📂 Crypto Original source ↗
Key points
  • Stablecoins can address currency volatility and high remittance costs in emerging markets.
  • They enable faster cross-border trade settlements and broader access to dollar-denominated savings.
  • Regulatory clarity and digital infrastructure are critical to unlocking these benefits.

A digital lifeline for emerging economies

Stablecoins are increasingly being discussed not just as crypto market tools, but as serious instruments for financial inclusion. A new analysis from Brookings argues that these dollar-pegged digital assets could fundamentally reimagine how money moves across the Global South.

The premise is straightforward. For countries with unstable local currencies and limited access to global banking, stablecoins offer a bridge. They provide a store of value that doesn't depend on a central bank's credibility or a nation's fiscal health.

That has real-world appeal in places where inflation erodes savings and cross-border payments remain painfully slow.

Cheaper remittances, faster trade

The report highlights remittances as an obvious use case. Migrant workers sending money home often lose a significant chunk to fees and unfavourable exchange rates. Stablecoins can reduce those costs to near zero and settle in minutes, not days.

Trade finance is another area ripe for disruption. Small and medium enterprises in developing countries struggle to access letters of credit and other trade instruments. Stablecoin-based smart contracts could automate payment upon delivery, reducing the need for trusted intermediaries.

This could open up export opportunities for businesses that were previously locked out of formal trade channels.

Digital dollars and financial sovereignty

Perhaps the most transformative aspect is the ability to hold US dollars digitally without a bank account. For citizens in countries with capital controls or weak banking systems, stablecoins represent a parallel financial layer.

This raises important questions about monetary sovereignty. If a significant portion of a country's savings moves into dollar-denominated stablecoins, local central banks may find their policy tools blunted.

But Brookings suggests that with thoughtful regulation, these risks can be managed. The key is not to ban the technology, but to integrate it within a framework that protects consumers and preserves financial stability.

Infrastructure and regulation are the real hurdles

The report is careful not to paint a utopian picture. For stablecoins to gain traction in the Global South, several prerequisites must be met. Reliable internet and mobile penetration are essential, as are clear legal rules on how stablecoins are treated for tax, property, and contract purposes.

In many emerging markets, crypto regulation remains ambiguous or hostile. This uncertainty deters both users and institutional investors. Brookings argues that policymakers should focus on anti-money laundering compliance and consumer protection, rather than outright bans.

It also points to the need for interoperability with existing payment systems. Stablecoins cannot thrive in a silo; they need on-ramps and off-ramps that connect them to local bank accounts and mobile money wallets.

A development tool, not a silver bullet

The Brookings analysis positions stablecoins as a complement to, not a replacement for, traditional development finance. They are unlikely to solve structural problems like poor governance or lack of infrastructure, but they can make existing systems more efficient.

For development agencies, the report suggests pilot projects that test stablecoin-based payment corridors in specific sectors, such as agricultural supply chains or micro-insurance. These could provide valuable data on what works and what doesn't.

International organisations like the IMF and World Bank also have a role to play in setting technical standards and advising member states on prudent adoption.

What comes next will largely depend on how regulators in key markets like India, Nigeria, and Brazil respond. If they create space for innovation while addressing risks, stablecoins could become a meaningful part of the Global South's financial architecture by the end of this decade. The groundwork is being laid now, and the decisions made in the next couple of years will shape whether this technology delivers on its promise or remains a niche tool.
