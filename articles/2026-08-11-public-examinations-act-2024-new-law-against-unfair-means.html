Public Examinations Act 2024: New Law Against Unfair Means

📅 2026-08-11 📂 Education Original source ↗
Key points
  • The Act targets organised cheating, paper leaks, and use of unfair means in public examinations.
  • Offences under the Act are cognisable, non-bailable, and non-compoundable.
  • The law aims to bring transparency and accountability to recruitment exams conducted by central bodies.
  • Private exam bodies are excluded; the Act focuses on public recruitment examinations.

Centre Enforces Strict Law to Curb Exam Malpractices

The Public Examinations (Prevention of Unfair Means) Act 2024 has come into force, marking a decisive step by the central government to tackle cheating and paper leaks in recruitment exams. The law, which received presidential assent earlier this year, criminalises a range of unfair practices, from leaking question papers to tampering with answer sheets.

Under the new legislation, any person found guilty of indulging in unfair means in public examinations can face imprisonment of up to ten years and a fine of up to Rs 1 crore. The Act makes these offences cognisable, non-bailable, and non-compoundable, meaning police can arrest without a warrant, bail is not automatic, and the case cannot be withdrawn by the complainant.

What Constitutes Unfair Means

The Act defines unfair means broadly. It covers direct and indirect involvement in activities such as leaking question papers, providing unauthorised solutions, and tampering with computer networks or answer sheets. It also includes assisting candidates through electronic devices, impersonation, and any form of manipulation that compromises the integrity of the examination process.

Examination authorities, service providers, and even candidates themselves can be held liable. The law also targets middlemen and organised syndicates that profit from selling leaked papers or arranging proxy candidates. The definition of 'public examination' is limited to exams conducted by central government departments and their attached bodies, including recruitment agencies like the Union Public Service Commission and the Staff Selection Commission.

Key Provisions and Penalties

  • Offences under the Act are punishable with a minimum jail term of three to five years, extendable up to ten years.
  • The fine can range from Rs 1 lakh to Rs 1 crore, depending on the severity of the offence.
  • Examination service providers found negligent can be fined up to Rs 1 crore and may also face closure of business for up to four years.

The Act also provides for confiscation of property and proceeds obtained through illegal means. This provision is aimed at dismantling the financial backbone of cheating rackets that have plagued competitive exams across the country.

Exemptions and Complaints Mechanism

Notably, the Act does not apply to exams conducted by private universities or state-level recruitment bodies. It specifically targets central government exams, leaving state governments to enact their own laws or adopt similar measures. This has been a point of debate, as many recent paper leaks have occurred in state-level exams.

Complaints under the Act can be filed by the examination authority or an officer authorised by it. Investigations are to be conducted by officers of the rank of inspector or above, ensuring a degree of seniority in handling cases. The Act also mandates that trials be concluded within a reasonable timeframe, though it does not specify a strict deadline.

Mixed Reactions from Stakeholders

Education experts and student groups have largely welcomed the law as a deterrent, but some have questioned its effectiveness. Critics argue that the exclusion of private and state exams leaves a significant gap. Recruitment scams in states like Bihar and Gujarat have involved both central and state exams, and the lack of uniform application could push malpractices into the unregulated sphere.

Officials have not yet confirmed whether the government plans to extend the Act's coverage to state and private exams in the future. The law is seen as a strong signal, but its real test will be in its implementation and the speed of prosecutions.

What to Watch

The coming months will reveal how the Act is enforced in practice. The central government is expected to issue detailed rules on procedures and reporting mechanisms. Students and exam authorities alike will be watching the first few prosecutions under the law to gauge its actual impact.
