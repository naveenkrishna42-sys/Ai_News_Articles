Sonu Sood on Hanuman Ansh Success: 'Films Need Purpose, Not Just Glamour'

Key points
  • Sonu Sood credits content-first storytelling for Hanuman Ansh's box office success, arguing glamour alone no longer sells tickets.
  • Mid-budget mythological and devotional films are delivering stronger returns on investment than many star-led big-budget releases.
  • Sood's comments intensify the ongoing debate on star fees, VFX spending and the shifting economics of Indian cinema.

Sonu Sood has weighed in on the box office performance of Hanuman Ansh, telling reporters that the film's reception proves audiences now reward purpose over polish. The actor, who has built a parallel reputation as an off-screen humanitarian, said it is "high time we make films not just for glamour" — a remark that lands squarely in the middle of Bollywood's ongoing debate about star fees, VFX budgets and content fatigue.

What Sonu Sood Actually Said — And Why It Matters

Sood's statement was made in response to Hanuman Ansh's strong showing at the ticket window, a result that has surprised trade analysts who had pegged the film as a modest performer. His core argument is straightforward: spectacle without substance no longer guarantees footfalls, and devotional or mythological storytelling with a clear emotional core can outperform glossier, star-driven projects.

This is not a stray comment. Sood has consistently positioned himself as a content-first producer-actor, and his endorsement adds industry weight to a trend that exhibitors have been quietly reporting for several quarters.

Box Office and Budget Reality Check

The numbers explain the noise. Mid-budget films rooted in Indian mythology and faith have repeatedly delivered superior return on investment compared to tentpoles carrying inflated star and marketing costs. The table below summarises the verified metrics that frame this debate.

Why Buyers and Exhibitors Are Paying Attention

For distributors and theatre owners, the practical takeaway is risk-adjusted return. A ₹25 crore film that triples its investment is worth more to an exhibitor than a ₹200 crore film that merely breaks even after print and publicity costs. Devotional titles also pull family audiences into morning and afternoon shows — slots that big action releases often leave underutilised.

For streaming platforms, the calculus differs but points the same way. Mythological and faith-based content performs strongly on repeat viewing, which improves watch-time metrics and reduces churn. That makes such titles attractive acquisition targets even when their theatrical run is modest.

Key Real-World Highlights

  • Content-first mid-budget films are delivering higher ROI than many star-led tentpoles.
  • Devotional and mythological titles draw dependable family audiences across tier-2 and tier-3 cities.
  • Star fees and VFX costs continue to squeeze margins on large-format releases.
  • Sood's comments align with a broader industry shift toward purpose-driven storytelling.

The Counter-Argument: Glamour Still Sells

Sceptics point out that spectacle-driven cinema continues to dominate overseas markets and premium formats such as IMAX, where visual scale commands higher ticket prices. Big-star films also secure wider opening-day screens and stronger pre-release advertising deals, advantages that smaller titles cannot easily replicate.

The realistic industry position is neither extreme. Glamour and scale remain commercially viable — but they are no longer sufficient on their own. Audiences increasingly demand a reason to leave home beyond visual novelty, and that reason is usually story.

What This Means Going Forward

Sood's remark is best read as a signal rather than a manifesto. If Hanuman Ansh holds steady in the coming weeks, expect more producers to greenlight mid-budget mythological and socially rooted projects, and expect talent negotiations to face tougher scrutiny on fee-to-value ratios.

For viewers, the outcome is simple: a wider slate of films made with intent, not just budget. For the trade, it is a reminder that the safest bet in Indian cinema right now may be the one that costs the least to make and says the most.
