Delhi Moves to Regulate PG Accommodations After Deadly Collapse

✍️ By TIVRA News Editorial Team 📅 2026-09-09 📂 India Original source ↗
Key points
  • New licensing board will vet PG operators and ban political ownership.
  • Deposit limits and height restrictions aim to curb unsafe conversions.
  • Compliance costs could push small hosts to formalise or exit the market.

Background of the crisis

The collapse of a four‑storey paying‑guest (PG) building in East Delhi last month left ten dead and dozens injured. Investigations revealed that the structure, originally approved for a single‑family dwelling, had been illegally converted into a 34‑room hostel, exceeding the 17.5‑metre height limit that triggers fire‑safety scrutiny.

Delhi’s Municipal Corporation (MCD) responded by demolishing 34 non‑compliant structures and sealing 17 more. Over 1,200 PGs were surveyed in a city‑wide audit, exposing a systemic loophole that allowed owners to sidestep building‑code enforcement.

Proposed regulatory framework

1. Creation of a dedicated PG board

The state government plans to set up a PG Accommodation Board under the Housing Department. The board will issue licences, conduct periodic safety audits, and maintain a public registry of approved PGs.

2. Licensing fees and eligibility

Applicants will pay a non‑refundable licence fee of ₹25,000 for the first year, with a 10% annual increase thereafter. Ownership by sitting politicians or parties will be barred to eliminate conflicts of interest.

3. Deposit caps and rent controls

To protect tenants, the ceiling on security deposits will be fixed at two months’ rent, not exceeding ₹20,000 per room. Rent hikes beyond 10% per annum will require board approval, a move aimed at curbing exploitative practices.

4. Structural safeguards

Any PG exceeding three floors or 17.5 metres in height must install fire‑suppression systems and obtain a fire‑safety certificate. Non‑compliance will attract penalties of up to ₹5 lakh per violation.

Implications for operators and tenants

For established PG owners, the licensing regime introduces a compliance cost that could range from ₹30,000 to ₹1 lakh per property, depending on the scale of retrofitting required. Small‑scale hosts, many of whom operate out of family homes, may find the financial outlay prohibitive, prompting a shift toward formal rentals or exit from the market.

Tenants stand to gain from greater transparency. The public registry will list verified safety ratings, allowing students and working professionals to compare options based on fire‑safety compliance, hygiene scores, and landlord credentials.

Cost and compliance outlook

Early‑stage market analysis suggests that the average monthly rent for a certified PG in Delhi will rise by 5‑8%, reflecting the added operational expenses. However, the premium is expected to be offset by reduced turnover and lower legal risk for landlords.

Financial institutions are signalling interest in offering low‑interest loans to PG owners for safety upgrades, a move that could accelerate adoption of the new standards.

Key takeaways for the market

  • Formal licensing will weed out unregistered operators, raising overall safety.
  • Deposit caps protect financially vulnerable students from excessive upfront costs.
  • Compliance costs may consolidate the market, favouring larger, better‑capitalised PG chains.
  • Transparent registries empower tenants to make informed housing choices.
