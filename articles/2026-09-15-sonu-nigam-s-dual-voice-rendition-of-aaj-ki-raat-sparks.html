Performance Benchmarks: How the RTX 5050 Compares

Synthetic and gaming benchmarks paint a clearer picture of where the RTX 5050 sits in the current stack. Based on leaked 3DMark results and early reviewer testing, the card lands roughly 15–20% ahead of the RTX 4060 in rasterization, with a more significant lead in ray-traced workloads thanks to improved RT core throughput and DLSS 4 support.

In 1080p gaming, the RTX 5050 comfortably exceeds 60 FPS in most modern titles at high settings. In esports titles like Valorant and Counter-Strike 2, it pushes well beyond 200 FPS, making it a strong choice for high-refresh-rate monitors. At 1440p, the card remains playable in most titles with settings tuned to medium-high, though demanding ray-traced titles require DLSS to maintain smooth frame rates.

Power, Thermals, and Efficiency

One of the RTX 5050's strongest selling points is its efficiency. With a rated TGP of around 115–130W depending on the board partner, it draws noticeably less power than the RTX 5060 and sips compared to previous-generation mid-range cards. This makes it an excellent fit for small-form-factor builds, pre-builts with modest PSUs, and users upgrading older systems without swapping out their power supply.

Thermals are similarly well-controlled. Reference designs and most AIB models use dual-fan coolers that keep load temperatures in the 60–70°C range under sustained gaming. Noise levels are generally low, and the card rarely exceeds 30 dBA in typical gaming scenarios. Idle power draw is minimal, and the card supports the full suite of NVIDIA's modern power-management features, including zero-fan idle modes on most partner cards.

For users in regions with high electricity costs, the efficiency story matters. Over a year of typical gaming, the RTX 5050 can save a meaningful amount of energy compared to both older mid-range cards and some competing AMD options, while still delivering competitive frame rates.

At a $249 launch price, the RTX 5050 undercuts both its predecessor and the RTX 5060, positioning it as the value-oriented entry point into the Blackwell generation. The trade-off is the 8GB memory buffer, which remains a point of contention in 2025 and beyond.

Strengths and Weaknesses

What the RTX 5050 Does Well

  • Excellent price-to-performance ratio at the entry level, especially compared to the RTX 4060 it replaces.
  • DLSS 4 and improved ray tracing give it a meaningful feature advantage over competing AMD cards in the same price bracket.
  • High efficiency and low power draw make it ideal for SFF builds and older systems with modest power supplies.
  • Strong 1080p performance across a wide range of modern titles, including esports and AAA games at high settings.
  • Compact, quiet cooler designs from most board partners keep noise and heat in check.

Where It Falls Short

  • 8GB of VRAM is increasingly limiting in texture-heavy games and at higher resolutions, and it caps the card's longevity.
  • 1440p performance is adequate but not ideal, especially in ray-traced titles where the memory buffer becomes a bottleneck.
  • Limited headroom for overclocking compared to higher-tier cards, though this is expected at the entry level.
