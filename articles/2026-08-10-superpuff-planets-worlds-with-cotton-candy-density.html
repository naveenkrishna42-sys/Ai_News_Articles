Superpuff planets: worlds with cotton candy density

📅 2026-08-10 📂 Science Original source ↗
Key points
  • Superpuff planets have densities comparable to cotton candy.
  • These worlds challenge existing models of planetary formation and evolution.
  • Astronomers use telescopes to probe their mysterious atmospheres.
  • Future observations may explain how these planets avoid collapsing.

Imagine a planet so light it could theoretically float on water. Astronomers have identified a class of exoplanets with densities so low they rival cotton candy. These 'superpuff' worlds are gas giants with puffy atmospheres that stretch their radii to enormous sizes while keeping their masses surprisingly small.

Unlike the rocky Earth or the dense metallic cores of Jupiter, superpuffs have average densities that are almost negligible. One well-known example, discovered in the Kepler-51 system, has a density of about 0.03 grams per cubic centimetre, comparable to the fluffy confection sold at carnivals.

How do superpuffs form?

The existence of superpuff planets poses a serious challenge to planetary formation models. Standard theories suggest that gas giants form by accumulating a solid core that then gravitationally attracts a thick envelope of hydrogen and helium. But superpuffs appear to have extremely small cores relative to their massive envelopes, or perhaps no solid core at all.

Some researchers propose that these planets formed further out in their solar systems and migrated inward, picking up gas along the way. Others suggest that they are actually young worlds, still contracting and releasing heat from their formation, which could temporarily inflate their atmospheres.

What keeps them from collapsing?

Gravity should pull these fluffy planets inward, compressing them to higher densities. Yet they remain puffed up. One possibility is that internal heat from radioactive decay or tidal forces—caused by the gravitational tug of a nearby star or sibling planet—provides the energy needed to keep the atmosphere expanded.

Another theory involves a process called 'heat trapping' in the upper atmosphere, where certain molecules like methane or water vapour absorb infrared radiation, preventing the planet from cooling. This would maintain high temperatures in the outer layers and stave off collapse.

Probing with telescopes

Astronomers have used instruments like the Hubble Space Telescope to study superpuffs in detail. By observing how starlight filters through their atmospheres during transits, scientists can identify chemical signatures and measure temperature profiles. A recent study of the Kepler-51 system revealed that these planets have remarkably flat spectra, suggesting the presence of high-altitude haze or clouds that obscure deeper atmospheric layers.

Such findings are preliminary, and researchers stress that more data are needed. The James Webb Space Telescope, with its infrared sensitivity, is expected to provide deeper looks into these strange worlds, helping to distinguish between competing theories.

Why superpuffs matter

Studying superpuffs is not just about cosmic oddities. These planets offer a window into the early stages of planetary evolution. If they are indeed young and contracting, they could represent a short-lived phase that many gas giants pass through before settling into more familiar configurations.

Understanding superpuffs could also refine our models of how planetary atmospheres behave under extreme conditions. Their low densities and high temperatures make them natural laboratories for atmospheric physics that cannot be replicated on Earth.

As instruments improve and more superpuff candidates are discovered, scientists hope to build a census of these objects across different ages and environments. That data will help answer one of the most fundamental questions in planetary science: how do gas giants form and evolve over time?

For now, the mystery of the cotton-candy planets remains open. Future observations, particularly those aimed at measuring the ages of these systems, could finally reveal whether superpuffs are young, hot, and transient, or a stable but rare class of world that persists for billions of years.
