India's Weakest Monsoon Since 2015: The 12% Rain Deficit Explained

India's southwest monsoon has closed the 2026 season with rainfall 12% below the normal for the June-to-September period, according to figures compiled by Reuters and the India Meteorological Department's seasonal assessment. That makes it the weakest monsoon since 2015 and a hard reset for agricultural planning, water management and household budgets heading into the rabi cropping cycle.

The national headline number, however, understates how unevenly the shortfall was distributed. Regional reporting from Down To Earth and Bloomberg shows the season's pain concentrated in the Northeast and parts of southern India, which is exactly where the agrarian and hydrological sensitivity is highest.

What the Rainfall Data Actually Shows

The season's performance is best read as a divergence rather than a uniform dry spell. A 12% shortfall at the national level would ordinarily sit inside the IMD's broad 'normal' band, which runs from roughly 19% below to 19% above the Long Period Average (LPA). But sub-national figures tell a sharper story.

Why the national average masks the real risk

A 60% deficit in Meghalaya is not a rounding error — it is a severe regional drought signal that can deplete hill catchments, disrupt tea and horticulture output, and cut hydel generation potential. Similarly, a 24% shortfall across the south affects reservoir recharge ahead of the dry months, when Bengaluru, Chennai and Hyderabad depend heavily on stored supply.

When the shortfall is concentrated rather than spread thin, national averages become a poor guide for procurement planning, commodity purchasing and municipal water decisions.

Farm Income and Food Price Exposure

A large share of India's kharif acreage remains rainfall-dependent, particularly for pulses, oilseeds and coarse cereals in central and western districts. A deficit season raises the probability of lower yields in exactly those categories, and pulses are the crop complex where India has historically been most import-dependent and most price-volatile.

What weak monsoons do to household budgets

Food carries an outsized weight in Indian household consumption baskets, so any sustained uptick in vegetable, pulse or edible oil prices feeds straight through to retail inflation readings. The risk is compounded by the fact that a dry September often coincides with stressed market arrivals of late-kharif produce.

The Economic Times flagged this food-price channel in its coverage of the deficit, and it is the single most actionable consequence for consumers. Bulk buyers, kirana retailers and institutional kitchens should build a modest price buffer into their Q4 procurement budgets rather than assuming flat input costs.

Key Real-World Highlights for Buyers and Households

  • Treat the 12% national figure as a floor, not a guide — regional numbers diverge sharply from it.
  • Expect the strongest price pressure in pulses, coarse cereals and vegetables grown in deficit districts.
  • Municipal water supply in southern cities warrants monitoring; reservoir carry-over will be thinner.
  • Track the IMD's rabi outlook and October rainfall before locking in long-dated agricultural contracts.
  • Crop insurance and commodity hedges become materially more relevant in a post-deficit season.
  • A weak monsoon historically eases energy demand less than it tightens food supply — net inflation risk is upward.

The Bottom Line

India has capped its weakest monsoon in eleven years, and the distribution of the shortfall — not just the headline 12% — determines who absorbs the cost. Buyers, retailers and households planning through the next two quarters should price in uneven regional shortages rather than a smooth national shortfall.
