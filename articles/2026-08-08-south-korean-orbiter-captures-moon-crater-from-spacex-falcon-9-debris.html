South Korean orbiter captures Moon crater from SpaceX Falcon 9 debris

📅 2026-08-08 📂 Science Original source ↗
Key points
  • South Korean space agency releases images of a new crater on the Moon.
  • The crater was formed when a SpaceX Falcon 9 rocket stage crashed into the lunar surface.
  • Scientists say the event offers new insights into how the Moon's surface responds to impacts.

South Korea's lunar orbiter has captured images of a fresh crater on the Moon, formed when debris from a SpaceX Falcon 9 rocket slammed into the surface. The images, released by the South Korean space agency, provide the first visual confirmation of the impact site and are already helping scientists understand how the Moon's fragile crust behaves under such collisions.

The Falcon 9 upper stage, which had been drifting in space for years after helping deliver a satellite into orbit, crashed into the lunar far side in a region that had not been previously surveyed in detail. The crash was not intentional; the rocket stage was left in a chaotic orbit after its mission, and gravitational forces eventually pulled it onto a collision course with the Moon.

How the impact was discovered

Astronomers first predicted the collision months in advance, after tracking the defunct rocket stage's trajectory. Initial observations suggested the debris would strike the Moon, but confirmation required direct imaging. The South Korean orbiter, which has been mapping the lunar surface since its arrival, was able to photograph the newly formed crater during a routine pass.

The images show a distinct depression with a bright ejecta blanket — material thrown out from the impact — spreading across the surrounding terrain. Scientists say the crater's size and shape are consistent with a high-speed impact from a lightweight, hollow object, rather than a dense meteoroid.

What the crater reveals

Unlike natural impacts from asteroids or comets, this crater was caused by human-made debris travelling at extreme velocity. That makes it a unique natural experiment. The Falcon 9 stage, made largely of aluminium and carbon composites, struck the surface at several kilometres per second, vaporising on contact and leaving behind a crater that would have formed slightly differently than one from a rocky projectile.

Researchers are now comparing the crater's morphology with existing models of impact physics. Early analysis suggests that the low-density nature of the rocket debris produced a shallower crater with a more irregular rim than a typical meteorite strike of similar energy. This could help refine models used to study both lunar history and the risks posed by the growing amount of space junk orbiting Earth.

Space debris reaching the Moon

While most space debris burns up in Earth's atmosphere or falls into the oceans, objects in high orbits can occasionally escape Earth's gravitational pull entirely. The Falcon 9 upper stage is among the first confirmed pieces of human-made debris to strike the Moon, and its impact highlights a growing concern: as more rockets are launched, more debris will end up in unstable orbits around Earth and the Moon.

The South Korean space agency said the images will be shared with international partners, including NASA, which had earlier announced plans to attempt observations of the impact. The data could also inform future missions that aim to study the Moon's subsurface, since impacts expose material that has been buried for millions of years.

What happens next

Scientists will continue to monitor the crater over the coming weeks, using the orbiter's instruments to measure its thermal signature and any subtle changes in the surrounding regolith. The findings are expected to be published in a peer-reviewed journal later this year.

As commercial space activity accelerates, the Moon is becoming a destination not just for explorers but also for discarded hardware. The next few years will likely see more such impacts, and each one offers a chance to learn something new about Earth's nearest neighbour.
