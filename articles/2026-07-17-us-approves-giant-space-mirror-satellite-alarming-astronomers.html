US approves giant 'space mirror' satellite, alarming astronomers

📅 2026-07-17 📂 Science Original source ↗
Key points
  • The US Federal Communications Commission (FCC) has approved the launch of a satellite equipped with a giant mirror designed to reflect sunlight onto Earth after dark.
  • The satellite, developed by Reflect Orbital, aims to provide illumination for commercial and industrial purposes.
  • Astronomers have raised alarms, arguing the artificial light will worsen light pollution and interfere with astronomical observations.

FCC gives green light to controversial satellite

The United States Federal Communications Commission (FCC) has approved the deployment of a satellite equipped with a giant mirror capable of reflecting sunlight onto Earth at night. The decision, announced this week, has drawn sharp criticism from the global astronomical community.

The satellite is developed by Reflect Orbital, a company that plans to use the reflective technology to illuminate targeted areas on the ground after sunset. The concept, which has been in development for years, is intended to provide artificial light for commercial, industrial, or emergency applications.

How the 'space mirror' would work

The satellite carries a large, deployable mirror that can tilt to direct sunlight toward a specific location on Earth. Once operational, it could brighten a patch of the planet’s surface for several minutes at a time, effectively turning night into day in that zone.

Reflect Orbital has pitched the technology as a way to boost productivity, aid disaster response, or reduce reliance on ground-based lighting. The company has not disclosed a precise launch timeline or the satellite’s exact orbital parameters.

Astronomers raise red flags

Astronomers and light-pollution researchers have reacted with alarm. They argue that any additional artificial light in the night sky will interfere with ground-based telescopes and exacerbate the already severe problem of light pollution.

“This is a dangerous precedent,” said one researcher familiar with the project. “Even a single bright source in orbit can ruin long-exposure images and disrupt observations of faint celestial objects.”

The International Dark-Sky Association has previously warned that space-based reflectors could brighten the night sky globally, not just at targeted sites. The approval has reignited debates about the regulation of commercial satellites that affect the shared resource of the night sky.

Regulatory context and next steps

The FCC’s approval is limited to the first satellite in the planned constellation. The agency has not yet ruled on applications for additional units. Critics have called for a broader environmental review before any further launches are authorised.

Reflect Orbital has said it will work with astronomers to mitigate impacts, but no concrete measures have been outlined. The company has also faced questions about the utility and safety of reflecting sunlight, including potential effects on wildlife and human health.

The approval comes amid a broader push by several countries and private firms to test reflective space technologies. No other nation has yet authorised a similar deployment.

Astronomical bodies are expected to petition the FCC for a reconsideration or seek intervention from other federal agencies. The coming months will show whether this single satellite becomes a test case for a new kind of orbital light pollution.
