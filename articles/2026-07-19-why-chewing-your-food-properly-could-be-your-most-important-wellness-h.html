Why chewing your food properly could be your most important wellness habit

📅 2026-07-19 📂 Lifestyle Original source ↗
Key points
  • Chewing food 30-40 times per bite can significantly improve digestion and nutrient absorption.
  • Proper chewing reduces overeating by giving the brain time to register fullness.
  • Mindful chewing may lower stress and improve mental well-being.
  • Many people eat too quickly, missing out on these health benefits.
  • Simple changes in eating habits can make chewing a daily wellness practice.

The forgotten art of chewing

In the rush of daily life, most Indians barely pause between bites. Lunch is eaten at a desk, dinner in front of a screen. But a growing number of wellness experts argue that the simple act of chewing — properly, slowly, deliberately — could be the single most impactful health practice you adopt.

Chewing is often dismissed as a mechanical prelude to digestion. But it is far more than that. It is the first and most controllable step in how your body processes food, and getting it right can influence everything from gut health to mental clarity.

What happens when you chew

When you chew, you break food into smaller particles, increasing its surface area for digestive enzymes. Saliva contains amylase, which begins breaking down starches. The longer you chew, the more thoroughly this process works.

Studies suggest that chewing each mouthful 30 to 40 times can significantly improve nutrient absorption. It also signals to your stomach and intestines to prepare for the incoming food, reducing bloating and indigestion.

But the benefits are not just physical. Chewing slowly gives your brain time to register fullness. It takes about 20 minutes for the brain to receive the signal that you have eaten enough. Speeding through a meal often means eating more than you need.

The mental health connection

Mindful chewing — paying full attention to the texture, taste and act of eating — has been linked to lower stress levels. Taking time to chew forces you to slow down, creating a pause in a busy day.

Some practitioners compare it to a form of meditation. The repetitive motion and focus on the present moment can reduce cortisol levels and improve mood. It is a small, accessible habit that does not require a gym membership or a special app.

Why most of us get it wrong

Modern eating habits work against proper chewing. Soft, processed foods require less effort. Busy schedules encourage speed. Many people swallow food after just a few chews, leaving much of the digestive work to the stomach and intestines.

This can lead to issues like acid reflux, gas, and poor nutrient uptake. Over time, it may contribute to weight gain and metabolic problems. The fix is simple but requires conscious effort.

Experts recommend starting with one meal a day. Put down your phone. Take smaller bites. Count your chews if needed. Aim for 20 to 30 chews per bite for softer foods, and 30 to 40 for denser items like vegetables or meat.

Practical tips to chew better

  • Set a timer for 20 minutes per meal.
  • Put your fork down between bites.
  • Drink water between bites to slow pace.
  • Choose whole, fibrous foods that require more chewing.

What comes next

More wellness experts are now incorporating chewing into broader health advice. Some nutritionists recommend chewing exercises for people with jaw tension or TMJ issues. The practice is also being studied for its role in weight management and metabolic health.

As the science becomes clearer, chewing may shed its image as a mere habit and take its place as a deliberate wellness tool. For now, the simplest advice holds: slow down, chew well, and let your body do the rest.
