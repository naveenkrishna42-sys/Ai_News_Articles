Trump says US controls Iran's frozen assets, threatens action

📅 2026-08-11 📂 World Original source ↗
Key points
  • Trump says US is Iran's 'banker', controlling frozen assets
  • He threatens economic collapse or military strike if Iran doesn't comply
  • Iran's demand for war reparations dismissed by US
  • New talks scheduled between Israel and Lebanon in Rome for September
  • Hormuz talks reportedly saw new conditions set by both sides

Trump's blunt warning to Tehran

President Donald Trump has ramped up pressure on Iran, declaring that the United States effectively acts as Tehran's banker by controlling its frozen assets. In a characteristically blunt statement, he said, "I'm their banker," while threatening either economic strangulation or direct military action if Iran fails to meet American demands.

The remarks come amid escalating tensions in the Middle East, with negotiations over the strategic Strait of Hormuz reportedly hitting a fresh impasse. Trump's comments signal a hardening of the US stance, leaving little room for diplomatic manoeuvre in the near term.

Hormuz talks and new conditions

According to reports, both Iran and the US have set new conditions during recent Hormuz talks, though details remain sketchy. The Strait of Hormuz is a critical chokepoint for global oil shipments, and any disruption there would send shockwaves through energy markets worldwide.

Diplomatic sources suggest that the talks, which were meant to de-escalate tensions, have instead exposed deeper divisions. Neither side has publicly confirmed the specifics of these new conditions, but the tone from Washington suggests a preference for pressure over patience.

War reparations demand dismissed

Trump has also scoffed at Iran's demand for war reparations, a move that analysts say is unlikely to gain traction in any serious negotiation. The demand, reportedly raised during recent exchanges, was met with derision from the US side, with Trump framing it as unrealistic and disconnected from the realities of power.

The Iranian government has not yet responded officially to Trump's latest remarks, but state media have historically framed such rhetoric as proof of American hostility. The demand for reparations, tied to past conflicts and sanctions, remains a sticking point that could further complicate any future talks.

Economic collapse or military force

Trump laid out what he sees as the two available options for Iran: let Tehran fail economically or "hit them really hard". This binary framing leaves little room for the kind of incremental diplomacy that has characterised past US-Iran engagements, including the 2015 nuclear deal from which Trump withdrew in his first term.

The threat of military action has been a recurring theme in Trump's approach to Iran, but the economic angle is equally significant. By controlling frozen assets, Washington can squeeze Tehran's access to foreign currency, potentially crippling its ability to import essential goods or fund its regional proxies.

Broader regional dynamics

The US has also announced a new round of Israeli-Lebanese talks in Rome scheduled for September, indicating that Washington is juggling multiple fronts in the region. These talks, separate from the Iran issue, aim to address long-standing disputes that have periodically flared into conflict.

Meanwhile, other Mideast developments continue to unfold, with the US maintaining a hawkish posture across the board. The intertwining of the Iran file with Israeli-Lebanese negotiations suggests that the region remains a complex web of alliances and enmities, where a shift in one arena can quickly ripple into another.

Officials have not yet confirmed whether the Rome talks will include any discussion of Iran's regional behaviour, but the timing is notable given the escalation in rhetoric.

What to watch next

All eyes will be on Tehran's response to Trump's latest salvo, as well as the September talks in Rome. Whether the frozen assets become a bargaining chip or a casus belli remains to be seen, but the window for de-escalation appears to be narrowing with each passing week.
