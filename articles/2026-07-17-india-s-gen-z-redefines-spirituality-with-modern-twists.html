India's Gen Z redefines spirituality with modern twists

📅 2026-07-17 📂 Religion Original source ↗
Key points
  • Gen Z in India mixes traditional faith with digital tools like meditation apps and spiritual podcasts.
  • Eco-conscious practices, such as using biodegradable idols, are gaining traction among young devotees.
  • Organised religion sees decline as personalised, experiential spirituality rises.

MUMBAI — For generations, Indian spirituality meant temple visits, ritualistic prayers, and family-led ceremonies. But Gen Z is writing a new chapter. Young Indians are blending ancient traditions with modern sensibilities, creating a spiritual identity that is both rooted and restless.

From urban hipsters in Bengaluru to college students in Delhi, the shift is visible. They chant mantras but also track their meditation streaks on apps. They visit ashrams but also subscribe to spiritual podcasts. The old box of organised religion no longer holds them.

Apps, podcasts, and personalised faith

Smartphones have become the new prayer books. Apps like Insight Timer, Headspace, and homegrown platforms such as The Art of Living's app see millions of downloads from users under 25. These tools offer guided meditation, breathing exercises, and daily affirmations — packaged for short attention spans.

Spiritual podcasts are another big draw. Shows like 'The Spiritual Hustle' and 'Bhakti Bytes' discuss everything from karma to mindfulness, often featuring young hosts who speak in a mix of Hindi and English. Listeners don't just consume — they comment, share, and debate.

This is not about abandoning faith. It is about curating it. Gen Z picks what resonates — yoga without the dogma, meditation without the ritual. Organised religion, with its hierarchies and rigid schedules, often feels like a bad fit.

Eco-conscious rituals

Environmentalism is reshaping how young Indians practise their faith. The Ganesh Chaturthi festival, once marked by large plaster-of-Paris idols that polluted water bodies, now sees a surge in clay and paper mache alternatives. Young organisers insist on natural colours and flower decorations that can be composted.

Similarly, during Diwali, many Gen Z households skip firecrackers and opt for diyas made from recycled materials. Temple offerings are increasingly organic. This shift is driven by awareness campaigns on social media and peer pressure — but also by a genuine desire to align spirituality with sustainability.

“I can't worship a god while poisoning the river,” says Priya Sharma, a 22-year-old student from Pune, who switched to a biodegradable idol last year. Her sentiment echoes a growing consensus.

Decline of organised religion

While personal spirituality is on the rise, organised religion is losing its grip. Data from surveys by Pew Research and local think tanks show that younger Indians are less likely to attend weekly temple services or follow dietary restrictions mandated by faith. The number of young people identifying as 'spiritual but not religious' has climbed steadily.

This does not mean atheism is sweeping the country. Most Gen Z Indians still believe in a higher power. But they reject intermediaries — priests, scriptures, institutions. The relationship with the divine is direct, digital, and often DIY.

Critics argue this approach is shallow, cherry-picking rituals without understanding their meaning. But supporters say it is honest. “Why should I follow rules made centuries ago for a different world?” asks Arjun Mehta, a 24-year-old software engineer from Hyderabad. “My faith is my own.”

The trend is most visible in cities, but it is spreading to smaller towns via social media. Instagram pages dedicated to Hindu mythology, Islamic mysticism, or Buddhist philosophy have millions of followers. The content is visual, relatable, and stripped of jargon.

What happens next? The line between tradition and innovation will keep blurring. Temples and religious organisations are already experimenting with QR codes for donations and live-streamed ceremonies. If they adapt fast enough, they might win back some of the young. If not, Gen Z will continue to build its own altars — physical, digital, and entirely personal.
