Iran rules out ceasefire extension, insists US return to interim deal

📅 2026-08-12 📂 World Original source ↗
Key points
  • Senior Iranian source says no talks on extending the 60-day ceasefire.
  • Tehran insists the US must return to the interim deal framework.
  • Mediators pushing for talks as the MoU deadline approaches.
  • US and Iran had earlier agreed to extend the ceasefire until Aug. 17.

Tehran has drawn a firm red line ahead of the Aug. 17 deadline: there will be no discussion of extending the current 60-day ceasefire. Instead, a senior Iranian source said on Wednesday, the United States must return to the interim deal. The message is unambiguous, and it complicates the already fraught diplomatic manoeuvring under way.

The source, speaking on condition of anonymity, told Reuters that Tehran sees the ceasefire as a temporary measure, not a foundation for prolonged negotiations. Any talk of another extension, the source indicated, is a non-starter. The focus, instead, is on reviving the interim agreement that both sides had signed but which has since frayed.

The Aug. 17 deadline looms

The current ceasefire, agreed between Washington and Tehran, runs until Aug. 17. Earlier reports confirmed that both sides had already extended it once, from an initial 60-day period. But with the new deadline days away, the atmosphere has shifted. The Iranian position now appears hardened, with no appetite for rolling over the truce again.

Mediators, however, are not giving up. Regional and international players are actively pushing for direct US-Iran talks, hoping to bridge the gap before the deadline expires. The urgency is palpable, with diplomatic channels working overtime to prevent a slide back into hostilities.

Mediators push, but Tehran holds firm

Efforts to bring the two sides to the table have intensified, according to multiple reports. Al Jazeera noted that mediators are pressing for US-Iran engagement as the memorandum of understanding deadline approaches. Yet, Tehran has signalled that progress on reviving the interim peace deal has been nil.

The senior Iranian source's remarks align with that assessment. There is no progress, the source said, on getting the US to recommit to the interim framework. This has left the diplomatic process in a precarious position, with the ceasefire's future hanging by a thread.

Regional dynamics add pressure

Beyond the bilateral standoff, regional actors are weighing in. Pakistan, for instance, has pushed for US-Iran talks even as Israel conducted twin strikes on Lebanon. These crosscurrents underscore the volatility of the wider Middle East, where any US-Iran miscalculation could have ripple effects far beyond their immediate confrontation.

The Iranian stance also carries domestic weight. Hardliners in Tehran have long viewed the interim deal with suspicion, and any concession on an extension could be politically costly. By insisting on a return to the interim deal, the leadership is drawing a clear line: the ceasefire was never meant to be an end in itself.

What happens next

With the deadline now just days away, the onus is on Washington to respond. Will it accept Tehran's framing and return to the interim deal, or will it push for another extension? Mediators are likely to intensify their efforts, but the ball is squarely in the US court.

For now, the clock is ticking. If no breakthrough emerges by Aug. 17, the ceasefire's fate remains uncertain. Officials have not confirmed what would follow, but the stakes are unmistakable. The coming days will test whether diplomacy can outpace the drums of war.
