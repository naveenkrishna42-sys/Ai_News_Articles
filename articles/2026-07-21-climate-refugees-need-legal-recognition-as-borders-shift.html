Climate refugees need legal recognition as borders shift

📅 2026-07-21 📂 Environment Original source ↗
Key points
  • Current refugee law does not cover those displaced by climate change or environmental disasters.
  • Millions are already moving due to rising seas, droughts, and extreme weather.
  • Legal experts say a new framework is needed to protect climate migrants.

Crossing borders, but not recognised

Every year, millions of people are forced to leave their homes because of environmental disasters and a changing climate. They cross international borders in search of safety. But under current international law, they are not considered refugees.

The 1951 Refugee Convention defines a refugee as someone fleeing persecution based on race, religion, nationality, political opinion, or membership in a particular social group. It does not include those displaced by floods, droughts, or sea-level rise. This legal gap leaves climate migrants in a precarious position.

The scale of the crisis

According to the Internal Displacement Monitoring Centre, weather-related disasters triggered more than 20 million internal displacements each year over the past decade. Many of these people eventually cross borders. The World Bank has estimated that by 2050, over 140 million people could be internally displaced within sub-Saharan Africa, South Asia, and Latin America due to climate change. Cross-border movements are expected to rise correspondingly.

Yet these individuals have no formal status. They cannot claim asylum based on environmental grounds. Host countries often treat them as irregular migrants, leaving them vulnerable to detention, deportation, and exploitation.

Why the law needs to change

Legal scholars and human rights advocates argue that the definition of a refugee must be updated. They point out that climate change is not a natural disaster in the traditional sense — it is driven by human activity, particularly greenhouse gas emissions from industrialised nations. This creates a chain of causation that resembles persecution: the actions of some countries force others to flee.

Some nations have taken limited steps. In 2020, the UN Human Rights Committee ruled that countries cannot deport people to places where climate change poses an immediate threat to life. But this does not grant refugee status. A broader international agreement remains elusive.

What happens at borders

Without legal recognition, climate migrants face a patchwork of responses. Some countries, like Bangladesh, have created bilateral agreements with neighbours to accept displaced populations. Others, such as the United States, have offered temporary protected status to people from specific disaster-hit countries. These measures are ad hoc and often temporary.

Experts say a dedicated legal framework is needed. This could be a new protocol to the Refugee Convention or a separate treaty. It would define who qualifies as a climate refugee, outline states' obligations, and provide a pathway to residency or citizenship.

Critics argue that such a framework could overwhelm asylum systems. But advocates counter that the current approach is already failing millions. They say the cost of inaction is far greater than the cost of reform.

What to watch next

International negotiations on climate migration are expected to continue at the upcoming COP meetings. A proposed global compact on migration already mentions climate change, but it is non-binding. The question now is whether nations will move from aspirational language to concrete legal protections for those forced to cross borders by a warming planet.
