India's BRICS Evolution: From Skeptic to Pillar

When BRICS held its first summit in 2009, India was often viewed as the bloc's 'weak link'—an economy grappling with high inflation, a wide current account deficit, and sluggish reforms. Fourteen years later, that narrative has flipped. India is now the fastest-growing major economy within BRICS, contributing nearly 15% of the group's combined GDP (PPP terms), up from around 9% in 2009. This shift is not just symbolic; it reflects deeper structural changes that matter for global investors and businesses.

Original reporting by News18 highlights how India's stance has evolved from cautious engagement to active leadership, particularly in areas like digital public infrastructure and climate finance. The transformation is rooted in a series of domestic reforms—GST, insolvency code, and liberalised FDI—that boosted India's attractiveness as a manufacturing and services hub. For buyers of Indian exports, this means more reliable supply chains and competitive pricing.

Trade Dynamics Within BRICS: China's Shadow and India's Rise

China remains the largest trading partner for every BRICS member, including India. In FY2023, India's trade deficit with China widened to $83 billion, a stark reminder of the asymmetry. Yet India has also deepened ties with other members: bilateral trade with Russia hit a record $45 billion in FY2023, driven by discounted crude oil imports. With Brazil and South Africa, India's trade is smaller but growing in agri-commodities and pharmaceuticals.

For Indian importers, the BRICS platform offers reduced tariff barriers and currency swap arrangements, but the dominance of China in critical sectors like electronics and APIs remains a strategic concern. Expansion and Global Ambitions: What's Next for BRICS

In 2024, BRICS expanded to include Egypt, Ethiopia, Iran, and the UAE, with Saudi Arabia invited. This expansion amplifies the bloc's share of global GDP to 37% (PPP) and its control over key commodities—oil, gas, and rare earths. For India, the new members present both opportunities and challenges. Iran and UAE offer energy security and trade routes, while Ethiopia and Egypt open African markets for Indian pharma and IT services.

However, expansion also dilutes consensus. India has consistently advocated for a multipolar world, not an anti-West alliance. As The Indian Express notes, BRICS is not a monolith; India's strategic autonomy remains paramount. For global buyers, this means BRICS cooperation will be pragmatic, not ideological—focusing on trade facilitation, local currency settlement, and infrastructure finance through the New Development Bank (NDB).

Pros and Cons for Indian Businesses and Investors

  • Pros:
    • Access to cheaper Russian crude and commodities, reducing input costs.
    • Expanded export markets in Africa and Latin America through BRICS trade pacts.
    • NDB funding for infrastructure projects at competitive rates.
    • Platform to push for rupee internationalisation and reduce dollar dependency.
  • Cons:
    • Growing trade deficit with China, risking domestic industry displacement.
    • Geopolitical tensions with the West could complicate technology transfers.
    • Slow decision-making due to divergent interests among members.
    • Currency volatility in some member nations affects trade settlement.

Value Justification: Why This Matters for Buyers

Understanding India's BRICS trajectory is not just academic. For procurement managers, it signals where to source competitively—Russian energy, Brazilian soy, South African coal—and where to sell—African infrastructure, Asian digital services. For investors, BRICS equities and bonds offer diversification, but require careful assessment of political risk. India's improved standing within the bloc enhances its bargaining power, potentially leading to better terms in future trade agreements.

As BRICS moves toward a multipolar order, India's role as a growth engine will be tested by its ability to balance domestic priorities with collective ambitions. The next decade will determine whether India can convert its demographic dividend and digital edge into sustained influence, or whether China's economic gravity will continue to dominate. For now, the data shows India is no longer the weak link—it is a driving force.
