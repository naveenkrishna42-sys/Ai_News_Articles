Global child vaccination rates inch up in 2025, but millions still miss out

📅 2026-07-18 📂 Health Original source ↗
Key points
  • Global childhood immunisation coverage saw a modest increase in 2025, according to UNICEF and WHO data.
  • 13.5 million children remain 'zero-dose', having received no routine vaccinations.
  • Conflict zones and growing vaccine hesitancy continue to undermine progress.

Modest uptick in global vaccination coverage

Global childhood immunisation coverage has edged forward in 2025, according to new data from UNICEF and the World Health Organization. The agencies report a slight increase in the number of children receiving routine vaccines, a rare piece of good news after years of stagnation and backsliding.

But health officials warn that the gains remain fragile. Conflict, displacement and rising vaccine hesitancy continue to leave millions of children unprotected. The WHO and UNICEF joint assessment shows that while more children are being reached, the pace is far too slow to meet global targets.

The crisis of 'zero-dose' children

Despite the modest progress, an estimated 13.5 million children worldwide are classified as 'zero-dose' — meaning they have not received a single routine vaccine shot. The term has become a rallying cry for global health agencies trying to close the immunisation gap.

Most of these children live in low-income countries or fragile states affected by armed conflict. India, Nigeria and Ethiopia account for a significant share of the global zero-dose burden. In many of these regions, health systems have been shattered by war, leaving basic immunisation services unavailable.

Conflict and hesitancy remain major hurdles

The report identifies two primary obstacles: conflict and vaccine hesitancy. In war zones like parts of the Middle East and the Sahel, health workers face direct threats, and supply chains are frequently disrupted. Vaccination campaigns have been suspended or scaled back in several areas.

Vaccine hesitancy, fuelled by misinformation and distrust, has also crept into new regions. Even in countries with historically high coverage, pockets of resistance have emerged, causing localised outbreaks of preventable diseases like measles and polio. The WHO has called for targeted communication strategies to rebuild public confidence.

What the data shows

The UNICEF-WHO report highlights that DTP3 coverage — a key benchmark for immunisation — rose by less than one percentage point globally in 2025. While this marks a reversal of the decline seen during the pandemic, it remains below pre-2019 levels.

Measles vaccination coverage also saw a slight improvement, but the number of large outbreaks has increased. More than 20 countries reported significant measles outbreaks in 2025, many linked to gaps in routine immunisation.

What happens next

UNICEF and WHO have called on governments to strengthen primary healthcare systems and prioritise reaching zero-dose children. The next round of global vaccination targets, set for 2030, will require a much faster pace of improvement. Without urgent action, the agencies warn, the gains of 2025 could easily be reversed.
