Delhi SIR in the Supreme Court: What the Plea Actually Seeks

The Supreme Court has agreed to give precedence to a batch of petitions challenging the Delhi Special Intensive Revision (SIR) of electoral rolls, including a plea seeking disclosure of the voters who were issued notices and the grounds on which their names were flagged for deletion. The draft SIR roll, published after the exercise, dropped roughly 47.6 lakh voters from Delhi's rolls.

The core legal question is procedural, not political: on what grounds were individual voters marked for exclusion, and were they given a meaningful opportunity to respond before deletion? The Court's willingness to prioritise the matter signals it intends to examine the audit trail behind the deletions rather than defer to the Election Commission's internal process.

Why 47.6 Lakh Deletions Matter for Delhi's Electorate

Delhi's total electorate before the SIR stood at close to 1.55 crore. A removal of 47.6 lakh names represents roughly 30% of that base — a scale with no recent precedent in a single-state revision. For context, the 2025 Assembly election in Delhi was decided by margins far smaller than the number of names now in dispute in several constituencies.

Newslaundry's constituency-level analysis found that in the seat former Chief Minister Arvind Kejriwal lost by a thin margin, nearly 40% of names added before the 2025 polls were missing from the draft SIR roll. That finding is significant because it undercuts the assumption that deletions fall evenly across parties and neighbourhoods.

The reappplication figure is the sharpest operational red flag. If lakhs of voters were genuinely shifted or deceased, the reapplication rate would be low by design. But when deletions run into tens of lakhs, the burden shifts to the Commission to show that notice was actually served, not merely dispatched.

Key Real-World Highlights

  • Scale: 47.6 lakh deletions is unprecedented for a single-state SIR and larger than the victory margins in several 2025 Delhi constituencies.
  • Low reapplication: Very few affected voters have reapplied, suggesting notice delivery or awareness failed at the last mile.
  • Judicial scrutiny: The Supreme Court's precedence order forces the Commission to justify individual deletions, not just the process.
  • Precedent value: Any ruling on disclosure will shape how SIR is conducted in other states.

Buyer's Guide Logic: What Voters Should Do Now

Treat this like a documentation exercise. Check your name on the draft roll immediately, keep your existing voter ID and Aadhaar-linked address proof ready, and file Form 6 without waiting for the Court's final order. If your name was deleted, request the specific ground in writing from your Electoral Registration Officer.

For political parties and civil society groups, the practical benchmark is simple: track reapplication rates ward by ward. A low rate in a dense, literate constituency is a delivery failure; a low rate in a migrant-heavy zone may be genuine attrition. The Court's disclosure plea is aimed precisely at separating the two.

The hearing will determine whether electoral roll purity can be pursued at this scale without an auditable, voter-level paper trail. Until that is settled, the 47.6 lakh figure remains an allegation with a number attached — and a number that the Commission must now defend in open court.
