Vikram-1 reaches orbit, but Skyroot faces business challenges ahead

📅 2026-07-19 📂 Top Stories Original source ↗
Key points
  • Skyroot's Vikram-1 successfully placed satellites in low Earth orbit on July 19, 2026
  • PM Modi praised the launch, recalling his decision to open the space sector
  • The startup now needs to secure commercial contracts and scale operations
  • Global competition from SpaceX, Rocket Lab, and others remains intense

The launch that made history

Skyroot Aerospace's Vikram-1 rocket reached orbit on Sunday, July 19, 2026, becoming the first privately built launch vehicle from India to do so. The rocket lifted off from the Satish Dhawan Space Centre in Sriharikota, carrying a payload of small satellites for commercial and research purposes.

The mission was a milestone for India's space sector, which the government opened to private players in 2020. For Skyroot, it was the culmination of years of work that began with a simple question: "Why can't we build a private rocket firm?"

PM Modi's message to Skyroot

Prime Minister Narendra Modi personally congratulated the Skyroot team after the launch. "I was discouraged from taking the bold decision to privatise the space sector. Today, you proved me right," he told the founders in a call that was later shared on social media.

The PM's remarks highlighted the political significance of the launch. For years, India's space programme was a monopoly of the Indian Space Research Organisation (ISRO). The government's 2020 reforms allowed private firms to build and launch rockets, a move that was initially met with scepticism within the bureaucracy.

The race for low Earth orbit

Vikram-1 is designed to carry small satellites to low Earth orbit, a market that has exploded in recent years. Companies like SpaceX and Rocket Lab have dominated this space globally, but Indian startups have been trying to carve out a niche.

Skyroot's rocket can lift up to 500 kg to orbit, placing it in the small-launch category. The company has already signed agreements with several Indian and international clients, but turning these into firm, paid contracts will be key to its survival.

Cost and competition

One Canadian space enthusiast called Vikram-1 "the biggest white pill of all time" on social media, referring to its promise of affordable access to space. But the reality is that Skyroot must compete with launch providers that have years of flight heritage and lower per-kilogram costs.

SpaceX's Falcon 9, for example, charges roughly $2,500 per kg to orbit. Skyroot has not disclosed its pricing, but analysts estimate it could be higher initially due to lower production volumes. The company will need to launch frequently to bring costs down.

What happens next

Skyroot has already announced plans for Vikram-2, a larger rocket capable of carrying heavier payloads. But the immediate priority is to secure a steady cadence of launches for Vikram-1. The company has a small backlog of orders, but scaling up will require more capital and customer confidence.

Industry watchers say the next 12 months will be crucial. If Skyroot can demonstrate reliability and cost-effectiveness, it could become a key player in the small satellite launch market. If not, it risks being squeezed out by larger, more established players.

The Indian government has signalled it will continue to support private space firms. But ultimately, the market will decide whether Skyroot's historic launch becomes the start of a success story or just a footnote.
