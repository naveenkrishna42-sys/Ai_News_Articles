ECI Tells SC: No Prosecution If You Reapply Via Form 6 After SIR Draft Roll

✍️ By TIVRA News Editorial Desk 📅 2026-11-15 📂 Top Stories Original source ↗
Key points
  • The ECI told the Supreme Court that voters omitted from SIR draft rolls will not face prosecution if they reapply through Form 6
  • The claim-and-objection window is the practical deadline to fix omissions before final rolls are published
  • Reports of Booth Level Officers reluctant to reach ASDD voters shift the burden of proof back onto applicants

What the ECI told the Supreme Court

The Election Commission of India has assured the Supreme Court that it is “alive” to voters’ difficulty in locating their names on the electoral roll, and that no prosecution will be launched against persons left out of the draft rolls prepared during the Summary Revision (SIR) if they reapply through Form 6. The assurance responds to the central fear driving voter petitions: that deletion from a draft roll would automatically expose an elector to penal action.

The Commission also stated before the court that it is committed to ensuring all inconsistencies in voter lists are addressed. Read together, the two positions signal that the ECI treats omissions as correctable administrative errors rather than automatic grounds for action against the voter.

Why the draft roll stage decides your vote

A draft roll published during SIR is the only practical window in which an elector can verify inclusion, correct a wrong address or spelling, or object to a bogus entry. Once the final roll is published, corrections become slower and costlier, often requiring election-year intervention. The ECI’s no-prosecution assurance therefore matters most to voters who were dropped during house-to-house verification or whose entries failed digitised matching.

The re-application route

Form 6 is the standard application for inclusion of a name in the electoral roll, filed with the Electoral Registration Officer of the assembly constituency, or online through the National Voters’ Services Portal. Applicants typically attach age and address proof, and the Booth Level Officer (BLO) may verify the claim before enrolment. The assurance removes the fear of filing the form; it does not remove the need to file it within the claim-and-objection period.

Reports of uneven ground-level follow-up

Alongside the court proceedings, the Deccan Chronicle reported that BLOs have been reluctant to reach out to ASDD voters during the SIR exercise. If field verification is patchy, the practical burden shifts to the voter: the name that disappears from a draft roll must be actively restored, not passively awaited. That is the gap the Supreme Court’s attention is meant to close.

Key real-world takeaways

  • Pro: The no-prosecution assurance lowers the risk of reapplying, encouraging voters with genuine omissions to come forward instead of staying silent.
  • Pro: The ECI’s stated commitment to fixing inconsistencies gives petitioners a documented administrative position to rely on.
  • Con: The assurance protects reapplicants; it does not itself restore names, so delays in BLO verification can still exclude voters on polling day.
  • Con: Reports of BLO reluctance toward ASDD voters indicate that house-to-house verification quality remains uneven across booths.
  • Action: Check the draft roll on the NVSP portal now, file the correct form before the claim window closes, and keep the acknowledgment with your address proof ready for verification.
