Bollywood classics reimagined in traditional art at new exhibition

📅 2026-07-17 📂 Movies Original source ↗
Key points
  • Exhibition reimagines classic Bollywood films using traditional Indian art forms.
  • Features works by multiple artists, blending cinema with heritage techniques.
  • Showcases iconic scenes from films like Sholay and Mughal-e-Azam in new mediums.

A new exhibition in India is bridging two cultural giants — Bollywood and traditional Indian art. Iconic scenes from classic films have been recreated using art forms such as Mughal miniatures, Tanjore paintings, and Pattachitra. The result is a visual dialogue between the country's cinematic history and its centuries-old artistic traditions.

The exhibition, curated by a team of art historians and film archivists, features works by several contemporary artists. Each piece takes a well-known moment from Bollywood — a dialogue, a song, a glance — and translates it into a style that predates the film industry itself.

From celluloid to canvas

The artists have chosen films that span several decades. Scenes from 'Sholay', 'Mughal-e-Azam', and 'Pyaasa' appear in forms that resemble illuminated manuscripts or temple frescoes. One painting shows Amitabh Bachchan's character from 'Deewar' as a Mughal emperor, standing in a regal pose with a miniature city behind him.

Another work reimagines the 'Mere Mehboob' song from 'Mughal-e-Azam' as a Tanjore-style panel. Gold leaf and semi-precious stones replace the film's vivid colours. The scene retains its emotional weight but gains a new texture, one that feels both ancient and contemporary.

Why this matters

Art critics have noted that the exhibition does more than just decorate film nostalgia. It forces a conversation about how Indian identity has been shaped by both cinema and traditional art. Bollywood, for all its modern influence, draws from the same cultural well as the miniature painters of the 17th century.

The curators have arranged the works in a way that encourages comparison. Visitors can see a Mughal-style painting of 'Sholay's iconic train scene next to a poster of the original film. The contrast highlights how each medium tells a story differently. One uses perspective and shadow; the other relies on flat planes and intricate detail.

Reception and reach

The exhibition has drawn crowds since its opening last week. Social media is filled with photos of visitors pointing out their favourite scenes reimagined in unfamiliar styles. Some have called it a fresh way to engage with films they thought they knew inside out.

Artists involved have reported a surge in interest for traditional techniques. One painter said younger visitors often ask about the materials used, from natural dyes to hand-ground pigments. The exhibition has thus become an accidental classroom for traditional art forms.

The show is scheduled to run for another three months. Organisers have hinted at a possible second edition featuring regional cinema, including films from the Tamil and Bengali industries.
