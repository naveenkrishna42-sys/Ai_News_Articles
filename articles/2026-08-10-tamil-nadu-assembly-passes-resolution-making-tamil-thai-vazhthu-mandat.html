Tamil Nadu Assembly passes resolution making Tamil Thai Vazhthu mandatory first

📅 2026-08-10 📂 Top Stories Original source ↗
Key points
  • Resolution moved by CM Vijay mandates Tamil Thai Vazhthu first at all events
  • DMK and AIADMK extend support, making the passage unanimous
  • CM Vijay says 'mother tongue is life' amid Centre's Vande Mataram push

Unanimous backing for Tamil anthem resolution

The Tamil Nadu Assembly on Monday unanimously passed a resolution moved by Chief Minister Vijay, mandating that the state song 'Tamil Thai Vazhthu' be rendered first at all official events and functions across the state. The resolution sailed through with the support of major opposition parties, including the DMK and AIADMK, marking a rare display of political unity.

Moving the resolution, CM Vijay said, "Our mother tongue is as pure as our mother," and asserted that the Tamil anthem must precede any other song at public gatherings. The Chief Minister's remarks came amid a renewed push by the central government to promote 'Vande Mataram' at official events, a move that has sparked debate in the southern state.

Political consensus across party lines

The DMK and AIADMK, often at loggerheads on most legislative matters, extended their full support to the resolution. Leaders from both parties said the state song's priority was a matter of cultural pride and not a political statement. The unanimous vote underscores the sentiment shared across the political spectrum in Tamil Nadu regarding the primacy of the Tamil language.

Observers note that the resolution is as much about cultural assertion as it is about sending a signal to the Centre. The timing is significant, coming weeks after the central government issued directives emphasising the singing of 'Vande Mataram' at official functions, which many in Tamil Nadu viewed as an infringement on the state's linguistic identity.

CM Vijay's strong words on language pride

In his address to the House, CM Vijay elaborated on the emotional and cultural significance of the Tamil language, calling it the "lifeblood" of the people. "Tamil is not just a language; it is our identity, our heritage, and our lifeline," he said, drawing applause from members across the aisle.

The Chief Minister also took the opportunity to reiterate the state's commitment to protecting its linguistic rights, while clarifying that the resolution is not aimed at disrespecting any other language or song. "We honour all languages, but our mother tongue comes first," he added.

What the resolution entails

The resolution mandates that 'Tamil Thai Vazhthu' be sung first at all state government functions, educational institutions, and public events. It also calls for strict compliance by all departments and local bodies. While the state song has been a tradition at official events for decades, this is the first time it has been codified as a binding requirement through a legislative resolution.

Legal experts say the resolution, though not a law, carries significant moral and administrative weight. It is expected to guide government protocols and could be followed by an executive order to ensure implementation. The education department is likely to issue fresh guidelines to schools and colleges in the coming weeks.

Reactions and implications

The resolution has been widely welcomed by Tamil cultural organisations and political commentators, who see it as a reaffirmation of the state's linguistic pride. However, some have cautioned against any perception of confrontation with the Centre, urging the state to focus on constructive engagement.

Union government officials have not yet commented on the resolution. Political analysts suggest that the unanimous passage could intensify the ongoing dialogue between the state and the Centre on the use of national and regional symbols, particularly in the context of education and public ceremonies.

As the state moves to implement the resolution, all eyes will be on how the Centre responds, and whether similar resolutions are introduced in other states with strong regional linguistic identities. The coming months may well define the contours of centre-state relations on cultural matters.
