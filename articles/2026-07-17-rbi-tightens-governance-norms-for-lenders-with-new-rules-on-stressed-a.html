RBI tightens governance norms for lenders with new rules on stressed assets, data management

📅 2026-07-17 📂 Banking Original source ↗
Key points
  • New rules mandate stricter timelines and processes for stressed asset identification and resolution.
  • Lenders must improve data management systems to ensure accuracy and timeliness of financial reporting.
  • Boards of directors are required to take greater responsibility for governance and risk oversight.

The Reserve Bank of India (RBI) has introduced a fresh set of governance norms for lenders, tightening rules on how banks and non-banking financial companies (NBFCs) handle stressed assets and manage data. The move, announced on Friday, aims to strengthen the financial sector's resilience and improve transparency in operations.

Stressed asset resolution gets stricter

Under the new guidelines, lenders must now follow a more rigid timeline for identifying and resolving stressed assets. The RBI has mandated that any loan showing signs of stress must be flagged within 30 days of default. Banks then have 180 days to finalise a resolution plan, failing which they must initiate insolvency proceedings under the Insolvency and Bankruptcy Code (IBC).

The central bank has also clarified that lenders cannot evergreen loans—disguising bad loans by extending fresh credit—to avoid provisions. Any such practice will attract higher provisioning requirements and supervisory action. The rules apply uniformly to all scheduled commercial banks and large NBFCs with asset sizes above ₹500 crore.

Data management and reporting overhaul

The RBI has also tightened data management standards, requiring lenders to maintain accurate, complete, and timely data on their loan portfolios. Banks must now submit monthly data on asset quality to the central bank, with a stricter verification process to ensure consistency across reports.

Lenders have been given six months to upgrade their internal systems to meet these requirements. The RBI warned that any discrepancies or delays in reporting could result in penalties, including restrictions on lending or dividend distribution. This move is seen as a response to past instances where lenders underreported non-performing assets (NPAs).

Board oversight and accountability

The new norms also place greater responsibility on bank boards. Boards must now approve a comprehensive governance policy covering risk management, data integrity, and compliance. They are required to review stressed asset portfolios quarterly and certify that resolution plans are being implemented effectively.

Independent directors will face additional scrutiny. The RBI has directed that audit committees must include at least one member with expertise in risk management or financial reporting. The central bank has also made it clear that board members can be held personally liable for governance failures, including delays in recognising bad loans.

Industry reaction and implementation

Banking industry executives have largely welcomed the move, though some expressed concerns about the tight timelines for resolution. A senior official at a public sector bank, speaking on condition of anonymity, said the 180-day window for resolution may be challenging for large corporate loans where multiple lenders are involved.

The RBI has given lenders a transition period of three months to align their internal policies with the new norms. The central bank will conduct a review after one year to assess compliance and impact on asset quality. Non-compliance could attract monetary penalties or restrictions on business activities.

These rules come at a time when the Indian banking sector is showing signs of recovery after a prolonged period of high NPAs. Gross NPAs of scheduled commercial banks had fallen to a multi-year low of 2.5% as of March 2026, according to RBI data. The central bank appears keen to ensure this improvement is sustained and not undermined by lax governance.

What to watch

Market participants will now closely watch how lenders adjust their internal processes and whether the new norms lead to a temporary spike in reported NPAs as banks clean up their books. The RBI's next supervisory assessment, due in early 2027, will reveal the true impact of these rules on the banking sector's health.
