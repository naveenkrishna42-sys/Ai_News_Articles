RBI proposes NBFC revolving credit ban; sector seeks selective rules

📅 2026-08-11 📂 Banking Original source ↗
Key points
  • RBI has proposed a ban on revolving credit facilities offered by NBFCs, following a two-year warning period.
  • The non-banking finance sector is pushing back, urging the central bank to consider selective restrictions instead.
  • The move is aimed at curbing potential risks in consumer lending, but industry players argue it could hurt genuine borrowers.

Regulator moves to curb revolving credit

The Reserve Bank of India (RBI) has formally proposed a ban on revolving credit facilities offered by non-banking financial companies (NBFCs), a move that has been in the works for over two years. The proposal, which was communicated to industry stakeholders earlier this week, seeks to prohibit NBFCs from offering products like credit lines linked to prepaid instruments or cards, which allow borrowers to draw and repay funds repeatedly.

The central bank's stance stems from concerns over unsecured lending growth and the potential for borrowers to fall into debt traps. Revolving credit, unlike term loans, does not have a fixed repayment schedule, making it harder for lenders to assess and manage credit risk.

Sector asks for nuance

The industry, however, has responded with a counter-proposal, urging the RBI to consider a more calibrated approach. NBFC associations and major players have argued that a blanket ban would hurt small businesses and low-income households that rely on such facilities for short-term liquidity needs.

Representatives from the sector have suggested that restrictions could be limited to certain categories of borrowers or capped at specific ticket sizes, rather than an outright prohibition. They also pointed out that regulated NBFCs have improved their underwriting standards and risk management frameworks significantly in recent years.

What the RBI has said

The RBI had first flagged concerns about revolving credit lines in its guidelines issued two years ago, when it warned NBFCs about the risks associated with such products. At the time, it had asked lenders to review their offerings and ensure adequate safeguards, but did not propose a ban.

Now, the regulator appears to have concluded that voluntary compliance has been inadequate. In its latest communication, the RBI has cited instances of aggressive marketing and inadequate disclosure practices, which it believes could lead to systemic stress if left unchecked.

Industry pushback

Industry bodies have been holding meetings with RBI officials over the past few weeks, presenting data to show that default rates on revolving credit remain manageable. They have also argued that such products are critical for financial inclusion, especially in semi-urban and rural areas where access to formal credit is limited.

Some NBFC executives, speaking on condition of anonymity, said a complete ban could push borrowers towards unregulated lenders, which would be counterproductive to the RBI's objective of safeguarding consumer interests.

The final decision, expected in the coming months, will likely hinge on whether the RBI accepts the sector's arguments for selective restrictions or proceeds with a full ban. Analysts note that the central bank has historically been open to industry feedback, but has also shown a willingness to act decisively when it perceives risks to financial stability.

For now, NBFCs are bracing for tighter norms, even as they hope for a middle path. The next round of consultations is expected to take place later this month.
