Google pulls AI satellite image tool after deepfake misuse

📅 2026-08-01 📂 Technology Original source ↗
Key points
  • Google launched Nano Banana, an AI image generator integrated with Google Earth, on Friday.
  • Users quickly generated photorealistic deepfakes of bombings, riots and destruction at real locations.
  • Alphabet removed the feature in under 24 hours, citing policy violations and widespread misuse.
  • The company acknowledged screenshots of fake scenes were being shared widely on social media.

Google has abruptly pulled the plug on its new AI image generator, Nano Banana, which was integrated with Google Earth, after users turned the tool into a machine for creating photorealistic satellite deepfakes. The feature, launched on Friday, was designed to let people transform any place on the map with a simple prompt. Within hours, it became a vehicle for simulated bombings, riots and scenes of destruction at real-world locations.

The rollback came in less than 24 hours, a remarkably swift retreat for a product that was meant to showcase Alphabet's generative AI capabilities. A company spokesperson said the decision followed policy violations and the rapid spread of misleading imagery. The statement noted that people had been sharing screenshots of the fake scenes across social media platforms, prompting an immediate response.

What was Nano Banana?

Nano Banana was pitched as a creative tool that allowed users to reimagine any location on Google Earth. Instead of merely viewing satellite imagery, users could prompt the AI to alter the visual appearance of a place, adding or removing elements in a hyper-realistic manner. The tool was integrated directly into the Google Earth interface, making it accessible to millions of users.

The name, a playful nod to the popular AI image generator Gemini, suggested a lighter, more experimental offering. But the underlying technology proved to be far more potent than expected. Users quickly discovered that the same capabilities could be used to fabricate catastrophic events, placing them at specific coordinates with alarming accuracy.

Misuse and public reaction

Within hours of the launch, screenshots began circulating online showing AI-generated satellite views of bomb craters, burning buildings and chaotic street scenes at identifiable locations. The Washington Post reported that people were using the tool to make fake scenes of bombings, riots and destruction, raising immediate concerns about disinformation and public safety.

The Times of India noted that Google acknowledged the problem in a statement, saying, "We have also seen people sharing screenshots." The company did not specify how many users had generated such images, nor did it detail the exact policy violations that triggered the rollback. Officials have not yet confirmed whether any of the fake images had been mistaken for real events before the tool was disabled.

Policy and accountability questions

The incident raises fresh questions about the safeguards around generative AI tools, especially those tied to geospatial data. Unlike text-based AI, which can be moderated through filters and prompts, image generators that operate on real-world maps present a unique challenge: they can fabricate events at precise locations, making them harder to debunk at a glance.

Alphabet's swift action suggests an awareness of the reputational and legal risks involved. But critics argue that the launch itself was reckless, given the known capabilities of such models. The company has not said whether Nano Banana will return in a more restricted form, or whether additional safeguards will be implemented before any future rollout.

For now, the feature remains offline, and Google Earth has reverted to its standard satellite imagery. The episode serves as a cautionary tale for tech firms racing to deploy generative AI in consumer products without fully anticipating how it might be weaponised.

What to watch next

Google is likely to face further scrutiny from regulators and researchers over its testing protocols. The company may also release a post-mortem explaining how the misuse slipped through. Whether Nano Banana makes a comeback, with stricter controls, remains an open question.
