Hanson-Young threatens Senate action on gambling reform

📅 2026-08-09 📂 Health Original source ↗
Key points
  • Senator Sarah Hanson-Young demands gambling reform, threatens Senate action
  • Bird flu cases in NSW native species raise concerns
  • Calls to vaccinate wildlife at risk from H5N1
  • Detection of bird flu in wild birds doubling rapidly

Senator issues ultimatum on gambling reform

Greens senator Sarah Hanson-Young has delivered a blunt message to the federal government: act on gambling reform now, or the crossbench will force the issue in the Senate. Her warning comes amid growing frustration over the pace of legislative change.

"We'll do it in the Senate ourselves," Hanson-Young said, according to reports. The comment signals a breakdown in patience with the government's approach to curbing gambling harms, a policy area that has seen repeated delays and watered-down measures.

The senator's remarks have intensified pressure on the government, which has faced criticism from advocacy groups and crossbench MPs for not moving fast enough on issues like advertising bans and online betting restrictions.

Bird flu spreads to native species

Separately, concerns are mounting over new bird flu cases in New South Wales, with authorities noting infections in native species at locations described as "really far inland." The development marks a worrying shift in the virus's reach.

Avian influenza, particularly the H5N1 strain, has been detected in wild birds at double the rate in less than four days, according to recent data. Experts fear Australia's unique wildlife could face devastating losses if the virus spreads unchecked.

The situation echoes the UK's experience, where seabirds died in their thousands when bird flu struck. Australian ornithologists and conservationists warn that wild flocks here could suffer a similar fate.

Calls for wildlife vaccination

In response, scientists and environmental groups are urging the government to urgently vaccinate wildlife most at risk from H5N1. The proposal, highlighted by Down To Earth, argues that proactive vaccination is essential to protect vulnerable species.

However, logistical challenges remain, including the scale of vaccination efforts and the difficulty of reaching remote bird populations. Authorities have not yet confirmed any formal vaccination programme.

The rapid spread among wild birds has also raised concerns for domestic poultry and the broader agricultural sector, though officials have yet to report any farm outbreaks linked to the recent cases.

Political and environmental crossroads

Hanson-Young's threat on gambling reform and the bird flu escalation represent two pressing issues demanding federal attention. Both require swift, decisive action, yet the government's response on each remains under scrutiny.

On gambling, the senator's stance could force a parliamentary showdown, with the crossbench potentially rallying to pass legislation independent of government support. Such a move would test the government's control over its legislative agenda.

On bird flu, the focus is on containing the outbreak and protecting biodiversity. The coming weeks will likely determine whether Australia can avoid the mass wildlife deaths seen overseas.

As the Senate returns and disease surveillance continues, all eyes will be on the government's next moves. Whether it heeds Hanson-Young's warning or faces a crossbench revolt, and whether it acts on vaccination advice, will shape both public health and political stability in the months ahead.
