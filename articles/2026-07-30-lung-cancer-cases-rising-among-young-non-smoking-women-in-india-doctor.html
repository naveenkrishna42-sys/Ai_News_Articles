Lung cancer cases rising among young non-smoking women in India, doctors say

📅 2026-07-30 📂 Health Original source ↗
Key points
  • Lung cancer incidence is increasing in young non-smoking women.
  • Indoor air pollution from cooking fumes and second-hand smoke are suspected causes.
  • Experts urge awareness and early detection through low-dose CT scans.

New trend worries oncologists

Doctors in India are reporting a troubling pattern. Lung cancer is being diagnosed more frequently in young women who have never smoked. The cases are emerging in their 30s and 40s, an age group traditionally considered low-risk for the disease.

Oncologists say the shift demands a rethinking of risk factors. Smoking remains the leading cause of lung cancer worldwide, but these women do not fit that profile. Their diagnosis often comes as a shock.

The silent danger inside homes

Experts point to indoor air pollution as a key suspect. The smoke from cooking stoves, especially biomass and kerosene burners, releases fine particulate matter that lodges deep in the lungs. Prolonged exposure over years can trigger cellular damage.

Second-hand smoke is another hidden threat. Many non-smoking women live with family members who smoke indoors, exposing them to carcinogens regularly. The cumulative effect, doctors warn, is as dangerous as active smoking.

Other habits that harm lungs

Beyond smoke, certain everyday habits are under scrutiny. The use of incense sticks and mosquito coils releases harmful chemicals when burned. Synthetic air fresheners and cleaning products also emit volatile organic compounds that can irritate lung tissue.

Urban women face additional risks from outdoor air pollution. But for those spending most of their time at home, the quality of indoor air may matter more. Poor ventilation amplifies the concentration of these pollutants.

Early detection is key

Lung cancer is often diagnosed at an advanced stage because early symptoms like a persistent cough or breathlessness are dismissed. Women with no history of smoking are especially unlikely to suspect cancer, delaying medical consultation.

Low-dose CT scans can detect tumours early, improving survival rates significantly. Countries like Taiwan and the UK have already begun screening high-risk populations. India, doctors argue, needs similar awareness campaigns.

Doctors stress that a cough lasting more than three weeks, unexplained weight loss, or chest pain should not be ignored — even in a young, non-smoking woman.

What to watch

Public health authorities are yet to issue specific guidelines for non-smoking women. But the trend is clear enough that experts are calling for larger studies to understand the scale of the problem. In the coming months, awareness drives targeting household air quality are expected to gain momentum.
