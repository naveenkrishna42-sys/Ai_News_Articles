Modi-Xi Reset: What the BRICS Bilateral Means for India-China Ties

✍️ By TIVRA News Editorial Team 📅 2026-09-13 📂 Analysis Original source ↗
Key points
  • First India-China bilateral on Indian soil since 2019 signals a calibrated diplomatic thaw after the Galwan rupture.
  • Border tranquillity remains the non-negotiable precondition New Delhi has attached to normalisation of ties.
  • Trade and supply-chain realities, including India's heavy import dependence on China, will decide how far the reset travels.

Prime Minister Narendra Modi and Chinese President Xi Jinping met on the sidelines of the BRICS summit, the first structured India-China bilateral on Indian soil since 2019. The encounter follows a multi-year freeze triggered by the Galwan Valley clash, and both sides framed it as an opportunity to discuss cooperation rather than a breakthrough. For New Delhi, the meeting is a calibrated reopening of a channel that has been largely dormant, not a reset in the sense of restored trust.

Why This Bilateral Matters Beyond the Photo Op

India-China relations are not a bilateral file alone. They shape Asian security, global supply chains and the pricing of everything from smartphones to solar modules. A functioning diplomatic channel reduces the risk of miscalculation along the Line of Actual Control (LAC), where thousands of troops remain deployed in close proximity even after disengagement at several friction points.

For New Delhi, the sequencing is deliberate: border tranquillity first, then economic normalisation. PM Modi conveyed this directly to Xi, according to multiple reports, making clear that peace on the frontier is the foundation on which any wider engagement rests.

The Four-Point Formula and Border Tranquillity

Xi outlined a four-point formula to reset ties, according to reports, covering mutual respect, dialogue, economic cooperation and multilateral coordination. The framing is familiar from Beijing's diplomatic playbook, but the emphasis on peacemaking roles in West Asia signals China's ambition to position BRICS as a parallel diplomatic platform.

New Delhi's reading is more cautious. India has consistently linked the pace of normalisation to verified disengagement and de-escalation along the LAC. Without that, trade and investment liberalisation toward Chinese firms remains politically difficult, particularly with domestic manufacturing incentives now entrenched under the Production Linked Incentive (PLI) schemes.

Trade, Supply Chains and the Real-World Buyer Impact

India's import dependence on China is structural. Active pharmaceutical ingredients, lithium-ion cells, solar wafers, electronics components and specialised machinery flow in at scale. Any easing of visa restrictions for Chinese technicians or approvals for joint ventures would directly affect production timelines for Indian manufacturers in electronics and EVs.

Conversely, an escalation would raise input costs. Indian buyers of laptops, solar panels and battery packs would absorb higher prices within one to two quarters, as seen during previous supply disruptions. The bilateral therefore has a measurable consumer price dimension, not just a strategic one.

Direct Chinese investment into India remains tightly screened under Press Note 3, and that regime is unlikely to be dismantled quickly. Expect calibrated sectoral openings, not a blanket liberalisation.

Pros and Cons of the Emerging Reset

  • Pros: Reduced border miscalculation risk; restoration of diplomatic and military hotlines; potential easing of visa bottlenecks for Chinese technicians; smoother supply of critical components for Indian manufacturing.
  • Cons: Structural trade deficit remains unresolved; Press Note 3 investment curbs stay in place; trust deficit from Galwan persists; domestic political opposition to rapid normalisation limits room for manoeuvre.
  • Real-world highlight: Disengagement at friction points has held, but full de-escalation and buffer-zone restoration remain incomplete, meaning any reset is reversible.

What to Watch Next

Three indicators will reveal whether this bilateral translates into substance. First, the pace of military-level talks on de-escalation and buffer zones. Second, any movement on direct flight restoration and visa issuance for business travellers. Third, sector-specific signals on Chinese investment approvals in electronics and renewables.

For Indian businesses and consumers, the practical question is not whether ties improve rhetorically, but whether component costs, project timelines and border stability improve materially. That will take quarters, not a single summit handshake.
