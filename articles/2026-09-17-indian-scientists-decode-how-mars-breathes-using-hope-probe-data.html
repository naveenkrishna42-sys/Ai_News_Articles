Indian Scientists Decode How Mars 'Breathes' Using Hope Probe Data

Why Mars Appears to 'Breathe' Every Single Day

Researchers at the Physical Research Laboratory (PRL) in Ahmedabad have mapped the daily thermal pulse of the Martian atmosphere using data from the Emirates Mars Mission (Hope probe). The findings describe a planet whose thin carbon dioxide atmosphere visibly swells under sunlight and contracts sharply after sunset — a rhythmic expansion and contraction that scientists describe as Mars 'breathing'.

The work matters beyond academic curiosity. Any spacecraft, parachute, or crewed habitat that enters the Martian atmosphere must survive this cycle. Understanding it precisely is the difference between a controlled entry and a mission-ending structural failure.

The 400-Degree Temperature Swing Explained

Mars' atmosphere is roughly 100 times thinner than Earth's, so it retains almost no heat. Surface temperatures near the equator can climb close to 20 degrees Celsius at local noon and plunge below minus 100 degrees Celsius before dawn. At higher altitudes and polar latitudes, the variance is even more extreme, with upper-atmosphere readings approaching minus 150 degrees Celsius.

The PRL team used Hope's Emirates Mars Infrared Spectrometer (EMIRS) to track how this heat redistributes vertically and horizontally. The instrument samples the lower and middle atmosphere across daily and seasonal cycles, letting researchers build a far more complete thermal map than earlier single-point measurements allowed.

What This Means for Future Mars Missions

Atmospheric density determines how much drag a spacecraft encounters. Because Mars' atmosphere thickens and thins daily, entry, descent, and landing (EDL) windows must be timed to the hour. A lander arriving during the coldest, thinnest phase needs more propellant; one arriving during peak daytime expansion can carry a lighter heat shield.

For India, this is commercially relevant. ISRO's Mars Orbiter Mission (Mangalyaan) cost roughly Rs 450 crore and succeeded on its first attempt in 2014. The planned Mars Orbiter Mission-2 and any future lander will benefit directly from thermal models built on Hope data. Reducing mass and propellant margins translates into cheaper launch vehicles and lower insurance premiums for the mission.

Real-World Buyer and Policy Implications

  • Lower mission cost: Accurate thermal modelling cuts unnecessary shielding mass, potentially saving crores in launch costs.
  • Better landing reliability: EDL teams can select arrival windows with predictable atmospheric density.
  • Scientific leverage: India gains analytical credibility in global planetary science without owning the spacecraft that collected the data.
  • Talent retention: High-visibility PRL work strengthens India's domestic space research pipeline.

Pros and Cons of India's Data-Driven Approach

  • Pro: Extracting world-class science from international datasets costs a fraction of building a dedicated orbiter.
  • Pro: Findings feed directly into ISRO's next-generation Mars mission planning.
  • Con: Dependence on foreign instruments limits control over data cadence and access timelines.
  • Con: Without an Indian Mars orbiter currently active, follow-up observations rely on partner goodwill.

The Bottom Line

PRL's analysis converts raw Hope probe telemetry into actionable engineering data. The 400-degree daily swing is not a curiosity — it is a design constraint that shapes heat shields, parachutes, and power budgets. India's ability to lead such analysis at low cost strengthens its position in the global Mars research community and makes the case for funding its own return to the Red Planet.
