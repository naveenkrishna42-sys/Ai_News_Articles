Delhi nutritionist Lovneet Batra debunks common health food myths

📅 2026-07-18 📂 Lifestyle Original source ↗
Key points
  • Lovneet Batra warns against blindly following health food trends without scientific backing.
  • She debunks myths around superfoods, detox diets and the idea of 'good' and 'bad' foods.
  • Batra advocates for balanced, sustainable eating habits over restrictive or fad diets.

Delhi-based nutritionist Lovneet Batra has been at the forefront of busting health food myths that often mislead consumers. In a recent interaction with Lifestyle Asia, she laid out the truth behind several popular but questionable dietary trends.

Superfoods are not magic bullets

Batra says the term 'superfood' is more marketing than science. While foods like quinoa, kale and acai berries are nutrient-dense, no single food can compensate for a poor diet.

She explains that what matters is the overall dietary pattern, not just a few trendy ingredients. A balanced meal with variety is far more effective than loading up on one expensive superfood.

The detox diet delusion

Detox diets are another area where Batra flags concern. She says the human body is equipped with its own detox mechanisms — the liver and kidneys work round the clock to eliminate toxins.

Extreme juice cleanses or fasting regimens can do more harm than good. They often lead to muscle loss, metabolic slowdown and nutritional deficiencies. Batra recommends supporting the body's natural processes with whole foods, hydration and adequate fibre, rather than restrictive detox plans.

Labeling foods as 'good' or 'bad'

Batra also takes aim at the binary classification of foods. Demonising sugar or carbs entirely can trigger guilt and disordered eating patterns. She advocates for moderation — it's fine to enjoy a dessert occasionally without feeling the need to 'compensate' later.

Her advice is to focus on adding nutritious foods to your plate rather than obsessing over what to cut out. This approach is more sustainable and mentally healthier.

Organic vs conventional: What you need to know

On the organic versus conventional food debate, Batra says the choice depends on personal budget and priorities. Organic produce may have lower pesticide residue, but conventional fruits and vegetables are still safe and nutritious when washed properly.

She advises consumers to prioritise eating more vegetables and fruits in any form rather than stressing over labels. The bigger issue, she notes, is that most Indians eat far too few vegetables overall.

Gluten-free and dairy-free trends

Batra points out that gluten-free diets are essential only for those with celiac disease or confirmed gluten sensitivity. For the general population, whole grains containing gluten are a valuable source of fibre, B vitamins and minerals.

Similarly, dairy is a key source of calcium and protein for many Indians. Unless there is a diagnosed intolerance, cutting it out without a substitute can lead to bone health issues down the line.

She encourages people to get tested before eliminating entire food groups based on trends or hearsay.

What to watch

Batra's core message is simple: be sceptical of health claims that promise quick fixes or dramatic results. She urges consumers to rely on evidence-based guidance from qualified professionals rather than influencers or viral posts. As more such myths get busted, the hope is that Indians will make more informed, balanced choices about what they eat every day.
