Childhood Pneumonia: Prevention, Symptoms, and Treatment Guide

Understanding Childhood Pneumonia

Pneumonia is an acute respiratory infection that inflames the air sacs in one or both lungs. In children, it is most commonly caused by bacteria such as Streptococcus pneumoniae or viruses such as respiratory syncytial virus (RSV). The infection fills the alveoli with pus and fluid, making breathing painful and limiting oxygen intake.

Recognising Warning Signs Early

Caregivers should watch for cough, fever, and rapid or difficult breathing. Chest indrawing, where the lower chest wall moves inward during inhalation, signals severe disease. Infants may also show lethargy, poor feeding, or grunting with every breath.

Danger signs requiring immediate care include inability to breastfeed or drink, repeated vomiting, convulsions, and blue-tinged lips or face. Fast breathing thresholds vary by age: 60 or more breaths per minute for infants under two months, 50 for those aged two to twelve months, and 40 for children aged one to five years.

Prevention Through Vaccination and Hygiene

Routine immunisation remains the strongest defence. The pneumococcal conjugate vaccine (PCV) and Haemophilus influenzae type b (Hib) vaccine protect against common bacterial causes. Measles and pertussis vaccines also lower pneumonia risk indirectly.

Exclusive breastfeeding for the first six months builds passive immunity. Reducing indoor air pollution from biomass cooking stoves and avoiding tobacco smoke exposure further cuts risk. Handwashing with soap limits viral spread in households and schools.

Treatment Pathways and Medical Guidelines

Bacterial pneumonia is treated with antibiotics, most commonly amoxicillin in dispersible tablet form for severe cases under World Health Organization and Integrated Management of Childhood Illness guidelines. Viral pneumonia usually requires supportive care, including hydration and oxygen when oxygen saturation drops below 90 percent.

Hospitalisation is necessary for severe pneumonia, especially when chest indrawing, central cyanosis, or inability to feed is present. Oxygen therapy and close monitoring can prevent progression to respiratory failure. Self-medication with leftover antibiotics should be avoided.

Key Real-World Highlights

  • Pneumonia is a leading infectious cause of death in children under five worldwide.
  • PCV and Hib vaccines are available under India's Universal Immunisation Programme in expanding districts.
  • Fast breathing and chest indrawing are urgent clinical red flags.
  • Amoxicillin dispersible tablets are the first-line antibiotic for non-severe pneumonia in many protocols.
  • Exclusive breastfeeding and smoke-free homes significantly lower infection risk.
