Police Refute 24‑Karati Gold‑Plated Ganesh Idol Claims in Hyderabad

Key points
  • Forensic tests show the idol contains trace gold and a copper‑zinc base.
  • Police filed cheating and forgery charges after determining the claim was misleading.
  • The episode stresses the need for verified authenticity in gold‑plated religious artefacts.

Investigation Initiated After Viral Claims

In mid‑September 2026, social media posts circulated images of a Ganesh idol displayed in Goshamahal, Hyderabad, described as being coated with 24‑karat gold. The posts claimed the idol’s value ran into several crores, prompting a surge of devotee visits and online debate. Local police, acting on complaints of possible fraud, seized the idol for forensic examination.

Forensic Analysis Reveals Base Metal Composition

Authorities sent the idol to the State Forensic Science Laboratory for X‑ray fluorescence (XRF) testing and microscopy. The results showed the core was a copper‑zinc alloy with a negligible gold presence, far below the threshold for genuine 24‑karat plating. The detectable gold layer, if any, measured less than 0.5 micrometre, which is considered a superficial wash rather than a functional plating.

Legal Action and Community Response

Police registered a case under Sections 420 (cheating) and 468 (forgery) of the Indian Penal Code after determining the claim was deliberately misleading. The idol’s custodian was questioned and released on bail pending further inquiry. Community leaders urged calm, emphasizing that religious sentiment should not be exploited for financial gain, and requested transparent communication from event organizers.

Market Perspective on Gold‑Plated Religious Artefacts

In the Indian market, genuine 24‑karat gold plating on large idols is rare due to cost; most decorative pieces use imitation gold or gold‑coloured paints. A authentic 24‑karat layer of sufficient thickness would add lakhs to the price, making the idol’s alleged value inconsistent with its size and material. Experts note that buyers should rely on certified assay reports rather than visual claims when assessing gold‑plated items.

  • Forensic testing exposed the claim as baseless, protecting public trust.
  • Legal proceedings deter future misrepresentation of religious artefacts.
  • The incident highlights the need for affordable, verifiable authenticity checks in the market.
