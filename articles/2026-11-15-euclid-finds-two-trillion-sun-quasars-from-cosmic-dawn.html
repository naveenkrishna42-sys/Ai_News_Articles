Euclid Finds Two Trillion-Sun Quasars From Cosmic Dawn

✍️ By TIVRA News Science & Health Desk 📅 2026-11-15 📂 Science Original source ↗
Key points
  • Euclid's wide-field near-infrared survey has identified two quasars whose output is put at roughly a trillion times the Sun, the earliest such beacons reported.
  • Quasars this early force astronomers to explain how supermassive black holes grew so large so soon after the Big Bang.
  • The candidates still need spectroscopic confirmation from ground telescopes and JWST before redshifts and masses are final.

What Euclid Actually Found

ESA's Euclid mission has turned up two quasars whose output is placed at about a trillion times that of the Sun, described as the earliest such objects yet seen, per the Science Daily report. Their light left the universe when it was a small fraction of its present age, which is why they function as beacons rather than as ordinary galaxy cores.

A quasar is not a star. It is the glare of gas heating up as it spirals into a supermassive black hole. At roughly 10^12 solar luminosities, the central engine outshines every star in its host galaxy combined, which is exactly what makes it visible across such distances.

Why "Earliest Ever" Carries Weight

Each confirmed quasar at extreme distance tightens the models of seed black hole formation. Growth to hundreds of millions of solar masses within the universe's first few hundred million years strains standard accretion timelines, so these objects act as direct stress tests for competing theories.

How A Wide Survey Catches Them

Euclid is a surveyor, not a deep-field telescope. It repeatedly images broad sky areas and takes slitless spectra with its near-infrared instrument, letting teams flag point sources with broad emission lines and hand the shortlist to ground observatories and JWST for confirmation.

Value For The Science Return

The practical gain is sample size. Deep fields give exquisite detail on a few dozen objects; a wide survey trades depth for area and can find rare bright quasars that targeted campaigns miss. That statistical reach is what lets cosmologists cross-check dark-energy measurements against the matter distribution these beacons trace.

Key Real-World Highlights

  • Pro: near-infrared reach captures high-redshift emission lines that optical surveys lose.
  • Pro: wide-area scanning is the efficient route to rare, luminous early-universe objects.
  • Con: two detections are not a population; statistics stay weak until the survey deepens.
  • Con: reported luminosity and distance await spectroscopic confirmation and host-galaxy detail.
  • Con: Euclid's resolution cannot resolve the black hole environment; JWST must do that.

What To Watch Next

Watch for published redshifts, black hole mass estimates from follow-up spectra, and whether additional trillion-sun candidates surface as the survey accumulates exposure. Confirmation would lock these two into the early-black-hole record; a revision downward would still leave a useful bright-source sample for cosmology.
