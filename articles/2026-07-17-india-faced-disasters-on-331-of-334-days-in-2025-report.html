India faced disasters on 331 of 334 days in 2025: Report

📅 2026-07-17 📂 Environment Original source ↗
Key points
  • India recorded disasters on 331 of 334 days in 2025, up from 295 in 2024 and 292 in 2022.
  • The year saw a mix of heatwaves, floods, landslides and cyclones across multiple states.
  • Experts link the trend to climate change, with no region spared from extreme events.

India experienced extreme weather events on 331 out of 334 days in 2025, according to a new analysis by Down To Earth. This marks a sharp increase from 295 disaster days in 2024 and 292 in 2022.

The report paints a grim picture of a year defined by heatwaves, floods, landslides and cyclones. Only three days in the entire year passed without a significant weather-related incident.

Heatwaves and floods dominate

Summer months saw record-breaking temperatures across northern and central India. Heatwaves stretched for weeks, straining power grids and leading to hundreds of heatstroke cases.

Monsoon season brought its own fury. States like Kerala, Assam and Maharashtra faced repeated flooding and landslides. In some regions, rainfall exceeded normal levels by over 40%.

Cyclones formed in both the Arabian Sea and the Bay of Bengal. Cyclone Remal in May and Cyclone Dana in October caused widespread damage along the eastern coast.

No region spared

From the Himalayan states to the southern tip, no part of the country remained untouched. Uttarakhand and Himachal Pradesh battled flash floods and cloudbursts. Tamil Nadu and Andhra Pradesh faced severe cyclonic storms.

Urban centres like Delhi, Mumbai and Bengaluru also felt the impact. Extreme rainfall led to waterlogging and traffic chaos in cities not designed for such events.

Agriculture took a heavy blow. Farmers in Punjab and Haryana reported crop losses due to unseasonal rains. In Rajasthan, drought-like conditions persisted even as other states drowned.

Climate change fingerprints

Meteorologists point to a clear pattern. Warmer oceans and changing wind patterns are making extreme events more frequent and intense. The Indian subcontinent, already vulnerable, is bearing the brunt.

Dr R. Krishnan, a climate scientist at the Indian Institute of Tropical Meteorology, told the publication that the data aligns with global warming projections. “What we once called once-in-a-century events are now happening every few years,” he said.

The Centre has not yet released its own assessment of 2025's weather disasters. Officials said a detailed report will be prepared after the year ends.

What comes next

With 2025 drawing to a close, the question is whether 2026 will bring any respite. Forecasts suggest La Niña conditions may develop, potentially altering rainfall patterns.

But experts caution that the underlying trend is clear. Without faster emission cuts and better disaster preparedness, extreme years like 2025 could become the new normal.
