COVID-19 Pushed Breast Cancer Radiotherapy to Shorter, Cheaper Regimens

Key points

  • Medicare saves approximately $10 million per year due to this practice change.
  • Shorter regimens are equally effective for most early-stage breast cancers, but not all patients are eligible.
  • The COVID-19 pandemic acted as a catalyst, overcoming inertia in clinical practice.

How COVID-19 Changed Breast Cancer Radiation Therapy Forever

The COVID-19 pandemic disrupted healthcare systems worldwide, but it also triggered a lasting shift in breast cancer treatment. A new study from the Neiman Health Policy Institute reveals that the pandemic accelerated the adoption of hypofractionated radiation therapy—a shorter, lower-cost approach that delivers the same effective dose in fewer sessions. This change is now saving Medicare an estimated $10 million annually and reducing the burden on patients.

Before the pandemic, many women with early-stage breast cancer received conventional radiation therapy requiring 25 to 30 daily sessions over five to six weeks. Hypofractionation condenses treatment into 15 to 20 sessions over three to four weeks, often at a lower total dose. While clinical evidence supporting its safety and efficacy existed for years, uptake was slow. COVID-19 made prolonged hospital visits risky, forcing oncologists to rethink standard protocols.

What the Data Shows: Adoption and Savings

Researchers analysed Medicare claims data from 2015 to 2022, tracking the proportion of breast cancer patients receiving hypofractionated versus conventional radiation. The study found a sharp increase in hypofractionation starting in 2020, coinciding with the pandemic's first wave. By 2022, hypofractionation had become the dominant approach for eligible patients.

The financial impact is significant. Medicare spending on breast radiation therapy dropped by approximately $10 million per year compared to what it would have been under previous practice patterns. For patients, out-of-pocket costs also fall—fewer sessions mean lower copayments and less travel and time off work.

Clinical Equivalence and Real-World Implications

Multiple randomised trials, including the UK START trials and the Canadian OCOG trial, have shown that hypofractionation offers equivalent tumour control and cosmetic outcomes for most women with early-stage breast cancer. The risk of long-term side effects, such as skin changes or heart complications, is not increased when modern techniques are used.

However, hypofractionation is not suitable for everyone. Patients with certain connective tissue disorders, prior chest radiation, or very large breast size may still require conventional fractionation. Also, those receiving regional nodal irradiation or who have undergone mastectomy with reconstruction may need individualised planning.

Dr. Anand Sharma, a radiation oncologist at a leading Indian cancer centre, notes: "The pandemic removed logistical barriers and showed that shorter courses are practical. But we must ensure that the right patients are selected—it's not a one-size-fits-all solution."

Pros and Cons of Shorter Radiation Therapy

  • Pros:
    • Reduces hospital visits by 40–50%, lowering COVID-19 exposure risk during the pandemic.
    • Saves patients time and money—less travel, parking, and time off work.
    • Lowers Medicare expenditure by ~$10 million per year, easing healthcare budgets.
    • Improves quality of life by minimising disruption to daily routines.
    • Evidence shows equivalent cancer control and safety for eligible patients.
  • Cons:
    • Not suitable for all breast cancer subtypes or patients with certain comorbidities.
    • Requires precise treatment planning and modern equipment, which may not be available in rural or low-resource settings.
    • Long-term data beyond 10 years is still maturing for some hypofractionation schedules.
    • Some clinicians remain hesitant to change longstanding practices without further real-world evidence.

What This Means for Patients and Policymakers

For patients, the message is clear: if you are diagnosed with early-stage breast cancer, ask your radiation oncologist whether a shorter course is appropriate for you. It could mean fewer trips to the hospital, less financial strain, and similar outcomes. In India, where out-of-pocket expenses often dominate cancer care, hypofractionation can reduce costs by ₹30,000–₹50,000 per patient, depending on the hospital.

Policymakers should take note. The $10 million annual savings in the US Medicare system demonstrate that evidence-based practice changes can yield substantial fiscal benefits. In India, where government schemes like Ayushman Bharat cover millions, promoting hypofractionation could free up resources for other cancer services.

The pandemic may have been a catalyst, but the shift to shorter radiation therapy is here to stay. It represents a rare win-win: better patient experience, lower costs, and maintained clinical outcomes.
