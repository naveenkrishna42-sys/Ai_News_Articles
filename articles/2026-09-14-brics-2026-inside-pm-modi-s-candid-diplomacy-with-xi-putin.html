BRICS 2026: The Diplomacy Behind the Handshakes

The 18th BRICS Summit in New Delhi has concluded, but the visuals that linger are not the formal plenary sessions. Unseen footage released after the event captures Prime Minister Narendra Modi sharing tea, exchanging hugs and engaging in animated handshakes with Chinese President Xi Jinping, Russian President Vladimir Putin and Indonesian President Prabowo Subianto. These candid clips are more than optics; they signal the informal groundwork that precedes formal agreements like the New Delhi Declaration.

For observers of multilateral diplomacy, such moments matter. They indicate which bilateral relationships are warm enough to absorb friction on trade, defence or border issues. For Indian businesses and consumers, the outcomes of those conversations directly influence import costs, energy security and export access.

What the New Delhi Declaration Actually Delivers

The New Delhi Declaration, the summit's core document, focuses on reforming multilateral institutions, expanding local-currency trade settlement and coordinating positions on food and energy security. Unlike previous declarations, this one carries specific language on supply chain resilience and digital public infrastructure, areas where India has a proven domestic track record.

The declaration does not create binding obligations, but it sets a negotiating baseline for the next summit. For Indian exporters, the practical takeaway is a gradual push toward rupee-based settlement in select markets, which could reduce dollar conversion costs over the medium term.

Why Expanded BRICS Changes Buyer Economics

BRICS now represents a majority of the world's population and a substantial share of global GDP on purchasing power parity. That scale matters for procurement teams sourcing raw materials, electronics and agricultural commodities. When member states coordinate on customs facilitation or payment rails, landed costs can shift by several percentage points.

Indian importers of crude oil, edible oils and fertilisers should track the declaration's language on local-currency settlement. If rupee-rouble or rupee-real channels deepen, hedging costs could fall. Conversely, if coordination remains rhetorical, buyers will continue absorbing dollar volatility.

Real-World Highlights and Watchpoints

  • Pro: Candid leader-level engagement reduces the risk of public diplomatic breakdowns during negotiations.
  • Pro: Local-currency settlement pilots could lower forex conversion costs for Indian traders over time.
  • Pro: India's digital public infrastructure is positioned as an exportable model within the bloc.
  • Con: The declaration is non-binding, so implementation depends on individual member priorities.
  • Con: Divergent interests among expanded members may slow concrete trade facilitation.
  • Con: Currency settlement gains will be gradual, not immediate, for small and mid-sized exporters.

The Bottom Line for Indian Stakeholders

The unseen clips of tea and handshakes are a useful proxy for diplomatic temperature, but they are not a substitute for policy detail. The New Delhi Declaration's value lies in the negotiating space it creates for rupee trade, supply chain cooperation and institutional reform. Buyers and exporters should treat the summit as a directional signal, not an instant pricing event.

Watch the next few months for pilot announcements on local-currency settlement and customs coordination. Those operational details, not the photo opportunities, will determine whether BRICS 2026 delivers measurable economic value for Indian businesses and consumers.
