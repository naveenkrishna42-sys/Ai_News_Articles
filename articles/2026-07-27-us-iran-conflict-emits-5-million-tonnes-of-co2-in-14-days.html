US-Iran Conflict Emits 5 Million Tonnes of CO2 in 14 Days

📅 2026-07-27 📂 Environment Original source ↗
Key points
  • 5 million tonnes of CO2 emitted in first 14 days of US strikes on Iran
  • Emissions equivalent to annual output of 1.1 million cars
  • Conflict adds to global carbon burden amid climate crisis

An analysis has found that the first 14 days of US military strikes against Iran generated approximately 5 million tonnes of carbon dioxide. The figure highlights the significant environmental cost of modern warfare, drawing attention to a crisis that often remains overshadowed by immediate geopolitical concerns.

Emissions equivalent to 1.1 million cars

The 5 million tonnes of CO2 released during the opening phase of the conflict is roughly equal to the annual tailpipe emissions of 1.1 million passenger vehicles. This estimate, based on data from military fuel consumption and explosive munitions, underscores the carbon intensity of sustained air campaigns.

Analysts warn that such emissions could undermine global climate goals. The war comes at a time when nations are struggling to curb greenhouse gas emissions under the Paris Agreement.

War's hidden carbon footprint

Military operations are among the most carbon-intensive human activities. Fighter jets, bombers, naval vessels, and ground vehicles burn vast quantities of fossil fuels. Each strike mission by a single fighter aircraft can consume thousands of litres of aviation fuel, releasing tonnes of CO2 into the atmosphere.

The analysis, conducted by a team of environmental researchers, used satellite data and fuel consumption models to calculate the emissions. It did not include indirect emissions from reconstruction, supply chains, or displaced populations.

Global implications

The conflict in Iran adds a new dimension to the climate crisis. While the war's geopolitical consequences dominate headlines, its environmental toll is likely to be long-lasting. The emissions from just two weeks of operations exceed the annual carbon footprint of several small nations.

Experts point out that the military sector is often excluded from national climate targets. The US Department of Defense is one of the world's largest institutional consumers of petroleum, burning about 100 million barrels annually in peacetime.

Indian officials have not commented on the emissions data. The Ministry of External Affairs continues to monitor the situation in West Asia, which remains a key source of India's crude oil imports.

What to watch

As the conflict continues, the cumulative emissions are expected to rise sharply. Environmental groups are calling for greater transparency in military carbon accounting. The coming weeks may see renewed debate over the inclusion of defence emissions in global climate agreements.
