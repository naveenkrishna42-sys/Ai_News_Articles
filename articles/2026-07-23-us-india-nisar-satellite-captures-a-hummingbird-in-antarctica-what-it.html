US-India NISAR satellite captures a ‘hummingbird’ in Antarctica. What it reveals

📅 2026-07-23 📂 Science Original source ↗
Key points
  • NISAR satellite captured a hummingbird-shaped formation in East Antarctica.
  • The feature is a geological structure, not an actual bird.
  • NISAR is a joint US-India mission using radar to monitor Earth's surface.

Radar catches a 'hummingbird' in the ice

An image from the joint NASA-ISRO NISAR satellite has triggered curiosity across the internet. The radar picture shows a distinct hummingbird-like shape hidden in the vast white expanse of East Antarctica.

NASA's Jet Propulsion Laboratory (JPL) released the image this week. It was captured during the satellite's early operational phase.

Not a bird, but a geological clue

The 'hummingbird' is not a living creature. Scientists at JPL identified it as a natural geological formation. The shape emerges from the interplay of ice, rock, and shadow under radar illumination.

Radar waves penetrate the ice surface and bounce back differently depending on the material beneath. This creates a visual illusion that resembles a bird in mid-flight. The feature is several kilometres long.

NISAR also captured an optical-light image of the same area. The optical view shows no such bird; it appears as a routine patch of ice and rock. This confirms the hummingbird is a radar artefact, not a physical carving.

What NISAR brings to Earth science

NISAR stands for NASA-ISRO Synthetic Aperture Radar. It is a joint mission between the US space agency and the Indian Space Research Organisation. The satellite was launched to monitor changes in Earth's land and ice surfaces with unprecedented precision.

Its radar can see through clouds and darkness. This makes it ideal for studying polar regions, where weather and long nights limit optical observations. Scientists use NISAR data to track glacial movement, ice sheet melt, and changes in forests and farmland.

Why East Antarctica matters

East Antarctica holds the largest ice sheet on the planet. If it melts significantly, global sea levels could rise by tens of metres. Understanding its structure and behaviour is critical.

Radar images like the 'hummingbird' help researchers map bedrock beneath the ice. This data feeds into climate models that predict future sea-level rise. The formation itself may indicate a buried ridge or a change in ice flow.

ISRO officials have not yet commented on the image. NASA JPL continues to process and release NISAR data for the scientific community.

Expect more such striking images as NISAR scans the globe every 12 days. The 'hummingbird' is a reminder that even a routine radar sweep can reveal the unexpected.
