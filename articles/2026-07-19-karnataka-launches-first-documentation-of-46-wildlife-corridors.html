Karnataka launches first documentation of 46 wildlife corridors

📅 2026-07-19 📂 Bengaluru Original source ↗
Key points
  • First-ever documentation of 46 wildlife corridors in Karnataka launched.
  • Corridors span forests, connecting critical habitats for species like elephants and tigers.
  • Documentation aims to guide development and conservation efforts to reduce conflict.

Karnataka has taken a significant step in wildlife conservation with the launch of the first-ever documentation of 46 wildlife corridors across the state. The initiative, unveiled in Bengaluru, maps critical pathways that animals use to move between forest patches.

These corridors are vital for species like elephants, tigers, and leopards. Fragmentation of forests due to roads, railways, and human settlements often blocks these routes, leading to increased human-animal conflict. The documentation aims to address this by providing a clear blueprint for conservation and development planning.

Mapping the corridors

The 46 corridors identified span across Karnataka's forests, including the Western Ghats and the Malnad region. Officials from the state forest department collaborated with wildlife experts and NGOs to compile the data over several years.

Each corridor has been assessed for its ecological importance and the threats it faces. The documentation includes details on species that use the corridors, the extent of habitat fragmentation, and recommendations for mitigation measures. This is the first time such a comprehensive exercise has been undertaken in the state.

Why corridors matter

Wildlife corridors are essentially lifelines for animals. They allow genetic exchange between populations, which is crucial for long-term survival. Without them, isolated groups of animals can become inbred and vulnerable to disease or local extinction.

In Karnataka, human-elephant conflict has been a persistent problem, particularly in areas like Kodagu and Hassan. By identifying and protecting corridors, forest officials hope to reduce incidents of elephants straying into farmlands and villages. The documentation will also help in planning infrastructure projects like highways and railway lines to minimise disruption.

A tool for planners

The documentation is not just for conservationists. It is designed to be a practical tool for policymakers, urban planners, and developers. The state government has emphasised that any new project near a corridor will need to consider the data to avoid blocking animal movement.

For instance, areas where corridors intersect with roads could be equipped with underpasses or overpasses for animals. Similarly, new settlements or industrial zones can be planned away from these critical zones. The forest department will now push for legal notification of these corridors, giving them formal protection under wildlife laws.

Challenges ahead

While the documentation is a landmark, implementation will not be easy. Many corridors are already encroached upon by agricultural land or villages. Convincing local communities to relocate or change land use patterns is a delicate process. Officials have indicated that they will work with local bodies to find solutions that balance development with conservation.

Another challenge is funding. Maintaining corridors involves regular monitoring, fencing, and sometimes buying private land to widen them. The state is exploring partnerships with corporate social responsibility (CSR) funds and international conservation agencies.

What to watch next: The forest department is expected to submit a proposal to the state cabinet for formal notification of these corridors. If approved, it could set a precedent for other states grappling with similar human-animal conflict issues.
