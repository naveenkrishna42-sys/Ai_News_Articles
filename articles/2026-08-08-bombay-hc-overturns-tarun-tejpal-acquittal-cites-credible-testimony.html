Bombay HC overturns Tarun Tejpal acquittal, cites 'credible testimony'

📅 2026-08-08 📂 India Original source ↗
Key points
  • Bombay HC overturned sessions court's acquittal of Tarun Tejpal
  • Court dismissed 'perfect victim' myth, stressing varied trauma responses
  • Judgment noted abuse of dominance and apology emails as key evidence
  • Tejpal convicted of rape; sentencing to follow
  • Case dates back to 2013 incident at Goa hotel

High Court reverses acquittal

The Bombay High Court has set aside the trial court's acquittal of Tarun Tejpal, the former editor-in-chief of Tehelka magazine, in a rape case dating back to 2013. The verdict, delivered on Friday, marks a dramatic turn in a case that has stretched over a decade.

The high court bench found the sessions court's reasoning flawed. It said the lower court had looked for a 'perfect victim' and ignored the survivor's credible testimony. The judgment, running into hundreds of pages, meticulously dissected the trial court's findings.

'Perfect victim' myth debunked

Justice, the high court observed, does not demand that a victim behave in a stereotypical manner. Women deal with trauma in different ways, and the absence of immediate complaint or visible distress does not negate the veracity of their account.

The court stressed that the trial judge had erred by applying an unrealistic standard. It pointed out that the survivor's conduct after the incident, including her continued professional dealings, could not be held against her. The judgment underlined that disbelief based on preconceived notions undermines the justice system.

Abuse of dominance and key evidence

Central to the high court's reasoning was the power dynamic between Tejpal and the complainant, a junior colleague. The court noted his statement—"I'm your boss, that makes it simpler"—as evidence of abuse of authority. This, the bench said, was not just a remark but a reflection of the coercive environment.

Apology emails sent by Tejpal to the survivor after the incident were also scrutinised. The high court treated these as significant corroborative material, indicating consciousness of guilt. The trial court had dismissed them as inconsequential, a view the high court firmly rejected.

What the trial court got wrong

The sessions court had acquitted Tejpal in May 2021, giving him the benefit of the doubt. It had questioned the survivor's credibility, citing inconsistencies in her statements. The high court, however, found these inconsistencies minor and immaterial.

The bench also took exception to the trial court's approach of expecting the survivor to have physically resisted. It observed that in workplace sexual assault, fear and hierarchical pressure often paralyse the victim, making resistance impossible.

Case background and legal journey

The case stems from an alleged assault on a woman journalist inside a hotel elevator in Goa during a Tehelka conference in November 2013. Tejpal was arrested, granted bail, and later acquitted by the sessions court. The state government and the survivor challenged the acquittal in the high court.

Tejpal, who has consistently maintained his innocence, now faces the prospect of sentencing. The high court has convicted him under relevant sections of the Indian Penal Code. The quantum of punishment will be decided in a subsequent hearing.

Legal experts say this judgment could have wider implications for how courts assess sexual assault cases, particularly those involving workplace hierarchies. The emphasis on the 'perfect victim' myth is being seen as a progressive step.

Reactions and next steps

The survivor's legal team welcomed the verdict, calling it a victory for justice. Tejpal's lawyers have indicated they will approach the Supreme Court. The case is likely to be heard by the apex court, which will decide if the high court's reasoning holds.

For now, the Bombay High Court's order stands as a robust critique of trial court complacency. It serves as a reminder that judicial scrutiny must be anchored in the realities of power and trauma, not abstract notions of how a victim 'should' behave.

As the legal process moves to sentencing, all eyes will be on the punishment. The Supreme Court's response, if an appeal is filed, will be the next significant development in this long-running case.
