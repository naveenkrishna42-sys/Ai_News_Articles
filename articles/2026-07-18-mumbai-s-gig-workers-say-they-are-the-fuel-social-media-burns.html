Mumbai’s gig workers say they are the fuel social media burns

📅 2026-07-18 📂 Mumbai Original source ↗
Key points
  • Gig workers in Mumbai describe themselves as the unseen workforce behind social media platforms.
  • They handle content moderation, data labelling, and other backend tasks for global tech companies.
  • Workers report low pay, job insecurity, and lack of basic benefits despite high demand for their labour.

Mumbai's gig economy has a new voice — one that claims it is the engine room of the internet. Workers who label data, moderate content, and train algorithms for global social media platforms say they are the fuel that keeps the digital machine running. But they remain largely invisible.

In cramped offices and home setups across the city, thousands of young Indians spend hours reviewing posts, flagging hate speech, and tagging images. Their labour powers the very feeds that billions scroll through every day.

The hidden workforce behind the screen

Content moderators and data annotators form the backbone of artificial intelligence systems. Without their work, platforms cannot filter harmful content or train recommendation algorithms. Yet most users never see their faces or know their names.

Workers in Mumbai's gig hubs say they are treated as disposable. Contracts are short, pay is low, and mental health support is minimal. Many work on a piece-rate basis, earning as little as a few rupees per task.

Low pay, high pressure

One worker, speaking on condition of anonymity, said he reviews over a thousand posts a day. His job involves deciding what stays online and what gets removed. The pay barely covers rent and food.

"We see the worst of the internet — violence, abuse, hate — and we are expected to move on to the next post in seconds," he said. "There is no counselling, no break, no recognition."

Industry estimates suggest India has lakhs of gig workers in content moderation and data annotation roles. Most are employed through third-party vendors, not directly by the big tech companies. This shields the platforms from direct responsibility.

The demand is rising

As social media companies expand in India, the need for local language moderators has grown. Platforms now need workers who understand Hindi, Tamil, Marathi, and other regional languages to flag problematic content accurately.

But the supply of willing workers far exceeds demand. This keeps wages low and bargaining power weak. Many workers say they have no choice but to accept the terms.

What workers want

Gig workers in Mumbai are beginning to organise. Informal groups on messaging apps share tips on which companies pay on time and which ones exploit workers. Some have started demanding minimum wage guarantees and mental health support.

"We are not asking for charity," another worker said. "We just want to be treated as human beings, not as machines that never tire."

Labour rights experts say the gig economy model needs urgent regulation. Without it, workers will continue to bear the cost of keeping social media safe and profitable.

What happens next depends on whether policymakers recognise this hidden workforce. For now, Mumbai's gig workers remain the invisible fuel that social media burns — and the flame shows no sign of dimming.
