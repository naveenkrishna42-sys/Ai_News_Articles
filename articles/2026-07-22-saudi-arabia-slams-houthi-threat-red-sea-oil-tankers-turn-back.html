Saudi Arabia slams Houthi threat: Red Sea oil tankers turn back

📅 2026-07-22 📂 Top Stories Original source ↗
Key points
  • Houthi rebels have threatened to attack oil tankers using Saudi Arabian ports on the Red Sea.
  • Several tankers carrying Saudi crude for India and China have made U-turns in the Red Sea.
  • Saudi Arabia has condemned the blockade, and analysts suggest strategic patience may have limits.

Oil tankers turn back as Houthis target Saudi Red Sea ports

Several crude oil tankers bound for India and China have made abrupt U-turns in the Red Sea after the Houthi rebel group threatened to attack vessels using Saudi Arabian ports on the vital waterway. The development marks a significant escalation in regional tensions and has sent fresh jitters through global energy markets.

Reports from multiple news outlets confirm that tankers carrying Saudi crude altered course following the threats. The Houthis have specifically warned they will target shipping tankers that dock at Saudi ports on the Red Sea, a critical chokepoint for global oil trade.

Why the Red Sea matters for India and China

The Red Sea route is a major artery for crude shipments from the Middle East to Asian economies. India and China are among the largest importers of Saudi oil. Any sustained disruption along this route forces tankers to take the longer journey around the Cape of Good Hope, adding weeks to delivery times and increasing freight costs.

For India, which meets over 80% of its crude demand through imports, the threat is immediate. A prolonged blockade could tighten supply and push up retail fuel prices, feeding into domestic inflation. Chinese refiners, already navigating a sluggish economy, face similar risks.

Saudi Arabia hits back, warns of consequences

Saudi Arabia has strongly condemned the Houthi blockade, calling it a reckless act that threatens international maritime security. Riyadh has not yet announced specific military retaliation, but analysts point out that Saudi strategic patience may soon run out.

The Houthis have grown bolder in recent months, using drone and missile capabilities to target infrastructure inside Saudi Arabia. However, the threat to commercial shipping on the Red Sea is a red line for Riyadh, which depends heavily on oil exports through ports like Yanbu and Jeddah.

Global oil markets on edge

The immediate impact has been felt in oil futures, with prices edging up on fears of supply disruptions. The U-turn of tankers signals that shipping companies are taking the Houthi threat seriously, even if no actual attacks have been reported so far.

Insurance premiums for vessels transiting the Red Sea are likely to rise, adding to the cost of moving crude. If the standoff continues, the world could see a repeat of the 2023-2024 Red Sea crisis, when Houthi attacks on commercial shipping forced a massive rerouting of global trade.

What happens next

For now, the situation remains fluid. Saudi Arabia is expected to step up naval patrols and may seek international support to secure the waterway. The Houthis, emboldened by their ability to disrupt trade, are unlikely to back down without a significant show of force. The coming days will reveal whether Riyadh's patience has, indeed, reached its limit.
