Unicef Report Highlights How Climate Change Hits Children Hardest

📅 2026-07-19 📂 Environment Original source ↗
Key points
  • Children face higher risks from extreme weather, air pollution, and water scarcity than adults.
  • Climate change threatens children's education, nutrition, and mental health globally.
  • Unicef calls for urgent policy action to protect children from environmental harms.

A new report from Unicef places children at the center of the climate crisis. Titled "In Focus: Children, climate change and environmental degradation," the document argues that younger generations are bearing the brunt of a problem they did little to create.

Children are not just small adults. Their bodies and developing immune systems make them more vulnerable to heatwaves, floods, droughts, and toxic air. The report outlines how environmental degradation is already reshaping childhood across the globe.

Health at Risk from a Warming Planet

Extreme heat can cause heatstroke and dehydration in children faster than in adults. Air pollution, linked to fossil fuel burning, leads to asthma, pneumonia, and low birth weight. Unicef notes that nearly 1 in 3 children—about 700 million—live in countries with high or extremely high water scarcity.

Contaminated water and poor sanitation are among the leading causes of death for children under five. Diarrhoeal diseases, often preventable, still claim lives because families lack access to clean water.

Education Disrupted by Disasters

Climate-related disasters force school closures and destroy infrastructure. When a cyclone hits or a river floods, children miss weeks of learning. Some never return to school. The report highlights that girls are especially affected—they may face early marriage or increased domestic work after a disaster.

Unicef warns that the long-term economic impact will be severe. A child who misses school due to climate disruption is less likely to escape poverty later.

Mental Health and Migration

Witnessing the destruction of one's home or community takes a psychological toll. The report finds rising rates of anxiety, depression, and post-traumatic stress among children in climate-vulnerable regions. Many feel helpless or betrayed by the adults who failed to act.

Environmental degradation also drives migration. Families uprooted by desertification or sea-level rise often settle in crowded, unsafe urban areas. Children in these settings face exploitation, trafficking, and loss of their cultural roots.

What Can Be Done

Unicef calls for integrating child-specific measures into climate policies. This means building climate-resilient schools, training health workers to treat heat-related illnesses, and designing early warning systems that reach children.

The agency also urges governments to phase out fossil fuels and invest in renewable energy. The benefits, it argues, will be felt most by the youngest citizens.

The report does not provide specific funding pledges or timelines. But it makes clear that the window for meaningful action is narrowing. Children today will inherit a planet that is hotter, dirtier, and less stable than the one their parents knew.

Unicef plans to release a series of follow-up briefs focusing on regional impacts. The global community will watch to see if commitments made at upcoming climate talks translate into real protection for children.
