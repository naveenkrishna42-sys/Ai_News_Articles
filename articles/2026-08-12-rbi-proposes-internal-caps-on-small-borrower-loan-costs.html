RBI proposes internal caps on small borrower loan costs

📅 2026-08-12 📂 Banking Original source ↗
Key points
  • RBI has proposed internal ceilings on interest rates for small-value loans to prevent usury.
  • The move targets lenders' pricing practices for vulnerable small borrowers.
  • Final guidelines will follow stakeholder consultation and feedback.

Reserve Bank moves to rein in lending rates for small loans

The Reserve Bank of India (RBI) has floated a proposal to set internal ceilings on the interest rates that lenders can charge on small-value loans. The draft framework, released for public comment, is aimed at protecting borrowers who take out modest sums from being saddled with steep borrowing costs.

The central bank's discussion paper suggests that regulated entities—including banks, non-banking financial companies (NBFCs), and microfinance institutions—should adopt board-approved policies that cap the total cost of credit for small loans. This includes not just the headline interest rate but also fees and other charges that can inflate the effective burden on the borrower.

Why the central bank is stepping in

Small-ticket loans, often taken by low-income households for consumption or emergency needs, have grown rapidly in recent years. With the rise of digital lending apps and fintech partnerships, some borrowers have found themselves paying annualised rates that run into triple digits once processing fees and penal charges are added.

The RBI's move is designed to curb such practices. By mandating an internal ceiling—set by each lender's board within a prescribed band—the regulator hopes to bring transparency and fairness to pricing without fixing a uniform external cap that could stifle credit flow.

What the proposal entails

Under the draft, lenders would be required to review their pricing models at least once a year. The ceiling would apply to all loans below a certain ticket size, which the RBI has not yet specified in the discussion paper. The central bank has invited feedback from stakeholders, including lenders, consumer groups, and the general public, before finalising the rules.

Officials have not yet confirmed the exact threshold or the band within which the internal ceiling must fall. The discussion paper, however, emphasises that the intent is not to impose a uniform rate across the industry but to ensure that no borrower is charged an exploitative rate.

Industry response and concerns

Lenders have had mixed reactions. While many large banks already have pricing guardrails, smaller NBFCs and fintechs worry that rigid caps could squeeze their margins, especially in the unsecured lending segment where risk is higher. Industry bodies are likely to seek flexibility in the final guidelines, arguing that risk-based pricing is essential for financial inclusion.

Consumer advocates, on the other hand, have welcomed the proposal, pointing to numerous cases of borrowers being trapped in debt cycles due to opaque charges. They have urged the RBI to also standardise the manner in which annual percentage rates are disclosed.

Road ahead

The RBI will finalise the framework after considering the feedback received. The timeline for implementation is not yet known, but market watchers expect the regulator to roll out the guidelines in phases, starting with the smallest loans.

All eyes will be on how the central bank balances consumer protection with the need to keep credit accessible. The coming months will show whether these internal ceilings become a template for broader regulation of lending costs in India.
