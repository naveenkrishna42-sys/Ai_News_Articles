FIFA faces rebellion as top confederations demand Infantino's exit

📅 2026-08-10 📂 Sports Original source ↗
Key points
  • UEFA, AFC and CONCACAF have issued an open letter accusing FIFA of breaking trust 'through deception'.
  • The confederations are pushing for Infantino to quit and have discussed rival competitions.
  • LaLiga chief Javier Tebas has publicly declared that the 'Infantino era is over'.

Football's global governing body is facing its most serious crisis in years after three of its six continental confederations jointly accused president Gianni Infantino of breaking trust "through deception". In an unprecedented open letter to the football family, UEFA, the Asian Football Confederation (AFC) and CONCACAF have demanded accountability over a controversial plan to privatise the World Cup.

The letter, published across the confederations' official channels, marks a rare public rupture in the sport's diplomatic ranks. It alleges that FIFA's leadership has misled member associations about the true nature of a proposal that would hand control of the men's World Cup to private investors. The confederations say they were not consulted in good faith, and that the plan threatens the sport's governance structure.

An open revolt against Infantino

The joint statement is being read as a direct challenge to Infantino's authority, which has rarely been questioned so openly since he took charge of FIFA in 2016. The three confederations, representing more than 200 national associations across Europe, Asia, North and Central America, have collectively called for a change in leadership at the top of world football.

Sources indicate that discussions have already taken place among these confederations about the possibility of organising rival competitions if FIFA does not back down. While no formal proposals have been made public, the very existence of such talks signals a breakdown in trust that goes far beyond policy disagreements.

Tebas: 'The Infantino era is over'

The rebellion has found an influential ally in LaLiga president Javier Tebas, who has been a long-time critic of Infantino. In a sharply worded statement, Tebas declared that "the Infantino era is over", urging FIFA's member associations to rally behind a new direction. His comments add weight to the growing perception that the Swiss-Italian administrator has lost the room.

Tebas's intervention is significant because LaLiga is one of the most powerful domestic leagues in the world. His call for change at FIFA is not merely symbolic; it reflects a broader unease among Europe's top clubs and leagues about the direction of the global game under Infantino's stewardship.

What is the privatisation plan?

At the heart of the dispute is a proposal that would fundamentally alter how the World Cup is financed and operated. Under the plan, FIFA would sell a stake in the tournament's commercial rights to private investors, potentially including sovereign wealth funds and global investment groups. Proponents argue this would maximise revenues and secure long-term financial stability.

But critics say privatisation would strip the World Cup of its public-interest character and concentrate power in the hands of a few wealthy entities. The confederations' letter argues that such a move was pushed through without proper consultation, and that FIFA's leadership has deliberately obscured the details to avoid opposition. The word "deception" has landed with particular force in football circles, where governance standards are already under intense scrutiny.

A fractured football family

The open letter is a stark reminder that FIFA's authority depends on the consent of its member confederations. When three of them band together publicly, the organisation's internal fault lines become impossible to ignore. The African (CAF) and South American (CONMEBOL) confederations have so far remained silent, leaving the balance of power uncertain.

FIFA has not yet issued a formal response to the letter, and officials have not confirmed whether Infantino intends to address the allegations directly. The coming weeks will be crucial: if more confederations join the revolt, Infantino's position could become untenable. If they do not, he may still weather the storm, but the damage to his credibility is likely to be lasting.

What happens next depends on whether FIFA's leadership chooses to engage with the confederations' demands or retreat behind institutional walls. The football world will be watching closely to see if this open letter becomes the catalyst for a genuine overhaul—or just another chapter in the sport's long history of governance battles. For now, the ball is firmly in Infantino's court.
