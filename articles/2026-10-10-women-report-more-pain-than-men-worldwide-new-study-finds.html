Women report more pain than men worldwide, new study finds

✍️ By TIVRA News Science & Health Desk 📅 2026-10-10 📂 Health Original source ↗
Key points
  • A global study of 6.1 million people found women consistently report more pain than men across the body and ages.
  • The gender gap affects diagnosis and treatment—buyers of care (patients, families, clinicians) should use structured symptom tracking.
  • Understanding pain patterns by age and sex can improve screening, referral, and personalized management plans.

What the new global study shows about gender and pain

A large international study examining pain across the lifespan has found a consistent gender gap: women report more pain than men. The analysis drew on data from 6.1 million individuals across 118 countries, using reference curves to map how pain changes with age.

Rather than focusing on one condition, the study looks at pain as a whole—capturing how often people experience pain and how that experience accumulates across the body over time. For clinicians and patients alike, that matters because “pain” is often treated as a single symptom when it may reflect multiple bodily and social factors.

Why the difference matters in real-world healthcare decisions

If women are more likely to report pain, it can influence everything from early screening to the speed of diagnosis and the intensity of treatment. In practice, people who describe pain frequently may be perceived as having “higher pain tolerance issues,” even when the pattern reflects a genuine difference in prevalence, severity, or persistence.

For patients, the most actionable takeaway is to document pain in a way clinicians can use: location, intensity, duration, triggers, and functional impact (sleep, walking, work, caregiving). Structured tracking helps separate “how much pain” from “how pain changes daily life,” which can change treatment choices.

For healthcare systems, gender-stratified reference patterns can support better triage. If baseline expectations differ by sex and age, screening thresholds and follow-up intervals should reflect that—reducing the risk of under-recognition in one group or over-medicalization in another.

Benchmarks you can use: turning “pain reports” into clinical signal

The study’s scale strengthens its credibility, but the practical value comes from how you translate it into care. Pain reporting is subjective, so the key is to capture enough detail for clinicians to assess severity and trajectory rather than relying on a single number.

Here are practical benchmarks commonly used in pain care that you can adapt when discussing symptoms with a doctor or physiotherapist. These are not study outputs, but they help convert global findings into day-to-day decisions.

Feature / MetricDetails
Pain intensityUse a 0–10 scale; record before and after activity, and at least once per day for 7–14 days.
Pain durationTrack onset date and whether pain is episodic or continuous (e.g., most days for ≥3 months).
Distribution (whole-body vs focal)List pain sites separately (neck, back, joints, headache, abdominal pain), not just “body pain.”
Functional impactMeasure changes in sleep hours, walking distance, lifting ability, work attendance, and household tasks.
Aggravating/relieving factorsNote activity type, stress, posture, weather sensitivity, and what reliably reduces pain.
Treatment responseDocument benefit and side effects after physiotherapy, exercise, medications, or other interventions.

What could be driving the gap (and what buyers of care should demand)

Gender differences in pain reports can arise from a mix of biological susceptibility, hormonal changes across the lifespan, differences in symptom perception and reporting, and variations in how healthcare professionals respond to pain narratives. None of these factors should be treated as excuses; instead, they should guide more consistent assessment and better communication.

From a procurement perspective—whether you’re selecting a pain clinic, physiotherapy program, or care pathway—look for services that use standardized assessment tools and provide follow-up based on function, not only symptom intensity. In other words: the goal should be measurable improvements in mobility, sleep, and daily capability.

Ask what the care team does when pain persists. That includes reassessment intervals, referral triggers (e.g., for imaging or specialist review when red flags are present), and non-drug options such as graded exercise, cognitive behavioral strategies, or multidisciplinary pain management where appropriate.

Key real-world highlights for patients and clinicians

  • Consistent pattern, large dataset: Findings are based on 6.1 million participants across 118 countries, reducing the chance that the “gender gap” is a local artifact.
  • Whole-body framing: The study emphasizes pain across the body, which aligns with how many people experience chronic and overlapping pain conditions.
  • Better tracking improves treatment: Structured symptom logs can help clinicians judge severity and trajectory more reliably than one-off descriptions.
  • Care systems need sex- and age-aware screening: Reference curves can support more equitable follow-up and earlier interventions when pain escalates.
  • Demand functional outcomes: The most useful targets are improvements in sleep, mobility, and work or caregiving capacity—not just reductions in reported pain.

Pros and cons of how this finding may be used

  • Pros: Encourages sex-aware screening and could reduce delays by validating that higher pain reporting in women is a measurable, not dismissible, pattern.
  • Pros: Supports more standardized pain assessment tools across settings.
  • Cons: Risk of stereotyping—clinicians must still evaluate each individual’s story, comorbidities, and red flags.
  • Cons: Reporting differences may not equal clinical severity in every case; pain outcomes must still be confirmed with function and treatment response.

Bottom line for buyers of healthcare

If you’re choosing where to get care—or how to prepare for a consultation—use this study as a prompt to request structured assessment and follow-up. Women’s higher pain reporting should translate into better, faster evaluation and a plan that tracks function over time.

For clinicians, the next step is to pair global evidence with individualized evaluation: confirm severity, map distribution, rule out red flags, and use measurable goals so treatment decisions are anchored to outcomes people can feel and measure.
