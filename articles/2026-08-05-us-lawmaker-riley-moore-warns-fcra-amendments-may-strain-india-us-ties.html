US lawmaker Riley Moore warns FCRA amendments may strain India-US ties

📅 2026-08-05 📂 India Original source ↗
Key points
  • Riley Moore, a US Congressman, has voiced concerns over India's FCRA amendments.
  • He warned that the changes could adversely affect India-US bilateral ties.
  • The remarks have sparked debate, with some Indian voices dismissing the criticism.

Congressman's warning

US Congressman Riley Moore has raised concerns over India's proposed amendments to the Foreign Contribution (Regulation) Act, or FCRA, warning that the changes could damage the bilateral relationship between Washington and New Delhi. His remarks, reported by multiple Indian outlets, have added a new layer of friction to an otherwise expanding strategic partnership.

Moore, a Republican from West Virginia, did not specify which provisions of the amendment he found problematic, but his statement signals unease in certain US political circles about the direction of India's regulatory environment for foreign funding.

What the FCRA amendments entail

The FCRA, which regulates foreign contributions to Indian entities, has been amended several times in recent years. The latest proposed changes are seen by the Indian government as a measure to ensure transparency and prevent foreign interference in domestic affairs. However, critics argue that the amendments could stifle civil society and non-governmental organisations that rely on international funding.

Moore's warning comes at a time when India-US ties are otherwise on an upward trajectory, with increased cooperation in defence, technology, and trade. The Congressman's concerns, if echoed by others in Washington, could complicate the narrative of a seamless partnership.

Reactions in India

Moore's comments have not gone uncontested. Mohandas Pai, a prominent Indian venture capitalist and public policy commentator, hit back at the Congressman, saying that the US itself has similar provisions regulating foreign influence. 'US too has similar provisions, stop running fake narratives,' Pai said, dismissing the criticism as misguided.

This exchange highlights a growing tendency in Indian public discourse to challenge foreign critiques of domestic legislation. Pai's response also reflects a broader sentiment among some Indian stakeholders who view such warnings as attempts to undermine India's sovereign decision-making.

Context and implications

Moore is not a widely known figure in India-US relations, but his position as a US Congressman gives his words weight in diplomatic circles. His warning, while not an official US government stance, could influence perceptions among lawmakers and policy experts in Washington.

The FCRA has been a point of contention before. In 2020, the Indian government tightened the act, requiring organisations to declare foreign contributions and restricting their use. Those changes drew criticism from human rights groups and foreign governments, though the Indian government maintained they were necessary for national security and transparency.

The latest amendments, still under consideration, are likely to face similar scrutiny. Moore's intervention suggests that the issue has caught the attention of US lawmakers, who may push for assurances from New Delhi that the changes will not target legitimate international cooperation.

What to watch

The coming months will reveal whether Moore's concerns translate into formal diplomatic démarches or remain a solitary voice. India's response, both in its legislative process and in its messaging to Washington, will be crucial in determining whether this becomes a lasting irritant or a passing storm in an otherwise robust relationship.
