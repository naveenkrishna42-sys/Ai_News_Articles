Married Man in Live-in With Adult Woman Not a Crime: Allahabad HC

📅 2026-07-30 📂 Crime & Law Original source ↗
Key points
  • The court quashed an FIR against a married man accused of cheating for a live-in relationship.
  • The bench held that a consensual relationship between consenting adults is not a criminal act.
  • The ruling distinguishes between legal marital obligations and criminal liability under IPC.
  • The judgment cites Supreme Court precedents on the limits of Section 498A IPC.

Lucknow: The Allahabad High Court has ruled that a married man entering into a live-in relationship with an adult woman does not amount to a criminal offence under Indian law. The bench of Justice Rajesh Singh Chauhan quashed an FIR lodged against a man accused of cheating and criminal breach of trust by his live-in partner.

The court observed that if a woman of sound mind willingly chooses to live with a married man, the relationship, whether moral or not, cannot be tried in a criminal court. The ruling reinforces the principle that not every act of moral turpitude invites penal action.

The Case and the FIR

The petitioner had been living with the woman, an adult, for several years. When the relationship soured, she lodged a complaint alleging that the man had concealed his marital status and tricked her into the arrangement. She sought action under Sections 420 (cheating), 406 (criminal breach of trust) and 506 (criminal intimidation) of the Indian Penal Code.

The man argued that the woman was fully aware of his marital status from the beginning. He maintained that the relationship was consensual and that no false promise had been made. The FIR, he contended, was a misuse of criminal law to settle a personal dispute.

What the Court Held

Justice Chauhan noted that the woman was a major when she entered the relationship. She had lived with the man for years without complaint. The court said that even if the man was married, the woman’s informed consent made the relationship a private matter, not a criminal one.

“Mere fact that a married man is having a live-in relationship with an adult woman does not constitute any offence, much less an offence of cheating,” the bench stated. It added that criminal law cannot be used to police the private lives of consenting adults.

The court also drew a distinction between the matrimonial offence of adultery, which is a civil wrong, and criminal fraud. Since the woman had not been coerced or deceived into the relationship, no case of cheating was made out.

Supreme Court Precedents Cited

The judgment relied on earlier Supreme Court rulings that limit the scope of Section 498A IPC (cruelty by husband or his relatives) and Section 420 IPC. The apex court has repeatedly held that broken promises in relationships, without evidence of initial fraudulent intent, do not amount to criminal cheating.

The High Court also referred to the landmark verdict that decriminalised adultery in 2018, observing that the state should not intrude into the personal domain of adults unless public order or a specific law is violated.

Implications of the Judgment

Legal experts say the ruling provides clarity in a grey area. Live-in relationships are still not legally recognised as equivalent to marriage in India, but the courts have consistently held that adults have the right to cohabit. This judgment reinforces that a married person’s live-in arrangement, while potentially a breach of marital vows, is not automatically a criminal act.

The court cautioned that the ruling does not condone deception. If a man actively conceals his marriage and induces a woman to live with him on the false promise of marriage, that could still attract criminal charges. But in this case, the woman’s long-term, voluntary participation negated the claim of fraud.

  • Consent of an adult woman is the key factor that removes a live-in relationship from the realm of criminal law.
  • The judgment may discourage the filing of FIRs in purely personal relationship disputes.
  • The ruling does not affect existing laws on bigamy or cruelty under the Hindu Marriage Act or other personal laws.

The Allahabad High Court’s order is likely to be cited in similar cases across the country. It draws a bright line between what is immoral, and what is illegal — a distinction that many litigants and lower courts sometimes blur.
