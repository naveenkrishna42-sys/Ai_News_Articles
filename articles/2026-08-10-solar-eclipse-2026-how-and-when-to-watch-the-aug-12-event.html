Solar Eclipse 2026: How and When to Watch the Aug. 12 Event

📅 2026-08-10 📂 Science Original source ↗
Key points
  • Total solar eclipse on Aug. 12, 2026, with totality visible along a narrow path.
  • UK high street stocks of eclipse glasses running short ahead of the event.
  • Partial eclipse visible across England; best viewing spots and times vary.
  • Safety advice: never view the sun directly without certified eclipse glasses.

A Rare Celestial Event Arrives

Skywatchers across the globe are gearing up for a total solar eclipse on August 12, 2026. The event, which occurs when the Moon passes directly between the Earth and the Sun, will plunge parts of the world into temporary darkness.

For those in the path of totality, the experience promises an otherworldly few minutes. But even outside that narrow corridor, a partial eclipse will be visible across large swathes of Europe, including England.

When and Where to Watch

The eclipse's timing depends heavily on your location. In the UK, the partial phase will begin in the late morning and peak around midday, with the Sun significantly obscured for viewers in the south and east. The best places to see the partial eclipse across England are generally those with clear, unobstructed views of the southern horizon.

For the full spectacle, eclipse chasers are heading to the path of totality, which cuts through parts of Spain and Iceland. There, the Sun will be completely hidden for over two minutes. Weather, of course, remains the wildcard—cloud cover could spoil the view for many.

Glasses Shortage Sparks Rush

As the date nears, a familiar problem has emerged: a rush to find solar eclipse glasses. High street retailers in the UK are reporting stocks running short, with many shops sold out entirely. Online sellers are also facing delays, leaving some enthusiasts scrambling.

Experts stress that ordinary sunglasses are not safe for viewing the eclipse. Only certified eclipse glasses with ISO 12312-2 filters should be used. Without them, looking at the Sun—even during a partial phase—can cause permanent eye damage.

What to Expect in the UK

For most of England, the eclipse will be a partial one. In London, observers can expect around 30% of the Sun to be covered at maximum. Further north, in cities like Manchester and Leeds, the coverage will be slightly less, but still noticeable.

The partial eclipse will last for roughly two hours from start to finish. During the peak, daylight will dim subtly, though not to the eerie darkness seen in totality. It's a reminder that celestial mechanics are at work, even if the effect is understated.

Safety First, Always

If you haven't secured glasses yet, don't panic. Pinhole projectors and solar viewing filters for telescopes are alternative options. The key is to avoid direct viewing at all costs.

Officials have not confirmed any special traffic or public event arrangements, but many astronomy clubs are hosting viewing parties. Check local listings for organised events where safe viewing equipment will be available.

As the clock ticks down to August 12, the message from astronomers is simple: plan ahead, find a clear spot, and protect your eyes. The next total solar eclipse visible from the UK isn't expected for decades, so this is a rare chance to look up and wonder.

What to watch: Live streams from the path of totality will be available online, and weather forecasts will sharpen in the final 24 hours. For now, keep your glasses close and your eyes on the sky.
