UPI Charges Above ₹2,000: Supreme Court Plea, MDR Explained

Key points
  • A Supreme Court plea challenges the Centre's decision to levy MDR on merchant UPI transactions above ₹2,000, raising questions of affordability and digital payment parity.

  • Niti Aayog and the Centre defend the move as a necessary step to sustain UPI infrastructure, with the government denying any US pressure behind the decision.

The UPI MDR Debate Reaches the Supreme Court

A plea filed in the Supreme Court has challenged the Centre's decision to introduce a Merchant Discount Rate (MDR) on UPI transactions above ₹2,000. The petition argues that levying charges on a payment system built and promoted as a public digital good undermines financial inclusion, particularly for small businesses that adopted UPI on the promise of zero-cost acceptance.

The government has countered that MDR is India's own policy decision, not a response to external pressure, and that a sustainable revenue model is needed to maintain UPI's scale. With over 10 billion monthly transactions, the infrastructure cost argument has gained traction in policy circles.

What Exactly Is MDR and Who Pays It?

MDR is a fee deducted by banks and payment service providers from the transaction value before settling the amount to the merchant. Crucially, the end consumer pays nothing extra. For a ₹2,500 UPI payment, the merchant receives ₹2,500 minus the applicable MDR, which typically ranges between 0.25% and 1.5% depending on the payment instrument and category.

The confusion among consumers is understandable. Many assume they will be charged for using UPI above ₹2,000. That is incorrect. The charge applies to merchants accepting payments, not to individuals sending money to friends, family, or businesses via their own bank accounts.

Why Merchants and Petroleum Dealers Are Pushing Back

Petroleum dealers have opposed MDR on fuel transactions, seeking a categorical exemption. Fuel retail operates on razor-thin margins, often under 1%, and adding even a 0.25% MDR on high-value fuel purchases can wipe out profitability on a per-transaction basis. A ₹5,000 fuel fill would attract roughly ₹12.50 in MDR, which the dealer absorbs.

Small kirana stores, food vendors, and service providers share similar concerns. For a business processing ₹5 lakh in monthly UPI collections, a 0.5% MDR translates to ₹2,500 per month, or ₹30,000 annually. That is a meaningful cost for micro-enterprises operating without formal accounting systems.

The Case For MDR: Infrastructure Has a Cost

Niti Aayog's Vice Chairman has defended the move with a blunt 'bees and honey' analogy: the government cannot subsidise everything indefinitely. Banks and payment aggregators currently bear the cost of processing UPI transactions, including server infrastructure, fraud detection, dispute resolution, and compliance. As volumes scale, so do these costs.

Without a revenue stream, banks have little incentive to invest in reliability and security. MDR on higher-value merchant transactions creates a funding mechanism while keeping person-to-person transfers free. The design attempts to balance inclusion with sustainability.

Key Real-World Implications

  • Consumers remain unaffected: Individuals sending money via UPI pay no MDR, regardless of amount.
  • Merchants face a new cost line: Businesses above the ₹2,000 threshold must factor MDR into pricing or absorb it.
  • Digital adoption risk: Small merchants may revert to cash for high-value transactions to avoid fees.
  • Fuel sector exemption demand: Petroleum dealers argue fuel retail margins cannot absorb MDR.
  • Legal uncertainty: The Supreme Court plea could delay or reshape implementation.
  • Policy signalling: The government insists this is a sovereign decision, not foreign pressure.

What Buyers and Businesses Should Do Now

Merchants should audit their monthly UPI collections to estimate MDR exposure above the ₹2,000 threshold. Those with high average ticket sizes, such as electronics retailers, jewellers, and fuel stations, face the greatest impact. Negotiating MDR rates with payment aggregators and exploring category-specific exemptions is advisable.

Consumers should ignore misinformation about being charged for using UPI. The charge remains a merchant-side cost. However, some businesses may pass on the cost through higher prices or minimum transaction values. Tracking those shifts matters for household budgets.

The Supreme Court plea introduces legal uncertainty, and implementation timelines may shift. Businesses should prepare contingency budgets while awaiting clarity. The core question remains whether India's digital payment success can sustain itself without some form of merchant-side revenue.
