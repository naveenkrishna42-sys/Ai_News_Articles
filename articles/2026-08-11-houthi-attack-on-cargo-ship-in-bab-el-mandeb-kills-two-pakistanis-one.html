Houthi attack on cargo ship in Bab el-Mandeb kills two Pakistanis, one Indonesian

📅 2026-08-11 📂 World Original source ↗
Key points
  • Two Pakistani and one Indonesian national killed in Houthi attack on cargo ship in Bab el-Mandeb.
  • Yemeni government confirms six deaths in the strike, which targeted a vessel the Houthis claim was Saudi and carrying military equipment.
  • This is the first deadly Houthi attack since the start of the Iran war, raising fears for Red Sea shipping lanes.
  • Houthi-run media says the ship was Saudi-owned; Saudi Arabia has not yet officially responded.

Two Pakistani nationals and one Indonesian were killed when Yemen's Houthi rebels struck a cargo ship in the Bab el-Mandeb strait, according to reports. The attack, which took place on Tuesday, left six people dead in total, the Yemeni government said. The vessel was sailing through the narrow waterway that separates the Arabian Peninsula from Africa, a critical chokepoint for global shipping.

The Houthi-run news agency claimed the ship was Saudi-owned and carrying military equipment. The rebels have not provided evidence for the claim, and Saudi authorities have not yet issued an official statement. The Times of India reported the deaths of the two Pakistanis and the Indonesian, citing initial details from the region.

Deadly escalation in a tense waterway

Tuesday's strike marks the first deadly Houthi attack on a commercial vessel since the start of the Iran war, according to the BBC. The conflict, which began earlier this year, has drawn in regional powers and heightened the risk of maritime attacks in the Red Sea and the Gulf of Aden.

The Bab el-Mandeb strait is one of the world's busiest shipping lanes, carrying millions of barrels of oil and vast amounts of cargo daily. Any disruption here sends ripples through global markets. The Yemeni government, which is backed by a Saudi-led coalition, condemned the attack but offered few details on the vessel's name or flag.

Saudi link and unanswered questions

Reuters, citing the Houthi-run news agency, reported that the rebels targeted a Saudi ship in the strait. The claim, if true, would mark a direct provocation against Riyadh, which has been involved in a years-long war in Yemen. But whether the ship was actually Saudi-owned or merely chartered remains unclear.

Officials have not confirmed the vessel's cargo, crew size, or the nationalities of the other three victims. The six dead include the two Pakistanis, the Indonesian, and three others whose identities have not been released. Local maritime authorities are likely to investigate, though access to the area is difficult due to ongoing hostilities.

Regional fallout and shipping risks

This attack underscores how the Iran war has inflamed existing conflicts. The Houthis, who are aligned with Iran, have stepped up their naval operations since the war began. Shipping firms have already rerouted some vessels around the Cape of Good Hope, adding weeks to voyages and raising costs.

Pakistan and Indonesia have yet to issue formal statements, but both countries have significant diaspora populations working in the shipping and maritime sectors. Their governments will likely demand answers from Saudi Arabia and the Yemeni government, though diplomatic channels remain strained.

The Houthis have a history of targeting vessels they claim are linked to Saudi Arabia or its allies. In past incidents, they have used anti-ship missiles and drones, forcing crews to abandon ship. Tuesday's deaths signal a more lethal phase in their campaign.

What to watch

All eyes will now be on Saudi Arabia's response. A formal confirmation of the ship's ownership could escalate the conflict further, while international navies patrolling the region may tighten their rules of engagement. The Houthis have promised more attacks, and shipping insurers are likely to raise premiums for vessels transiting the strait.

For the families of the dead, the wait for answers has just begun. The coming days will reveal whether this attack was a one-off or the start of a broader campaign to choke off trade through the Red Sea.
