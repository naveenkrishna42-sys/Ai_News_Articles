End of UGC, two board exams, global campuses: India's 2025 education shake-up

📅 2026-07-18 📂 Education Original source ↗
Key points
  • University Grants Commission (UGC) abolished, replaced by a new regulatory body
  • Students can now take board exams twice a year, retaining best score
  • Several foreign universities opened campuses in India under new policy
  • National Curriculum Framework rolled out in schools, focusing on experiential learning

It was a year of sweeping change for India's education system. In 2025, the government implemented some of the most ambitious reforms in decades, reshaping how students learn, how colleges are run, and how global institutions operate on Indian soil.

From the abolition of the University Grants Commission (UGC) to allowing students to take board exams twice, the changes touched every level of education. Here's a look at what transformed.

UGC abolished, new regulator takes over

The biggest structural shift was the dismantling of the University Grants Commission, the nearly 70-year-old body that oversaw higher education. In its place came the Higher Education Commission of India (HECI), a leaner regulator with a broader mandate.

HECI will focus on quality assurance, accreditation, and academic standards. The move, announced earlier in the year, was part of the National Education Policy 2020's roadmap to separate regulation from funding and grant-making.

Officials said the new body would reduce bureaucratic hurdles and allow universities more autonomy. Critics, however, flagged concerns about how quickly the transition was happening and whether state universities would adapt smoothly.

Two board exams a year become the norm

School students got a major flexibility boost. From the 2025 academic session, the Central Board of Secondary Education (CBSE) and several state boards allowed students to appear for Class X and XII board exams twice a year.

Students can now take the first exam in February-March and a second in May-June. The better of the two scores will be considered for final results. The aim, the board said, was to reduce exam stress and give students a genuine second chance.

Early data showed that nearly 30% of students availed the second opportunity, with many improving their scores. Schools reported that the option helped ease anxiety, though some parents expressed concern about the extended academic calendar.

Foreign campuses open doors in India

Another landmark shift was the arrival of global universities. Under the International Financial Services Centres Authority (IFSCA) and the University Grants Commission (Foreign Universities) Regulations, several top foreign institutions set up campuses in India.

Oxford University, the University of Cambridge, and New York University were among those that announced campuses in Gujarat's GIFT City and other locations. The campuses will offer degrees equivalent to those at their home institutions, with tuition fees capped and a portion of seats reserved for Indian students.

The government said this would stem the outflow of students going abroad and boost research collaboration. However, questions remain about affordability and whether the model can be replicated outside special economic zones.

New curriculum framework kicks in

In schools, the National Curriculum Framework (NCF) rolled out in full force in 2025. The framework, aligned with NEP 2020, emphasises experiential learning, vocational skills, and multidisciplinary studies.

Students now study fewer subjects but in greater depth, with a focus on critical thinking over rote memorisation. Arts, sports, and vocational education are treated on par with academic subjects. Assessments also changed, with more internal evaluation and less weightage on final exams.

Implementation has been uneven. While private schools adapted quickly, many government schools struggled with teacher training and resource shortages. State education ministers have asked for more central funding to support the rollout.

What to watch next: The 2026 academic year will test how well the new regulator handles disputes, whether foreign campuses can sustain enrolments, and if the dual-exam model reduces stress or simply extends the grind. The government is expected to release a review of these reforms by mid-2026.
