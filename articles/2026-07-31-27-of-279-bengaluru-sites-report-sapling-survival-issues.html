27 of 279 Bengaluru sites report sapling survival issues

📅 2026-07-31 📂 Bengaluru Original source ↗
Key points
  • 27 out of 279 plantation sites in Bengaluru have reported sapling survival issues.
  • Officials are yet to confirm the exact causes or the scale of damage.
  • The development raises questions about the city's green drive and maintenance efforts.

Bengaluru's ambitious tree-planting drive has hit a rough patch. A recent review of 279 plantation sites across the city has found that saplings are in trouble at 27 of them. The revelation comes as a reality check for the city's green ambitions, which have been a cornerstone of its environmental agenda.

The affected sites, spread across various wards, are now under scrutiny. Officials have not yet confirmed the exact reasons for the saplings' distress. Water scarcity, poor soil quality, and lack of timely care are among the likely factors, but nothing has been officially stated.

What the numbers say

Of the 279 sites surveyed, 27 show signs of saplings struggling to survive. That is roughly one in every ten sites. While the majority of sites appear healthy, the troubled ones are a cause for concern.

The Bangalore Mirror report, which first brought this to light, did not specify which areas are affected. City officials have been tight-lipped about the details, leaving residents and environmentalists guessing.

Maintenance is the missing piece

Planting saplings is only half the battle. Ensuring they grow into trees requires consistent watering, protection from grazing animals, and regular health checks. In many cases, saplings planted with much fanfare are forgotten once the cameras leave.

Environmentalists have long argued that Bengaluru's green drive lacks a robust post-plantation care plan. The current findings seem to echo those concerns. A tree that dies after planting is a waste of resources and effort.

What the city is doing

The city's forest department and the Bruhat Bengaluru Mahanagara Palike (BBMP) are yet to issue a formal response. Sources suggest that a detailed assessment is underway, and remedial measures may be taken at the affected sites.

There is also talk of involving local residents and resident welfare associations in monitoring the saplings. Community participation could be the key to turning the tide, but such plans are still in the early stages.

The bigger picture

Bengaluru has been striving to increase its green cover, which has been shrinking due to rapid urbanisation. Each lost sapling is a setback to that goal. The city's tree cover is not just about aesthetics; it impacts air quality, local temperatures, and even groundwater recharge.

The 27 sites with troubled saplings are a small fraction, but they represent a pattern that needs fixing. If left unaddressed, the problem could spread to other sites.

Officials are expected to release a detailed report soon. Until then, the fate of these saplings hangs in the balance, and the city waits to see if its green drive will truly take root.
