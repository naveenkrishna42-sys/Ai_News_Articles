New Zealand to ban social media for under-16s

📅 2026-08-25 📂 World Original source ↗

  • New Zealand follows Australia's lead in social media regulation for under-16s

New Zealand to introduce bill banning social media for children under 16

New Zealand's government is set to introduce a bill that would ban children under the age of 16 from using social media platforms. Prime Minister Christopher Luxon has proposed this measure, following in the footsteps of Australia, which has already implemented similar regulations.

Why this matters

The proposed ban is part of a broader global trend towards regulating social media use among children. Experts argue that social media platforms can have a significant impact on young people's mental health, cognitive development, and privacy. By banning children under 16 from using social media, New Zealand aims to protect them from these potential harms.

Broader impact

The introduction of this bill has the potential to set a precedent for other countries to follow suit. If successful, it could lead to a significant shift in how social media platforms are regulated and used by children worldwide. Additionally, it could have implications for the social media industry, as companies may need to adapt their platforms to comply with these new regulations.

Expert opinion and critical analysis

Psychologists and child development experts have expressed concerns about the potential impact of this ban. While they acknowledge the potential risks of social media use among children, they also argue that a complete ban may not be the most effective solution. Instead, they suggest that a more nuanced approach, such as age-appropriate content restrictions and parental controls, could be more effective in protecting children while still allowing them to benefit from the educational and social opportunities that social media can provide.

Furthermore, critics argue that the ban could have unintended consequences, such as driving children to use social media in secret or encouraging them to turn to other, potentially more dangerous online platforms. They also question the government's ability to effectively enforce the ban, given the widespread availability of social media platforms and the difficulty of monitoring children's online activities.
