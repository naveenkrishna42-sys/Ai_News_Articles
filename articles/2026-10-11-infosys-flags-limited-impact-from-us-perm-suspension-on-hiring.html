Infosys flags limited impact from US PERM suspension on hiring

What Infosys said—and why PERM matters

Infosys has indicated it does not expect a “material impact” from the US PERM suspension, a move that has unnerved parts of India’s IT offshoring workforce supply chain. PERM is a key employment-based green card pathway for certain roles, and a suspension can slow approvals that many employers rely on for longer-term talent continuity.

For IT services firms, the direct operational question is not only whether work visas are affected in the short run, but whether staffing plans—especially for onsite assignments, bench conversion, and role stability—get distorted over the next few quarters. Buyers should read such statements as “limited immediate business disruption,” not a guarantee of smooth immigration outcomes.

PERM suspension: the real-world effects on IT delivery

PERM is largely linked to green card processing, which tends to be slower and more process-heavy than many other work authorization routes. Still, it shapes employer planning because it influences how confidently firms forecast long-cycle immigration outcomes for specific categories of employees.

In delivery models common to global IT outsourcing—where onsite support, migration work, and customer-facing implementation often require continuity—any sustained immigration friction can increase churn costs. Even if projects can proceed with existing work authorizations, slower green card pipelines can push companies to lean more heavily on temporary mobility options, subcontractors, or longer lead times for replacement hires.

For procurement teams, the practical takeaway is to separate headline immigration from project staffing risk. The risk shows up in travel/onsite availability windows, role backfills, and whether a vendor can maintain steady throughput when key specialists rotate out.

Buyer checklist: how to assess vendor risk beyond the press release

Infosys’ posture may reassure markets, but contract managers should verify what “limited impact” means operationally. Ask for transparent staffing assumptions: which roles are onsite-dependent, what fraction of planned ramp-up requires employees whose status may be indirectly affected by PERM backlogs, and how the vendor will mitigate if approvals extend.

Good procurement practice is to review delivery resilience in measurable terms—bench strength, replacement SLAs, and cross-skilling coverage. The goal is not to second-guess immigration policy; it’s to ensure the vendor has a staffing plan that survives delays.

  • Timing clarity: Request ranges for onboarding lead times, not single-point dates.
  • Role mapping: Identify “critical onsite” positions and the backup coverage strategy.
  • Rate-card flexibility: Confirm whether substitution staffing changes billing rates or margin.
  • Travel contingency: Ensure onsite deliverables can be reconfigured to remote delivery if needed.
  • Escalation clauses: Include contract triggers tied to staffing availability, not immigration policy headlines.

Pricing and contract implications: where costs can shift

When immigration pathways tighten, IT firms typically manage by re-allocating capacity—shifting work to offshore teams, adding subcontractor capacity, or delaying onsite milestones. None of these are free. Procurement should expect potential cost pressures from increased travel delays, higher utilization of remote delivery, and additional coordination overhead.

That said, buyers are not helpless. Contract structuring can reduce the financial shock: milestone-based payment tied to verifiable deliverables, staffing continuity assurances, and clear acceptance criteria for remote substitutions. These tools convert uncertainty into governance rather than surprise invoices.

In practical budgeting, focus on total delivery cost over the quarter, including ramp, ramp-back, and transition overhead. If a vendor reduces onsite hours, evaluate whether re-planning still preserves outcomes—or whether productivity drops should be shared under the agreement.

Instead of relying on a single management statement, procurement should demand operational metrics that reveal how immigration constraints translate into staffing resilience. Below is a practical set of figures to ask for during vendor reviews.

For buyers managing multi-year outsourcing relationships, these metrics matter more than whether PERM is suspended or resumed. Immigration policy affects timelines, but delivery performance depends on resilience.

Real-world highlights buyers should watch

  • If vendors increase remote execution, check whether acceptance criteria and performance baselines are recalibrated.
  • If onsite roles stay fixed, confirm whether substitutions can meet the same customer-facing requirements.
  • If hiring ramps slow, ensure project staffing plans include contingency buffers for critical specialists.

Infosys’ confidence that the impact will not be material may hold for the near term—but procurement teams should still treat PERM-related uncertainty as a governance problem. Good contracts and quantified staffing controls can prevent immigration turbulence from becoming delivery risk.
