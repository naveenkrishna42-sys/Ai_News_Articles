India's Higher Education Sector Seeks Overhaul: Challenges and Reforms Ahead

📅 2026-07-18 📂 Education Original source ↗
Key points
  • India's higher education system faces challenges in employability, access, and quality.
  • The National Education Policy 2020 proposes structural reforms including multidisciplinary education.
  • Experts call for increased investment in research and faculty development.

The State of Higher Education in India

India's higher education system is at a crossroads. With over 1,000 universities and 40,000 colleges, it is one of the largest in the world. Yet, the sector faces persistent criticism over quality, employability of graduates, and access for marginalised communities.

Government data shows that only about 27% of India's youth enroll in higher education. The gross enrollment ratio lags behind many developing nations. More troubling: a 2020 survey found that nearly half of Indian graduates are not employable in the formal sector.

Key Challenges: Quality, Access, and Employability

Quality remains uneven. Many colleges lack basic infrastructure, qualified faculty, and updated curricula. The system rewards rote learning over critical thinking. This disconnect leaves students unprepared for the job market.

Access to higher education is another hurdle. Students from rural areas, low-income families, and disadvantaged castes often drop out early. The cost of private education, which accounts for nearly 60% of all colleges, puts it out of reach for many. Scholarships and reservation policies help, but gaps persist.

The National Education Policy 2020

The National Education Policy 2020, approved by the Union Cabinet, aims to address many of these issues. It proposes a shift from a rigid 10+2 structure to a 5+3+3+4 system, with greater flexibility in subject choices. Higher education will see a move towards multidisciplinary universities and a four-year undergraduate programme with multiple exit options.

The policy also emphasises vocational training, digital learning, and research. It targets raising the gross enrollment ratio to 50% by 2035. However, implementation has been slow. States have raised concerns about funding and the readiness of institutions to adapt.

Funding and Faculty: The Missing Links

India spends about 3.1% of its GDP on education, well below the global average of 4.5%. Higher education gets a smaller share. This underfunding affects everything from lab equipment to teacher salaries.

Faculty shortages are severe. Many colleges operate with a fraction of the required teaching staff. Low pay and limited research opportunities make academia an unattractive career. The policy aims to address this through faculty development programmes, but results are yet to be seen.

Technology and the Future of Learning

The pandemic accelerated the adoption of online learning. Platforms like SWAYAM and DIKSHA offer free courses, but digital infrastructure remains weak in rural areas. The government is pushing for blended learning models, but the digital divide remains a major obstacle.

Private edtech companies have grown rapidly, offering courses in coding, data science, and management. While they fill some gaps, concerns about regulation, quality, and affordability persist. The future may see a hybrid model where traditional universities partner with tech platforms.

The path forward for India's higher education system depends on sustained investment, political will, and institutional reform. The NEP 2020 provides a roadmap, but turning it into reality will take years of consistent effort. The question now is whether the country can move from policy to practice.
