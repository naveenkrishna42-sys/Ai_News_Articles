Mandakini's Claim: No Payment for 16-17 Films

In a candid interview, actor Mandakini has said she was never paid for 16-17 of her films. She attributed the loss to the informal nature of her contracts in the 1980s, when verbal assurances often replaced signed paperwork. The claim, first reported by NDTV, revives scrutiny of an era when star-making machinery ran on personal trust rather than enforceable agreements.

Mandakini, best known for her breakout role in Ram Teri Ganga Maili (1985), was among the most photographed actors of her time. Yet her disclosure suggests that visibility did not translate into financial security, a pattern still familiar to many working actors in Indian cinema.

Why Informal Contracts Were the Norm in 1980s Bollywood

During the 1980s, the Hindi film industry functioned largely as an unorganised sector. Producers, financiers and distributors frequently struck deals on the phone or at film parties. Actors, especially newcomers, accepted roles on verbal promises of payment after release. There was no standard contract format, no minimum wage board for performers, and no statutory body to arbitrate unpaid dues quickly.

Payment structures were equally opaque. Artists were often told they would be paid after a film's release, after satellite or music rights were sold, or in instalments tied to a producer's next project. When a film flopped or a producer's finances collapsed, the actor's fee simply disappeared from the ledger. Women actors faced additional pressure to avoid confrontation for fear of being labelled difficult.

What This Means for Buyers, Viewers and Aspiring Actors

For streaming platforms and film buyers, back-catalogue titles featuring Mandakini carry nostalgia value, but the unpaid-dues claim raises a practical question: who actually holds the residual rights? When contracts were informal, chain of title can be disputed decades later, complicating re-release, restoration or digital licensing deals.

For aspiring actors, the lesson is direct. A written contract should specify the fee, payment schedule, overtime, dubbing, promotional duties and royalty share. CINTAA and FWICE membership offers a paper trail and mediation route if a producer defaults. Agents and lawyers cost money, but an unpaid fee costs more.

Pros and Cons: The Informal-Deal Era

  • Pro: Fast casting decisions; newcomers could get work without legal costs or formal representation.
  • Pro: Personal relationships sometimes ensured repeat work and long-term patronage.
  • Con: No enforceable proof of payment terms, leaving actors with little legal recourse.
  • Con: Women and newcomers were especially vulnerable to exploitation and silence.
  • Con: Unclear rights chains still obstruct film restoration and digital monetisation today.

Industry Accountability and the Road Ahead

Mandakini's disclosure is not an isolated anecdote. Similar accounts have emerged from actors across regional cinema, where daily-wage artists and junior technicians often work without written agreements. The shift to OTT platforms has improved documentation for lead cast, but supporting actors and crew remain exposed.

Trade unions now push for registered contracts, minimum fees and timely payment. Enforcement, however, depends on actors reporting defaults and producers facing consequences. Until that becomes routine, stories like Mandakini's will keep surfacing, reminding the industry that fame and fair payment are not the same thing.
