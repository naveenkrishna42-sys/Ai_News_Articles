Europe Cannot Sustain Prolonged War of Attrition With Russia: Officials

Key points
  • European defence officials warn of insufficient industrial depth for sustained conflict
  • Ammunition and artillery production remains below wartime demand
  • Strategic autonomy requires faster integration and funding mobilisation

War of attrition assessment emerges

Defence officials cited by The Guardian on 5 September 2026 warn that Europe would not be able to sustain a war of attrition with Russia. The assessment focuses on industrial endurance rather than battlefield tactics. Officials stress that current European posture is built for short, high-intensity deterrence, not prolonged material consumption.

The warning reflects concern over the gap between political ambition for strategic autonomy and the material base required to support it. A war of attrition tests production lines, logistics chains and fiscal stamina over months and years. European leaders are being urged to align procurement and industrial policy with that reality.

Industrial and supply constraints

European defence production remains fragmented across national programmes and suppliers. Ammunition output has risen since 2022 but remains constrained by limited shell factories, powder production and skilled labour. Artillery barrel and missile component manufacturing faces long lead times due to reliance on single-source facilities.

Production and sustainment bottlenecks

Sustainment readiness is limited by spare parts availability and maintenance capacity. Many European armies entered the post-Cold War era with reduced stockpiles and just-in-time logistics. Rebuilding those stocks requires sustained investment and stable demand signals to industry.

Financial mobilisation is uneven across the European Union. Defence budgets have increased in nominal terms, yet spending is spread across many national programmes, duplicating costs and slowing scale effects. Officials argue that pooled procurement and joint industrial capacity are essential to reduce costs and increase output.

Strategic implications for European defence

The assessment pushes European policymakers toward industrial consolidation and long-term contracting. Officials highlight the need for guaranteed orders, workforce training and dual-use capacity to maintain output under stress. Without these, Europe risks strategic dependence on external suppliers in a prolonged conflict.

Buyer implications for member states include prioritising high-readiness munitions, standardising calibres and investing in maintenance infrastructure. Procurement cycles must shift from bespoke national systems to interoperable, scalable platforms. The focus moves from platform acquisition to throughput and replenishment.
