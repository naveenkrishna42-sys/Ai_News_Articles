A Week of American Assertiveness

The week of August 22, 2026, saw the United States flex its geopolitical muscle across multiple fronts simultaneously—and India found itself squarely in the crosshairs of Washington's most aggressive postures. From contested waterways to domestic political organisations, American actions this week have forced New Delhi to recalibrate how it manages a relationship that has been called one of the most consequential bilateral partnerships of the 21st century.

Three distinct but interconnected developments have dominated headlines: President Trump's declaration that the Strait of Hormuz constitutes "American territory," a US government commission's recommendation to impose sanctions on the Rashtriya Swayamsevak Sangh, and the broader context of US trade actions that ripple across global markets. For ordinary Indians, these are not abstract foreign policy matters—they translate directly into fuel prices, diplomatic leverage, and the sovereignty of institutions that shape national life.

The Strait of Hormuz Gambit

The most immediately consequential development came when Trump declared the Strait of Hormuz—through which roughly a fifth of the world's oil passes—to be under American jurisdiction. The statement, made amid escalating tensions with Iran, sent shockwaves through global energy markets and directly threatened India's strategic interests.

India imports approximately 80 percent of its crude oil, with a significant portion flowing through the Persian Gulf and the Strait of Hormuz. Any disruption to shipping through this chokepoint translates almost immediately into higher petrol and diesel prices at Indian pumps. Energy security analysts have long recognised this vulnerability, but Trump's assertion raises it from theoretical concern to immediate practical problem.

"The Strait of Hormuz is international waters governed by international law," said one former foreign ministry official who requested anonymity to speak freely on sensitive matters. "When a major power claims jurisdiction over such a critical waterway, it creates uncertainty that markets hate. India's response needs to be measured but firm."

Iran has not remained silent. The Islamic Republic controls the northern shore of the strait and has in the past threatened to close the waterway entirely if its security was threatened. Any American attempt to enforce Trump's claim could trigger exactly the kind of escalation that would devastate Indian energy imports.

The RSS Sanctions Push

Perhaps more surprising to many observers was the recommendation from a US government-affiliated body to impose sanctions on the RSS, India's largest voluntary Hindu organisation. The US Commission on International Religious Freedom, in its latest report, suggested targeted measures against theorganisation that serves as the ideological parent of India's ruling Bharatiya Janata Party.

India's foreign ministry responded sharply, with official spokespersons calling the recommendation "interference in India's internal affairs" and noting that the RSS, as a cultural organisation, operates well within Indian law. The statement emphasised that India determines its own approach to civil society organisations and resents external pressure on these matters.

"They have their own agenda," a ministry official said, according to official briefings. "This kind of recommendation reflects a fundamental misunderstanding of how Indian civil society functions."

The timing of this recommendation has drawn particular scrutiny. It comes as the RSS celebrates major events and as the BJP prepares for significant political engagements. Critics within India see it as an attempt to influence domestic political dynamics through external pressure—precisely the kind of intervention New Delhi has long opposed in principle.

The Tariff Shadow

While the Strait of Hormuz declaration and RSS sanctions recommendation dominated diplomatic headlines, the US imposition of 50 percent tariffs on 20 billion dollars worth of Canadian goods served as a reminder that American trade unilateralism continues to reshape global commerce. The tariffs, imposed after bilateral negotiations collapsed, affect aluminium, lumber, and several agricultural products.

For India, this development carries both direct and indirect implications. Directly, any disruption to North American supply chains can affect Indian manufacturers who rely on Canadian raw materials. Indirectly, the aggressive use of tariffs by Washington underscores a pattern of unilateral action that makes all trading partners, including India, nervous about the stability of global trade rules.

India has its own trade disputes with the US, particularly regarding technology tariffs and agricultural subsidies. The Canadian episode demonstrates how quickly bilateral tensions can escalate when Washington decides to act unilaterally.

What This Means for Ordinary Indians

The convergence of these developments might seem like a series of unrelated headlines, but together they paint a coherent picture of a global order where American unilateralism is testing the limits of international cooperation. For Indian citizens, the practical implications are tangible.

Oil prices, which affect everything from commuting costs to food prices through transportation expenses, depend partly on the stability of shipping routes like the Strait of Hormuz. Any military tension in the Persian Gulf translates into premium payments for alternative supply arrangements or risk premiums built into oil prices.

Diplomatic relationships with the US, while important for technology access and strategic positioning, cannot come at the cost of sovereignty over domestic institutions. The RSS sanctions recommendation, even if ultimately non-binding, represents an intrusion that many Indians find deeply objectionable regardless of their political affiliations.

The government's response so far has been measured but firm. New Delhi has avoided escalation while making clear that these actions cross lines that India considers non-negotiable. How this balance is maintained will define the trajectory of US-India relations in the coming months.

Looking Ahead

The coming weeks will test whether this represents a temporary spike in tensions or a sustained shift in how the US approaches its relationship with India. Scheduled diplomatic engagements between the two countries remain on the calendar, but their success now depends partly on how the Hormuz situation develops and whether the sanctions recommendation gains traction in Washington.

Energy markets will be the first indicator of how serious the Strait of Hormuz situation becomes. If oil prices remain stable, it suggests markets do not expect immediate disruption. If they rise sharply, pressure will mount on New Delhi to take a clearer public position.

India's parliament is expected to discuss some of these international developments in its upcoming session, with opposition parties already demanding clarity on the government's response strategy. The debate will test whether India's approach to American unilateralism can maintain the bipartisan consensus that has generally characterised New Delhi's engagement with Washington.
