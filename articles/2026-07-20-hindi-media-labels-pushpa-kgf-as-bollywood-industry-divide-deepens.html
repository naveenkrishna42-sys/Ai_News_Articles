Hindi Media Labels Pushpa, KGF as Bollywood: Industry Divide Deepens

📅 2026-07-20 📂 Movies Original source ↗
Key points
  • Hindi media often labels successful South Indian films as Bollywood productions.
  • Industry observers cite a mix of marketing strategy, linguistic bias, and audience reach.
  • The trend has sparked debate about regional identity and credit where it's due.

The Headline That Sparked a Debate

When Pushpa: The Rise and the KGF franchise shattered box office records across India, Hindi media outlets routinely referred to them as 'Bollywood films'. The practice has angered some fans and industry insiders, who argue it erases the films' South Indian origins.

The question being asked on social media and industry forums is simple: Why do national dailies and entertainment portals insist on claiming these movies for Bollywood?

Marketing Muscle and Pan-India Appeal

Trade analysts point to one dominant reason: distribution and marketing. Both Pushpa and KGF were released in Hindi by major Bollywood studios. KGF: Chapter 2 was distributed by Excel Entertainment and AA Films, while Pushpa's Hindi version was handled by Mantena Movies.

When a film's Hindi release is backed by a Mumbai-based powerhouse, the media naturally tags it as a Hindi film. For casual audiences, the line between a 'Hindi film' and a 'pan-India film' blurs quickly.

Linguistic Bias in National Media

Critics say the issue runs deeper than marketing. Hindi-language media, by its very nature, prioritises content that speaks to its audience. A film shot in Telugu or Kannada but dubbed in Hindi gets reframed as a 'Bollywood product' because that is the lens through which the reporter sees it.

This isn't new. Earlier, dubbed versions of Tamil blockbusters like Baahubali and 2.0 were also frequently described as Bollywood ventures. The trend reveals a systemic tendency to centre Mumbai's film industry as the default for Indian cinema.

Fan Backlash and Industry Reaction

Fans of stars like Allu Arjun and Yash have called out the misclassification. They argue it robs the original industries — Tollywood and Sandalwood — of their due credit. 'A film made in Telugu or Kannada doesn't become Bollywood just because it makes money in the North,' one Twitter user wrote.

Some trade experts have a softer view. They say the tag is a lazy shorthand, not a conspiracy. 'It's easier for a Delhi-based editor to call it a Bollywood film than to explain the nuances of regional cinema to a reader who doesn't know Tollywood from Mollywood,' a journalist noted.

No official clarification has come from either the Hindi media houses or the filmmakers themselves.

What This Means for Indian Cinema

The debate reflects a larger shift. South Indian films now dominate the national conversation, but the infrastructure of film journalism hasn't fully adapted. As long as Hindi remains the lingua franca of national media, the tension between regional identity and pan-India success will persist.

Industry watchers expect the trend to continue until more outlets hire reporters who understand the full map of Indian cinema, not just the Mumbai circuit.
