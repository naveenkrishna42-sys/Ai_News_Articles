New Criminal Laws: Are We Heading Towards a Police State? Lawyer Rebecca John Weighs In

📅 2026-07-19 📂 Crime & Law Original source ↗
Key points
  • Rebecca John criticises new criminal laws for expanding police powers without accountability.
  • She raises concerns about the dilution of procedural safeguards for the accused.
  • The lawyer warns that the laws could erode the presumption of innocence.

India's three new criminal codes—the Bharatiya Nyaya Sanhita, Bharatiya Nagarik Suraksha Sanhita, and Bharatiya Sakshya Adhiniyam—came into effect on July 1, 2024. They replace the colonial-era Indian Penal Code, Code of Criminal Procedure, and the Indian Evidence Act. But not everyone is convinced this is progress.

In an interview with Frontline Magazine, senior criminal lawyer Rebecca John offered a sharp critique. She argued that the new laws expand police powers while weakening the checks that protect ordinary citizens. Her central question: are we building a police state?

More Power, Less Accountability

John pointed out that the new codes grant law enforcement authorities wider discretion in arrests and detentions. For instance, the police can now arrest without a warrant for a broader range of offences. This shift, she said, removes a crucial layer of judicial oversight.

She also flagged the introduction of preventive detention provisions that allow holding individuals for up to 90 days without trial. While the government claims these measures target terrorism and organised crime, John warned they could be misused against political dissenters and activists.

“The balance between state security and individual liberty is tilting dangerously,” she said.

Dilution of Procedural Safeguards

The lawyer highlighted changes to bail provisions. Under the old CrPC, bail was the rule and jail the exception for bailable offences. The new law reverses this for certain categories, making it harder for the accused to secure release.

John also criticised the reduced role of magistrates in the remand process. She said the new system allows the police to keep a person in custody for up to 15 days without producing them before a magistrate. That window, she noted, is long enough for coercion or forced confessions.

“Procedural safeguards are not technicalities. They are the only shield a common person has against arbitrary state power,” she told Frontline.

Presumption of Innocence Under Threat

Another concern is the burden of proof. The Bharatiya Sakshya Adhiniyam introduces provisions where the accused must prove their innocence in certain cases—like those involving electronic evidence. John argued this contradicts the fundamental principle that one is innocent until proven guilty.

She pointed to the expanded definition of “documents” to include digital records. While that keeps pace with technology, she said, it also makes it easier for the prosecution to introduce unverified data as evidence. The defence, meanwhile, may lack the resources to challenge it.

“We are moving towards a system where the state’s word is taken as truth unless the accused can disprove it,” she said.

What About Judicial Oversight?

John acknowledged that the new laws aim to speed up trials and reduce pendency. But she questioned whether faster justice at the cost of fairness is justice at all. She noted that the role of the judiciary in pre-trial stages has been significantly reduced.

For example, the requirement for a magistrate to personally approve a long-term detention has been diluted. The police can now seek extensions with minimal judicial scrutiny. John called this a dangerous precedent.

“The judiciary was the last line of defence. If that is weakened, who protects the vulnerable?” she asked.

The government has defended the reforms, saying they modernise the legal framework and prioritise victim rights. But critics like John argue that the pendulum has swung too far—sacrificing the rights of the accused in the name of efficiency.

As the laws begin to take effect, legal experts will be watching closely. The Supreme Court is likely to hear challenges in the coming months. The question remains: will these codes deliver justice, or will they concentrate too much power in the hands of the police?
