Infosys skips AI data centre race as TCS, HCLTech invest big

📅 2026-07-31 📂 Business Original source ↗
Key points
  • Infosys is avoiding direct investments in AI data centres.
  • TCS and HCLTech are actively building AI infrastructure.
  • CEO Salil Parekh emphasises AI labs and software services over hardware.

Different Paths in the AI Gold Rush

As India's top IT services firms rush to build AI data centres, Infosys is taking a conspicuously different route. The Bengaluru-headquartered company is staying out of the infrastructure race, even as rivals TCS and HCLTech plow money into massive computing facilities.

The divergence marks a strategic fork in the road for India's $250-billion IT industry. While TCS and HCLTech are betting that owning AI infrastructure will be a competitive advantage, Infosys is doubling down on software and services — the traditional core of its business.

Why Infosys Is Holding Back

Infosys CEO Salil Parekh has made it clear that the company sees greater value in building AI labs and solutions for clients rather than owning physical data centres. The logic is simple: data centres require huge capital expenditure and offer thin margins, while software services deliver higher returns and deeper client relationships.

Industry analysts suggest Infosys may also be wary of the risks involved. Building and operating AI data centres involves significant energy costs, land acquisition, and rapid technology obsolescence — all challenges that could weigh on profitability.

Instead, Infosys is focusing on what Parekh calls "AI labs" — specialised centres where the company develops AI use cases for clients across banking, retail, and manufacturing. These labs prove that IT services firms still matter in an AI-driven world, without the heavy lifting of infrastructure ownership.

Hiring and Growth Plans Remain Intact

Despite the cautious approach to infrastructure, Infosys is not slowing down on talent. The company has reaffirmed its plan to hire 20,000 freshers in FY27, following a round of salary hikes. This signals confidence in demand for its services, even as it avoids capital-intensive bets.

Parekh, who is set to step down as CEO, has said he will continue to add value after his tenure ends. His successor will inherit a company that is choosing agility over assets — a stance that may prove prescient if AI infrastructure becomes commoditised.

A Contrast in Strategies

TCS and HCLTech have both announced significant investments in AI data centres, betting that clients will need secure, compliant computing power to run AI workloads. They argue that owning infrastructure gives them control over costs, security, and customisation.

Infosys, however, appears to believe that the real value lies in the intellectual property and domain expertise built on top of AI models. By partnering with cloud providers rather than competing with them, Infosys can offer clients flexibility without the balance-sheet burden.

This is not the first time Infosys has diverged from its peers. During the cloud migration wave, the company was initially slower to build its own cloud platforms, yet still captured significant market share through partnerships. The AI data centre decision follows a similar playbook.

What Clients Should Watch

For IT buyers, the strategic split means different trade-offs. TCS and HCLTech may offer integrated infrastructure-plus-services deals, while Infosys will emphasise faster time-to-market and lower upfront costs through software-centric AI solutions.

The bet on AI labs is already showing results, with Parekh pointing to client engagements that have moved from pilots to production. But the long-term question remains: will clients value the convenience of bundled infrastructure, or the flexibility of a services-only approach?

As the AI cycle matures, Infosys's strategy will face its first real test. If AI infrastructure becomes a commodity, the company's asset-light model could emerge as the more profitable path. If not, it may find itself playing catch-up in a race it chose to sit out.
