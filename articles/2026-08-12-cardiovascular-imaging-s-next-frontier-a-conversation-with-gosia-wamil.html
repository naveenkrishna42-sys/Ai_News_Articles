Cardiovascular Imaging's Next Frontier: A Conversation with Gosia Wamil

📅 2026-08-12 📂 Health Original source ↗
Key points
  • Dr Gosia Wamil, a leading cardiologist, shares insights on the evolving role of imaging in heart care.
  • The interview highlights the integration of artificial intelligence in cardiovascular diagnostics.
  • Experts emphasise the need for earlier detection and personalised treatment approaches.

Cardiovascular disease remains one of India's and the world's biggest killers, and the tools used to spot it are changing fast. In a recent interview with the European Medical Journal (EMJ), Dr Gosia Wamil, a prominent voice in cardiovascular imaging, laid out what the next decade could look like for heart diagnostics.

Dr Wamil's perspective comes at a time when non-invasive imaging is moving beyond simple anatomical pictures. The focus, she suggests, is shifting toward a more dynamic, functional understanding of the heart. This is not just about seeing a blockage; it is about understanding how blood flows, how tissue behaves, and how to predict risk before a major event occurs.

The Rise of AI in the Reading Room

One of the central themes of the conversation was the growing role of artificial intelligence. AI is no longer a futuristic concept in cardiology; it is being tested and implemented in reading rooms across the globe. For Dr Wamil, the promise lies in AI's ability to handle vast amounts of imaging data quickly, picking up subtle patterns that the human eye might miss.

This could mean faster, more accurate readings of echocardiograms, CT scans, and MRIs. It also raises the possibility of standardising care, ensuring that a patient in a small town receives the same level of diagnostic scrutiny as one in a major metropolitan hospital. However, Dr Wamil also stressed that AI is a tool to augment, not replace, the clinician's judgment.

Beyond the Static Image

The interview also touched on the move away from static images towards more dynamic assessments. Advanced imaging techniques are now allowing cardiologists to assess the physiology of a blockage, not just its presence. This is crucial for making decisions about whether a patient needs an intervention like a stent or whether medication and lifestyle changes will suffice.

This approach is particularly relevant in India, where the burden of heart disease is high and often strikes at a younger age. Early and precise diagnosis can lead to better outcomes and reduce the number of unnecessary procedures. The ability to look at the 'plaque' itself and understand its composition is a key area of development, helping to identify vulnerable plaques that are prone to rupture.

Personalised Care Through Better Imaging

Ultimately, the future of cardiovascular imaging is about personalisation. By combining advanced imaging with clinical data, doctors can move away from a one-size-fits-all treatment plan. They can tailor therapies to the individual patient's specific risk profile, based on the detailed pictures and data gathered from a scan.

This vision requires not only technological advancement but also training. Dr Wamil's discussion implicitly underscores the need for the next generation of cardiologists and radiologists to be fluent in both imaging technology and data science. The days of a simple ultrasound are giving way to a multi-modal, data-rich environment.

As these technologies mature, the cost of advanced imaging is expected to come down, making them more accessible in emerging economies. The challenge for healthcare systems will be to integrate these tools effectively, ensuring that the promise of better imaging translates into real-world patient benefits.

For now, the message from experts like Dr Wamil is one of cautious optimism. The pieces are in place for a revolution in how we detect and manage heart disease. What remains to be seen is how quickly these advances can be adopted on a global scale, bringing the future of cardiac care to every patient who needs it.
