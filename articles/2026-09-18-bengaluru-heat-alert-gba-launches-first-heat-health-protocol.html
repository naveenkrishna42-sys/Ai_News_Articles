Bengaluru's Heat Crisis Spurs First-Ever Heat-Health Protocol

The Greater Bengaluru Authority (GBA) has rolled out a comprehensive heat-health protocol, a first for the city, as rising temperatures and humidity push heat stress to dangerous levels. The protocol, developed with public health experts and civic bodies, aims to protect residents through a graded alert system, real-time advisories, and on-ground interventions. For a city historically known for its pleasant climate, this marks a significant policy shift.

Why Bengaluru Needs a Heat-Health Protocol

Bengaluru's rapid urbanisation has replaced green cover with concrete, creating urban heat islands. Summer temperatures now routinely cross 35°C, and humidity often exceeds 60%, compounding heat stress. The Indian Meteorological Department (IMD) has recorded a steady rise in heatwave days over the past decade. Vulnerable groups—outdoor labourers, street vendors, the elderly, and children—face heightened risks of dehydration, heat exhaustion, and heatstroke. The GBA's protocol is a direct response to this escalating threat.

How the Protocol Works: Thresholds and Actions

The protocol categorises heat risk into four levels—Green, Yellow, Orange, and Red—based on temperature and humidity thresholds. Each level triggers specific actions from civic agencies, health departments, and employers. For example, at Orange, outdoor work between 12 PM and 3 PM is discouraged, and cooling centres open. At Red, all non-essential outdoor activity is halted, and hospitals are put on high alert. The table below summarises the key specifications.

Real-World Implications for Residents and Businesses

For residents, the protocol means earlier warnings and easier access to relief. The GBA will push alerts through multiple channels, including a dedicated helpline. Businesses, especially construction and delivery firms, must adjust schedules to avoid peak heat. Compliance will be monitored by ward-level committees. While the protocol is not legally binding yet, the GBA plans to integrate it into building codes and labour regulations. The success hinges on funding and inter-agency coordination—challenges that have plagued similar initiatives in other Indian cities.

Pros and Cons of the GBA Heat-Health Protocol

  • Pros: Standardised response framework; focus on vulnerable groups; real-time data-driven alerts; potential to reduce heat-related mortality.
  • Cons: Lack of enforcement teeth; funding uncertainties; limited public awareness; cooling centres may be insufficient for a city of 13 million.

Expert View: What Makes This Protocol Effective

Public health researchers emphasise that heat-health protocols work only when they are localised and inclusive. Dr. Anjali Sharma, a climate-health expert, notes that Bengaluru's protocol is a good start but requires continuous evaluation. "The thresholds must be revised as the climate changes, and the plan must reach the last mile—slum dwellers and migrant workers," she says. The GBA has committed to an annual review and a public dashboard showing heat risk levels.

Comparing Bengaluru to Other Indian Cities

Ahmedabad pioneered India's first heat action plan in 2013, which reduced heat-related deaths significantly. Mumbai and Delhi followed with similar plans. Bengaluru's protocol borrows best practices but adds a unique humidity component, crucial for coastal and plateau climates. However, unlike Ahmedabad, Bengaluru lacks a robust early warning system integrated with the IMD. The GBA is working on a partnership to access real-time weather data.

What Lies Ahead

The protocol's rollout begins immediately, with pilot cooling centres in 10 wards. The GBA will train volunteers and conduct mock drills before the peak summer of 2027. For a city grappling with climate change, this protocol is a necessary step—but its effectiveness will depend on political will, public cooperation, and sustained investment. Residents can track alerts via the GBA's official website or the Bengaluru One app.
