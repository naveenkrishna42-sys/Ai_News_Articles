The volunteers who kept the Jantar Mantar protest running day and night

📅 2026-07-27 📂 India Original source ↗
Key points
  • 12 volunteers managed protest logistics at Jantar Mantar
  • Water and food shortages disrupted the site, affecting women the most
  • Volunteers worked without rest, driven by commitment over fear

At Delhi's Jantar Mantar, where protests have become a fixture of political life, a small group of volunteers ensured the recent stir never lost momentum. With basic amenities stretched thin and demonstrators facing severe water cuts, a 12-member team stepped in to manage the chaos.

These volunteers, many of them young and first-time activists, ran the protest site like a tight operation. They organised food distribution, managed water supplies, and coordinated with authorities. Their work was invisible to the cameras but essential to the movement.

Water, food, and the daily grind

Water became a scarce commodity at the protest site. Authorities cut off regular supply, forcing demonstrators to rely on tankers and personal reserves. Women protesters faced the brunt of this shortage, struggling to access toilets and clean water for days.

Volunteers stepped in to fill the gap. They arranged for bottled water, set up makeshift sanitation units, and coordinated with local suppliers. One volunteer said the team worked around the clock, often skipping meals to ensure others were fed.

"We had to be bigger than fear," a volunteer told reporters. "If we stopped, the protest would collapse." The statement captured the mood of a group that saw itself as the backbone of the stir.

Logistics in a pressure cooker

The protest site at Jantar Mantar is a historic venue for demonstrations. But it lacks permanent infrastructure for large gatherings. With hundreds camping for days, garbage piled up, food ran short, and tempers flared.

The 12-member team managed these logistics without formal training. They divided tasks—some handled kitchen duty, others managed crowd control, and a few liaised with police. Their coordination was improvised but effective.

"We didn't sleep for three days," one volunteer recalled. "But we knew if we stopped, people would go hungry." The group also sent out SOS calls for food supplies, which local NGOs and sympathisers answered with donations of rice, lentils, and packaged snacks.

Women at the frontlines of hardship

Women protesters bore the heaviest burden. Toilets were few, and water cuts made hygiene nearly impossible. Several women reported health issues due to unsanitary conditions. The volunteers prioritised their needs, setting up separate sanitation areas and ensuring female volunteers handled distribution.

"Men don't understand the struggle," a woman protester said. "We had to fight for every drop of water." The volunteers acknowledged the gap and worked to bridge it, but resources were limited. The protest continued, but the cost was visible in exhausted faces and frayed nerves.

Why they kept going

None of the 12 volunteers were paid. Many had taken leave from jobs or colleges to be at Jantar Mantar. They cited a cause larger than themselves—a belief that the protest needed to be sustained, no matter the personal cost.

"Fear is real," one volunteer said. "But when you see people suffering, you forget your own problems." The team's dedication kept the stir alive even as mainstream attention waned. They became the unsung backbone of a movement that relied on ordinary people doing extraordinary things.

What happens next remains uncertain. The protest continues, and volunteers are preparing for a long haul. But their strength is finite. Without sustained support, the site could face deeper crises. For now, the 12 are holding the line—one meal, one bucket of water, one day at a time.
