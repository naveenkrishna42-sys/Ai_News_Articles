Zerodha’s Dinesh Pai: US green-card pause could shift demand to Indian IT

What Dinesh Pai’s “upside” framing implies for Indian IT buyers

Zerodha’s Dinesh Pai’s view—casting the US green-card suspension as an “opportunity” for Indian IT firms—boils down to a familiar buyer reality: when a destination market makes long-term staffing harder, clients often restructure how work gets delivered.

In US labour markets, green-card policy affects long-horizon hiring pipelines (not just near-term visa turns). If PERM timelines freeze or approvals slow, large enterprises can face uncertainty about backfills and onsite talent continuity—especially for projects where local presence is tied to regulatory, customer-facing, or security requirements.

For Indian IT providers such as TCS, Infosys, and Wipro, the potential upside is that clients may lean harder on offshore delivery, dual-shore models, and repeatable onsite rotations that do not require the same long-term immigration runway.

How a PERM/green-card slowdown changes real procurement decisions

Procurement and delivery leadership tends to respond to immigration friction with measurable operational changes. The most common levers: re-scoping deliverables, increasing remote governance, shifting certain workstreams to regions with stable staffing, and tightening vendor substitution clauses in master services agreements.

That can translate into more demand for:

  • Offshore/nearshore “capacity with continuity” where project delivery depends less on long-term US-based roles.
  • Managed services and outcome-based milestones that reduce dependency on a specific onsite headcount.
  • Strong transition support—documentation, knowledge capture, and automated handoffs—because client teams are less able to rely on stable long-duration staffing.

For buyers, the question is not whether onsite delivery disappears; it’s whether the mix changes, how quickly clients can ramp delivery teams, and what it does to total cost of ownership.

What to watch in TCS, Infosys and Wipro if policy friction persists

Even if the “upside” is real, buyers should treat it as conditional. US immigration slowdowns can also raise delivery friction costs: more subcontracting, more reliance on short-term visas for specific roles, and higher spend on compliance and travel logistics.

Instead of expecting an immediate revenue step-up, procurement-minded investors should watch second-order indicators that usually show up in guidance commentary and segment trends—things like utilisation, onsite/offshore mix, and travel intensity.

Here are practical benchmarks buyers and analysts often track when assessing an IT provider’s resilience:

Buyer playbook: turning immigration uncertainty into vendor selection criteria

If you’re a business buyer considering vendors in 2026-27, a US immigration crackdown shouldn’t just be treated as macro noise. It changes how you should evaluate delivery risk—and how you should structure contracts.

Concrete selection questions that map to immigration-driven constraints:

  • Can the vendor deliver key milestones with offshore-first staffing? Ask for delivery plans that specify fallback models if onsite headcount is delayed.
  • Do they have robust knowledge-transfer tooling? Look for evidence of automation, documentation standards, and repeatable transition playbooks.
  • How do they manage onsite dependency? Request an allocation strategy showing which workstreams truly require onsite presence.
  • What’s the subcontracting governance? If third parties become necessary, ensure quality, security, and SLA oversight are clear.

The “upside” in Pai’s framing is most likely to show up when clients prioritise execution continuity over staffing optics—choosing vendors who can absorb onsite constraints without derailing delivery outcomes.

Key real-world highlights and trade-offs

US green-card suspension can be both a headwind and a catalyst, depending on the service mix and contract structure. The firms’ ability to translate offshore strength into measurable client outcomes will determine whether the policy shift becomes incremental demand or a temporary substitution pattern.

  • Pros: Offshore-first models can reduce delivery dependency on long-term US immigration approvals.
  • Pros: Clients may re-balance budgets toward vendors with proven remote governance and rapid ramp capability.
  • Cons: Short-term visas, travel, and compliance costs can rise, pressuring margins if pass-through is limited.
  • Cons: Project initiations may slow if client hiring uncertainty delays approvals, reducing net new demand.
  • Buyer takeaway: Contract terms, delivery fallback plans, and onsite dependency mapping matter more than headlines about immigration freezes.
