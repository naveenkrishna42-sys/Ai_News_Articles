Bombay High Court Upholds Right to Protest

The Bombay High Court on Saturday declined to restrain activist Manoj Jarange Patil from marching to Mumbai, stating that in a democracy, people cannot be stopped from protesting. The court was hearing a plea seeking to halt the march, which is scheduled to culminate in a hunger strike from September 19, 2026. The bench emphasised that the right to protest is a fundamental democratic right, but also cautioned that it must be exercised peacefully. This decision clears the way for Jarange and his supporters to proceed with their agitation demanding Maratha reservation under the Other Backward Classes (OBC) category.

Background: The Maratha Reservation Demand

The Maratha community, comprising roughly 30% of Maharashtra's population, has long demanded reservation in education and government jobs. Previous agitations, notably in 2016 and 2018, turned violent, resulting in loss of life and property. In 2019, the Maharashtra government introduced a 16% reservation for Marathas, but the Supreme Court struck it down in 2021, citing the 50% cap on reservations. Since then, Jarange has led a renewed movement, including a hunger strike in 2023 that pressured the state to issue a draft notification recognising Marathas as Kunbis (a sub-caste within OBC), thereby entitling them to OBC benefits. The current march aims to escalate pressure for a concrete legislative solution.

Why the High Court's Decision Matters

The court's refusal to intervene sets a significant precedent for protest rights in India. It underscores that while authorities can regulate traffic and maintain law and order, they cannot preemptively ban peaceful assemblies. For Mumbai residents and businesses, the march poses logistical challenges: expect road closures, diversions, and heightened security around the protest site. The economic impact could be substantial if the agitation prolongs, affecting daily wage earners, small businesses, and commuters. However, the court's nod also places a responsibility on organisers to ensure the protest remains non-violent.

Real-World Implications for Mumbai

Mumbai, India's financial capital, is no stranger to protests, but a march of this scale could strain infrastructure. Past Maratha agitations have seen incidents of violence, prompting authorities to deploy additional police forces. Commuters should plan for delays on major arteries like the Eastern Express Highway and Western Express Highway. Businesses in affected areas may face reduced footfall. The state government is caught between appeasing the Maratha community and adhering to Supreme Court guidelines on reservation caps. Chief Minister Eknath Shinde has appealed for calm, while opposition leaders have extended support to the agitation, intensifying political pressure.

Pros and Cons of the Agitation

  • Pros:
    • Highlights the legitimate demand for social justice and economic upliftment of the Maratha community.
    • Reinforces democratic rights to peaceful protest, as affirmed by the High Court.
    • Could accelerate government action on a long-pending issue.
  • Cons:
    • Risk of violence and property damage, as seen in previous agitations.
    • Disruption to daily life and economic activity in Mumbai.
    • Potential for political polarisation and caste tensions.

What Lies Ahead

The Maharashtra government must now balance legal constraints with political realities. The Backward Class Commission is expected to submit its report on the Kunbi certification process, which could influence the reservation decision. Jarange has threatened to intensify the agitation if demands are not met by September 19. For now, Mumbai braces for a week of heightened activity. The High Court's decision serves as a reminder that in a democracy, the right to protest is sacrosanct, but it must be exercised with responsibility. As the situation unfolds, all eyes are on whether this agitation will lead to a lasting solution or further unrest.
