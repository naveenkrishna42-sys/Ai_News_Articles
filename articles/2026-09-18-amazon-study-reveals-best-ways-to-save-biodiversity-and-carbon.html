Amazon Study Reveals Best Ways to Save Biodiversity and Carbon

Key points
  • Indigenous territories and protected areas deliver the strongest co-benefits for biodiversity and carbon.
  • Restoring degraded lands is less cost-effective than preventing deforestation in intact forests.
  • Policy must prioritize large, interconnected landscapes over isolated patches for long-term resilience.

New Amazonian Research Pinpoints High-Impact Conservation Strategies

A landmark study spanning the Amazon basin has identified the most effective ways to conserve both biodiversity and carbon stocks simultaneously. Conducted by an international team of researchers and published in a peer-reviewed journal, the analysis evaluates multiple land-use scenarios across the region. The findings offer critical guidance for policymakers, investors, and conservation organisations aiming to maximise environmental returns on limited funding.

Unlike previous studies that focused on single objectives, this research quantifies co-benefits—where actions to protect carbon also safeguard species. The results show that not all conservation approaches are equal; some deliver far greater value per hectare than others.

Methodology and Scope

The team combined high-resolution satellite data on forest cover and degradation with field surveys of species richness across the Brazilian Amazon, Peru, Colombia, and Bolivia. They modelled the impact of five common intervention types: creating protected areas, securing indigenous territories, restoring degraded pastures, implementing sustainable logging, and establishing payment for ecosystem services schemes. Each scenario was assessed for its effect on carbon storage and habitat integrity for vertebrates and plants.

Key Findings: What Works Best

According to the study, legal recognition and enforcement of indigenous lands emerged as the single most effective strategy. These territories not only prevent deforestation but also maintain contiguous habitat corridors that are vital for wide-ranging species like jaguars and tapirs. Strictly protected areas ranked second, particularly when located near agricultural frontiers where deforestation pressure is highest.

In contrast, restoring already-cleared lands delivered lower co-benefits per dollar spent. While restoration is necessary to meet global climate goals, the researchers argue that preventing further loss of intact forests is far more efficient in the short term.

Real-World Implications for Buyers and Policymakers

For carbon offset buyers, the study provides a clear hierarchy of project quality. Offsets generated from indigenous-led forest protection are likely to have higher additionality and lower leakage risk than those from restoration or improved forest management. Corporates with net-zero targets should scrutinise the source of their credits—not all tonnes are equal in biodiversity terms.

Governments and NGOs can use these findings to direct limited conservation budgets toward the highest-return activities. The research also supports calls for direct funding to indigenous communities, who have historically been underfunded despite being the most effective stewards.

Pros and Cons of Prioritising Co-Benefits

  • Pros: Maximises environmental return per dollar; aligns with global biodiversity and climate frameworks; strengthens indigenous rights; reduces risk of greenwashing in carbon markets.
  • Cons: May neglect restoration of already-degraded lands; could concentrate funding in specific regions, leaving other ecosystems underprotected; requires robust monitoring to verify outcomes.

Limitations and Future Research

The study acknowledges that its biodiversity metric focuses on vertebrates and woody plants, omitting insects and fungi that are crucial for ecosystem function. It also notes that enforcement of protected areas varies widely, affecting real-world outcomes. Future work should integrate socioeconomic data to understand trade-offs with local livelihoods.

Despite these caveats, the research provides a rigorous, data-driven framework for prioritising conservation actions. As the Amazon approaches a tipping point, such evidence is indispensable for avoiding irreversible loss.
