Machine Learning Predicts Lung Disease in Preterm Infants Within a Week

📅 2026-07-21 📂 Science Original source ↗
Key points
  • A machine learning model predicts bronchopulmonary dysplasia (BPD) in preterm infants within the first week.
  • The tool analyses clinical data from electronic health records of babies born before 32 weeks.
  • Early prediction allows for timely interventions, potentially reducing severity of chronic lung disease.

A new machine learning model can predict bronchopulmonary dysplasia (BPD) in preterm infants within the first week of life. The development, reported in the European Medical Journal (EMJ), offers a potential early intervention window for a condition that affects thousands of newborns in India each year.

How the Model Works

Researchers trained the algorithm on electronic health record data from infants born before 32 weeks of gestation. The model analyses clinical parameters available during the first seven days after birth. These include gestational age, birth weight, respiratory support needs, and oxygen saturation levels.

The tool then calculates a risk score for developing BPD. Unlike traditional scoring systems, this machine learning approach can identify subtle patterns in the data that human doctors might miss.

Why It Matters for Neonatal Care

BPD is a chronic lung disease that primarily affects premature babies who require mechanical ventilation or oxygen therapy. It can lead to long-term respiratory problems, neurodevelopmental delays, and increased healthcare costs. In India, neonatal intensive care units (NICUs) in major hospitals see dozens of such cases every month.

Current diagnostic methods often take weeks to confirm BPD. By that time, lung damage may already be significant. The new model's ability to flag high-risk infants within seven days gives neonatologists a critical head start.

Potential Applications in Indian Hospitals

Neonatologists in Indian tertiary-care hospitals could use this tool to stratify patients soon after birth. High-risk babies could receive targeted therapies such as caffeine citrate, vitamin A supplements, or less aggressive ventilation strategies earlier.

The model also reduces reliance on subjective clinical judgment. This is especially valuable in settings where experienced neonatologists are in short supply. A standardised, data-driven risk assessment can help junior doctors make quicker decisions.

However, researchers caution that the model needs validation on Indian patient populations. Most training data come from Western NICUs, where patient demographics and care protocols differ.

What Happens Next

The research team plans to conduct prospective clinical trials in multiple centres. If successful, the tool could be integrated into hospital electronic medical record systems. For now, it remains a promising prototype that could reshape how neonatal intensive care is delivered across the country.
