Your gut acts as a second brain, influencing mood and mental health: Neurologist explains

📅 2026-07-23 📂 Health Original source ↗
Key points
  • The enteric nervous system in the gut is often called the 'second brain' due to its complex neural network.
  • Gut microbes produce neurotransmitters like serotonin and dopamine that affect mood.
  • Disruptions in the gut microbiome are linked to stress, anxiety, and depression.
  • Researchers are exploring diet-based interventions to support mental health through the gut.
  • Experts advise a fibre-rich diet and probiotics to maintain a healthy gut-brain connection.

The hidden link between your gut and your brain

Your gut does far more than digest food. On World Brain Day, neurologists are highlighting a lesser-known fact: your digestive system acts as a second brain, quietly shaping your mood, stress levels and even your mental health.

This connection is not metaphorical. The gut contains the enteric nervous system, a complex network of over 100 million neurons lining the gastrointestinal tract. This is why some neuroscientists call it the 'second brain.'

Researchers at the University of Alberta are now wiretapping the conversation between the gut and the brain in hopes of changing how we approach healthy eating. Their work is part of a growing global effort to understand how the gut microbiome influences the mind.

How gut microbes talk to your brain

The gut is home to trillions of bacteria, fungi and other microbes collectively known as the gut microbiome. These microorganisms produce neurotransmitters — the same chemicals your brain uses to regulate mood.

Serotonin, the 'feel-good' chemical often linked to happiness, is primarily produced in the gut. Dopamine and gamma-aminobutyric acid (GABA), both involved in stress and anxiety regulation, are also manufactured by gut microbes.

These signalling molecules travel along the vagus nerve, a direct highway between the gut and the brain. Dr. Emeran Mayer, a neurologist and gut-brain researcher, has described the gut as 'the most underappreciated organ in mental health.'

Stress, anxiety and the microbiome

New research from News-Medical suggests that gut microbial metabolites may shape a person's vulnerability to stress-related mental disorders. An imbalance in gut bacteria — known as dysbiosis — has been linked to increased cortisol levels, anxiety and even depression.

A study covered by New Scientist found that exploring the gut microbiome could open new doors for supporting mental health. Some clinical trials are already testing probiotics — often called 'psychobiotics' — to treat mood disorders.

While the field is young, the evidence is compelling enough that several major universities, including the University of Arizona's College of Medicine in Tucson, are now running dedicated gut-brain research programmes.

What this means for your daily health

For the average person, the practical takeaway is simple: what you eat affects how you feel. A diet rich in fibre, fermented foods and diverse plant-based ingredients supports a healthy microbiome.

Probiotic foods like yoghurt, kimchi and kheer, as well as prebiotic foods like bananas, onions and whole grains, can help maintain the gut's microbial balance. Avoiding excessive processed sugar and antibiotics unless necessary also protects gut health.

Neurologists stress that this does not replace conventional mental health treatment. But the gut-brain axis offers a new, accessible way to support emotional well-being through daily habits.

What to watch next

Researchers are now testing whether personalised diets and microbiome-targeted therapies can help patients with depression and anxiety. The coming years may see doctors prescribing not just antidepressants, but also specific meal plans to treat the 'second brain.'
