India Approves 654MW Power Export to Flood-Hit Nepal Till Dec 31

Key points
  • India has approved 654MW of electricity exports to Nepal for 18 hours a day, running till December 31, 2026, routed through the Dhalkebar-Muzaffarpur cross-border link.

India Opens Emergency Power Corridor to Nepal

India has cleared an expedited export of 654MW of electricity to Nepal, to be supplied for 18 hours a day through December 31, 2026. The decision follows catastrophic flooding that knocked out 12 hydropower plants in Nepal, crippling a generation fleet that already leans heavily on run-of-river projects concentrated in the same river basins hit by the deluge.

The approval compresses a regulatory process that normally takes months of bilateral committee review into weeks. New Delhi invoked an emergency supply provision that allows exports above standing bilateral ceilings, clearing the way for state utilities and central generators to schedule additional capacity.

What Exactly Has Been Approved

The volumes flow through the Dhalkebar-Muzaffarpur cross-border transmission corridor, the primary high-voltage link between the two grids. Nepal's state utility, the Nepal Electricity Authority (NEA), will schedule the imports through existing power trading channels rather than a new dedicated contract.

Why the 18-Hour Window Matters

Nepal's grid load peaks twice daily, morning and evening, when residential demand and industrial operations overlap. Hydropower losses of this scale leave the NEA with limited levers: diesel backup is expensive per unit, and domestic solar cannot cover night-time peaks. An 18-hour supply window covers both peak blocks and most of the daytime industrial load.

The remaining six hours of low demand are expected to be met by surviving plants and residual imports. This is a deliberate scheduling choice, not a technical limitation of the corridor, which can carry higher volumes when required.

Cost, Tariff, and Commercial Reality

Cross-border power under such emergency arrangements is typically priced against the prevailing bilateral tariff structure, with any incremental energy charged at rates agreed between the NEA and Indian suppliers. Because the window extends into the winter months, Nepal avoids the worst-case scenario of buying spot power at elevated short-term market rates during a supply crunch.

For Indian generators, the deal absorbs surplus capacity that would otherwise sit idle or be sold into the domestic exchanges at lower clearing prices. The export is commercially rational on both sides, which is why clearance moved quickly.

Key Real-World Highlights

  • 654MW for 18 hours daily translates to roughly 11.8 million units of energy per day flowing into Nepal's grid.
  • The supply is strictly time-bound. It expires on December 31, 2026, and is not a permanent increase in Nepal's import quota.
  • Flood damage to 12 hydropower plants is the sole trigger; restoration progress will determine whether Nepal seeks an extension.
  • The Dhalkebar-Muzaffarpur corridor remains the single most critical artery for Nepal's cross-border electricity trade.
  • No new transmission infrastructure is needed, so supply can begin without construction delays.

What Happens After December 31

The arrangement is a bridge, not a fix. Nepal must either restore damaged plants, commission replacement capacity, or negotiate fresh import volumes before the deadline. Grid operators in both countries will watch reservoir levels and monsoon patterns through the post-flood recovery period.

For Indian exporters and traders, the episode reinforces a broader pattern: South Asian cross-border power is increasingly being used as disaster-response infrastructure, not just routine commerce. Utilities with spare thermal and hydro capacity near the border stand to gain recurring short-term contracts as climate-driven grid shocks become more frequent in the region.
