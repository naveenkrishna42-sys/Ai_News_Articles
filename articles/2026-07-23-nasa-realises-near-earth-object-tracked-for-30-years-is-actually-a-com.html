NASA Realises Near-Earth Object Tracked for 30 Years Is Actually a Comet

📅 2026-07-23 📂 Science Original source ↗
Key points
  • Object 1998 SH2 was classified as an asteroid after its discovery in 1998.
  • New observations revealed a faint coma and tail, confirming cometary activity.
  • The reclassification highlights the challenges in distinguishing asteroids from comets.
  • The object's orbit remains near-Earth, posing no threat to the planet.

A 30-Year Misidentification

For nearly three decades, NASA kept tabs on a space rock designated 1998 SH2, believing it was a near-Earth asteroid. The object was first spotted in 1998 and routinely cataloged as part of the agency's asteroid survey.

But fresh observations have now forced a complete rethink. The rock is not an asteroid at all. It is a comet.

Astronomers caught the object exhibiting a faint coma—a cloud of gas and dust—and a small tail. These are telltale signs of cometary activity that had gone unnoticed for 30 years.

What Changed the Classification

The discovery came when researchers turned more sensitive telescopes toward 1998 SH2. Earlier instruments had not been powerful enough to detect the thin veil of material surrounding its nucleus.

Once the coma and tail were spotted, the object's true nature became clear. Instead of a rocky asteroid, it turned out to be an icy comet that only becomes active when it nears the Sun.

NASA's study, published recently, confirms that the object is a 'dark comet'—a term used for comets that are difficult to spot because they reflect very little light until they heat up.

Why It Matters for Planetary Defense

This case underscores a blind spot in how space agencies track near-Earth objects. If a dark comet were on a collision course with Earth, it might escape detection until it is too late.

Comets are more unpredictable than asteroids. Their orbits can change due to outgassing—the release of gas and dust as they warm up. This makes trajectory calculations trickier.

By identifying 1998 SH2 as a comet, scientists can now refine its orbit and better understand its behavior. It poses no threat to Earth, but the lesson is clear: not every near-Earth object is what it seems.

The Challenge of Spotting Dark Comets

Dark comets are notoriously hard to detect. They lack the bright, reflective surfaces of typical asteroids and often only reveal themselves through their activity.

1998 SH2 is not an isolated case. Astronomers suspect there may be many more such objects hiding in plain sight—misclassified as asteroids because their cometary features are too faint for current surveys to catch.

Upcoming telescopes, like the Vera C. Rubin Observatory in Chile, are expected to change this. Their increased sensitivity will likely uncover many more dark comets in the years ahead.

For now, this reclassification serves as a reminder that the line between asteroids and comets can be blurry. A rock that sits quietly for decades can suddenly reveal itself to be something far more dynamic.
