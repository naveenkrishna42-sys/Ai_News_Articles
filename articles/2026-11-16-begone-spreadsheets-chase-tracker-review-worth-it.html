For a one-time, sub-$25 purchase, the Chase tracker delivers genuinely useful category breakdowns, annual-fee amortization math, and rewards tracking that would otherwise take hours of manual formula building. It loses points for requiring manual CSV imports and offering no live bank sync or mobile app.

Deal & Pricing Analysis

The newly released Begone, Spreadsheets Chase Tracker lands at a street price under $25, a one-time payment that undercuts most subscription budgeting tools within a single billing cycle. Historically, comparable personal-finance spreadsheet bundles from independent creators retail between $29 and $49, and premium templates with pre-built dashboard logic often exceed $60. At this price point, the tracker functions less like a software purchase and more like a productivity shortcut: the hours you would otherwise spend building pivot tables, category lookups, and fee-amortization formulas are delivered pre-configured. There is no recurring charge, no trial cliff, and no feature gating after purchase, which is a meaningful advantage over subscription rivals that raise rates annually. Buyers should note that the deal covers the template itself, not a data connection to Chase, so the value you receive scales directly with how consistently you import statements.

Real-World Performance & Daily Use

In daily use, the tracker's strongest quality is its restraint. The workflow is deliberately simple: download your Chase activity as a CSV, paste it into the import tab, and let the pre-written formulas sort transactions into categories, tally statement credits, and net out annual fees against earned rewards. Build quality is solid, with clearly labeled columns, protected formula ranges, and a dashboard that stays legible even with a full year of data. Battery and hardware concerns are irrelevant since the file runs in a browser or desktop spreadsheet app, though that also means there is no native mobile experience and no push notification when a charge posts. The most practical limitation is that reconciliation is only as current as your last import, so anyone expecting real-time balances will be disappointed. Users who already download statements monthly, however, will find the habit fits naturally into existing routine.

Competition & Market Alternatives

The closest rivals are Tiller Money, which automates bank feeds into spreadsheets at a recurring subscription cost, and full-featured apps such as YNAB and Monarch Money, which offer live syncing, mobile access, and debt-planning tools at $100 or more per year. Begone, Spreadsheets wins decisively on price and privacy, since no credential is ever shared. It loses on automation, depth of budgeting methodology, and cross-institution support, because it is purpose-built for Chase card spending rather than whole-finance management.

Who Should Buy This Deal

This tracker is ideal for Chase cardholders who want clearer visibility into rewards, annual fees, and category spend without subscribing to another monthly service or handing login credentials to a third party. It suits spreadsheet-comfortable users who import statements regularly and value a one-time purchase. Skip it if you need real-time sync, multi-bank aggregation, or a polished mobile app, and look instead at Tiller or Monarch Money.

Final Buying Verdict

Begone, Spreadsheets is a smart, low-risk buy for Chase cardholders who want structured rewards and fee tracking without a recurring subscription or shared login credentials. It is not a replacement for automated budgeting software, but at this price the template pays for itself quickly. Buy it if you already reconcile statements monthly; wait if you require real-time sync.
