Red Sea Pipeline Attack Unfolds

On 10 September 2026, three stations along Saudi Arabia’s 650‑km East‑West pipeline were damaged in an attack that halted flows from Yanbu to the Gulf. The incident coincided with the Red Sea’s strategic maritime corridor operating at peak season traffic, amplifying scrutiny on alternate shipping lines. Early assessments estimate repairs could take 4–6 weeks, causing a short‑term supply shock for 2.5 million barrels per day.

The attack also sparked a flare‑up of regional security concerns, prompting Saudi Aramco to announce a “no crude oil next month” stance to certain European refiners. The statement underscored the company’s focus on operational safety and recovery, signaling a temporary supply pause while the pipeline undergoes extensive reinforcement.

Aramco’s Supply Warning and Market Response

Aramco’s shift prompted immediate market repricing. Brent Futures closed 09/18 at $88.30 a barrel, a marginal uptick from the prior day’s $86.80, reflecting investors’ reassessment of supply curves. Asian spot markets remained largely flat, indicating that the short‑term disruption is perceived as localized.

European refiners, reliant on the Yanbu terminal for processed feedstock, are recalibrating logistics. Some are turning to the Yanbu‑Sohar alternative route—doubling transport distance to 1,200 km—and adding a $3.5 billion capital outlay for pipeline upgrades and security upgrades.

Alternative Routes and Regional Implications

The Yanbu‑Sohar corridor, running through western Saudi Arabia and Kuwait, offers a 50‑per‑cent longer haul but benefits from a secured 600‑km segment and the ability to tap into Gulf tanker throughput. Refining hubs along the Gulf are now diverting a larger share of shipments to mitigate exposure.

Moreover, Gulf states have announced contingency plans to accelerate builds of the Red Line pipeline network, which serves as a benchmark for resilient supply chains. The shift is expected to boost regional pipeline construction to 1.2 mtpa over the next three years, as per Gulf Cooperation Council economic forecasts.
