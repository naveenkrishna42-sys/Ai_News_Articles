Crypto needs clarity, not the CLARITY Act: an opinion

📅 2026-07-21 📂 Crypto Original source ↗
Key points
  • The CLARITY Act is criticised for adding complexity rather than providing clear rules for crypto.
  • Stakeholders say the bill's definitions and compliance burdens are vague and contradictory.
  • The author argues for a single, simple regulatory framework instead of layered legislation.

India's cryptocurrency sector has been waiting for regulatory clarity for years. The government's latest attempt, a bill dubbed the CLARITY Act, was supposed to provide that clarity. Instead, it has left many in the industry more confused than before.

The CLARITY Act, which stands for Cryptocurrency and Legal Asset Regulation and Identification of Transactions Y Act, is being debated in Parliament. Industry insiders and legal experts have pointed out that the bill's definitions are vague, its compliance requirements overlap with existing laws, and it creates new ambiguities about what is allowed and what isn't.

What the CLARITY Act proposes

The bill attempts to classify digital assets into three categories: crypto securities, crypto commodities, and utility tokens. Each category would fall under a different regulator — the Securities and Exchange Board of India, the Forward Markets Commission, or a new yet-to-be-formed Digital Assets Authority.

This three-regulator approach has been criticised as impractical. Startups and exchanges would have to deal with multiple agencies, each with its own registration process, reporting standards, and tax rules. For a sector that thrives on speed and innovation, this layered oversight is seen as a burden.

Why the industry is unhappy

Several crypto exchanges and industry bodies have submitted responses to the government's consultation paper. Their main complaint is that the CLARITY Act does not address the core issue — whether crypto is legal or illegal. Instead, it assumes legality but creates so many hurdles that compliance becomes nearly impossible for smaller players.

For example, the bill requires every crypto transaction above a certain threshold to be reported to the Financial Intelligence Unit within 24 hours. Critics argue that this is impractical for peer-to-peer trades and decentralised finance platforms, where transaction data may not be easily accessible.

Another contentious clause is the requirement for all crypto companies to maintain a physical office in India with at least two directors who are resident Indians. This provision, the industry says, could force many startups to relocate or shut down.

What the government wants

The government's stated goal is to prevent money laundering and protect retail investors. The CLARITY Act includes know-your-customer norms, transaction reporting, and penalties for non-compliance. Officials have said that the bill is not meant to stifle innovation but to bring order to a chaotic market.

However, the opinion piece argues that the way to bring order is not through a complex, multi-regulator bill. Instead, the government should consider a single, simple law that clearly states what is legal, what is not, and who regulates what. The author points to the success of the Information Technology Act in providing a clear baseline for digital transactions.

The need for simplicity

The crypto industry in India has grown despite regulatory uncertainty. Many startups have moved to friendlier jurisdictions like Singapore or Dubai. A clear, predictable law could bring them back. But the CLARITY Act, with its multiple layers and vague definitions, is likely to push more businesses away.

The author concludes that what the sector needs is not another acronym-laden bill but a straightforward piece of legislation that says: here are the rules, here is the regulator, here is what you must do. Anything less is just noise.

The bill is still in the consultation stage. The government has not set a timeline for its introduction in Parliament. Whether the feedback from industry will lead to changes remains to be seen.
