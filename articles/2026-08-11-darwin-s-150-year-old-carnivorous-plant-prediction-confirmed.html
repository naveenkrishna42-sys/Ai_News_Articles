Darwin's 150-Year-Old Carnivorous Plant Prediction Confirmed

📅 2026-08-11 📂 Science Original source ↗
Key points
  • Researchers identified carnivory in Saxifraga using multidisciplinary evidence.
  • The finding confirms Charles Darwin's 1875 prediction about sticky-haired plants.
  • The alpine flower is an unexpected addition to the world's carnivorous plants list.

A 150-Year-Old Botanical Mystery Solved

In 1875, Charles Darwin suggested that some plants could capture insects using sticky hairs. At the time, he couldn't prove it. Now, a team of scientists has confirmed his prediction, adding an unlikely new member to the world's carnivorous plants.

The plant in question is Saxifraga, a flowering genus commonly found in alpine regions. Researchers employed multidisciplinary evidence, including field observations, genetic analysis, and laboratory experiments, to establish its carnivorous nature.

What Darwin Suspected, Science Now Shows

Darwin's original hypothesis focused on plants with glandular hairs that trap prey. He theorised that these sticky surfaces could absorb nutrients from insects, supplementing poor soil conditions. Saxifraga fits this description perfectly, with its leaves covered in adhesive trichomes.

Modern tools allowed scientists to go beyond Darwin's limitations. They observed insects getting stuck on the plant's hairs, then measured nitrogen uptake from the decaying prey. The results were conclusive.

An Unlikely Addition to the Carnivorous Club

Carnivorous plants are typically associated with bogs and tropical jungles. Think Venus flytraps, pitcher plants, or sundews. Saxifraga, a hardy alpine flower, was not on anyone's radar.

Its inclusion expands our understanding of how plants adapt to nutrient-poor environments. The study, published in Nature, highlights how overlooked species can hide surprising ecological strategies.

Why This Discovery Matters

This finding validates a century-and-a-half-old scientific intuition. It also underscores the importance of revisiting historical hypotheses with modern techniques.

For conservationists, it means protecting alpine habitats becomes even more critical. These ecosystems may harbour more carnivorous species than previously thought.

Scientists are now planning to survey other sticky-haired plants in similar environments. They believe more carnivorous species could be lurking in plain sight.

What remains clear is that Darwin's insight was remarkably ahead of its time. As one researcher put it, the natural world still holds surprises that even great minds could only hint at.

The next step is to map the full distribution of Saxifraga's carnivorous traits. That could take years, but the groundwork is laid.
