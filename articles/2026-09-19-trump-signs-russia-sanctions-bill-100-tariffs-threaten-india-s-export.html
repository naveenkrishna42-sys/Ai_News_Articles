Trump Signs Russia Sanctions Bill: What the Law Actually Does

US President Donald Trump has signed the Russia sanctions bill into law, closing a legislative window that had kept global energy traders guessing for weeks. The statute grants the White House authority to impose secondary sanctions and tariffs of up to 100 percent on imports from countries that continue purchasing Russian crude oil. India and China are the two largest buyers named in the political debate surrounding the bill.

For Indian businesses, the headline is not the sanctions text itself but the tariff trigger buried inside it. The law does not automatically impose duties. It arms the US President with discretionary power, which means the real question is enforcement, not intent.

Why India Is Squarely in the Firing Line

India has been the largest seaborne buyer of discounted Russian crude since 2022, with refiners in Jamnagar, Vadinar, and Paradip processing millions of barrels monthly. That discount, often USD 8-15 per barrel against Brent, has been a structural cushion for India's current account deficit and retail fuel prices.

A 100 percent tariff on Indian exports would hit roughly USD 87 billion in annual goods shipments to the US, based on recent commerce ministry data. Textiles, shrimp, gems and jewellery, pharmaceuticals, and engineering goods are the most exposed categories.

Exporter Reality Check: Margins, Not Politics

Tiruppur textile units, Andhra Pradesh shrimp farms, and Surat diamond polishers operate on thin single-digit margins. A 100 percent duty does not reduce profits; it eliminates the order entirely. American buyers would shift sourcing to Vietnam, Bangladesh, and Mexico within one or two shipping cycles.

Exporters quoted in Indian media have flagged that even the threat of tariffs disrupts forward contracts. Buyers delay purchase orders, demand indemnity clauses, and renegotiate prices. That uncertainty costs money before any duty is actually levied.

Key Real-World Highlights

  • Tariff power is discretionary, so diplomatic negotiation still matters more than the statute's text.
  • Indian refiners must weigh Russian crude discounts against US market access for finished goods.
  • Small and medium exporters lack the balance sheet to absorb even a 25 percent duty, let alone 100 percent.
  • Pharmaceutical and IT services exports remain less exposed than physical goods trade.
  • Retaliatory or diversified trade pacts with the EU and Gulf nations become commercially urgent.

What Buyers and Importers Should Watch

US importers sourcing from India should model three scenarios: no enforcement, partial tariffs on select categories, and full 100 percent duties. The middle scenario is the most likely near-term outcome, since blanket tariffs would raise US consumer prices on apparel, seafood, and generic drugs.

Indian suppliers should document Russian crude sourcing through transparent refining chains and explore blended feedstock strategies. Compliance paperwork and origin certification will become as commercially important as production capacity.

The Bottom Line for Indian Trade

The signed law converts a political threat into a legal instrument. India's response will likely combine diplomatic engagement, continued Russian crude purchases at moderated volumes, and accelerated trade diversification. For exporters, the practical advice is blunt: stress-test contracts, hedge currency and tariff risk, and prepare for a period where Washington's discretion, not market demand, decides order books.
