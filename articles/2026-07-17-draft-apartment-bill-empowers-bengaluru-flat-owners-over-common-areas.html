Draft Apartment Bill empowers Bengaluru flat owners over common areas

📅 2026-07-17 📂 Bengaluru Original source ↗
Key points
  • Flat owners in Bengaluru would get greater control over common areas under a proposed law.
  • The draft bill mandates separate property titles for common areas, preventing builders from selling or mortgaging them.
  • The bill aims to resolve disputes over maintenance and usage of amenities in apartment complexes.

Flat owners in Bengaluru may soon have a stronger legal say over the common areas in their apartment complexes. A draft Apartment Bill, reported by The Hindu, proposes sweeping changes to how ownership and control of shared spaces are defined. This comes as a relief for countless residents who have faced disputes with builders over parking lots, gardens, and clubhouses.

The bill, currently in its draft stage, aims to bring clarity to a murky area of property law. Common areas—including staircases, lifts, terraces, and playgrounds—are often controlled by developers long after flats are sold. The new legislation would change that.

Clearer titles for shared spaces

One of the most significant provisions is the requirement for a separate title deed for common areas. Under the draft, builders must register these spaces in the name of the apartment owners' association before selling any individual flat. This prevents developers from later selling or mortgaging common facilities.

Currently, many apartment buyers discover that the swimming pool or gym they were promised is not legally theirs. The draft bill makes such practices illegal and gives associations the power to recover unlawfully sold common areas. Legal experts say this could reduce litigation that has clogged consumer courts.

Mandating association formation

The draft also mandates the formation of a residents' association within three months of the first flat sale. This association would have the legal standing to sue builders for non-compliance. In many Bengaluru apartments, associations exist only on paper, with builders retaining de facto control.

By law, the builder must hand over all common areas to the association within six months of obtaining an occupancy certificate. Non-compliance would attract penalties, though specific fines have not been detailed in the current draft.

Maintenance and usage disputes

Another key area the bill addresses is the maintenance of common areas. Owners often face arbitrary increases in maintenance charges or restrictions on using amenities. The draft bill lays out a framework for transparent accounting and dispute resolution.

It requires associations to maintain audited accounts and hold annual general meetings. Flat owners would have the right to inspect all records. If disputes arise, the bill proposes a fast-track mechanism through the state's real estate regulatory authority.

Reaction from stakeholders

Residents' welfare groups have largely welcomed the draft. A representative of the Bangalore Apartments' Federation said it addresses long-standing grievances. Builders, however, have expressed concerns about the burden of compliance and potential delays in project completion.

Officials have not yet confirmed when the bill will be tabled in the state legislature. The government is expected to invite public comments before finalising the draft.

What happens next? The bill's journey through the legislative process will be closely watched by lakhs of apartment owners in Bengaluru. If passed, it could become a model for other Indian cities grappling with similar disputes. For now, flat owners can only wait—and hope—for the law to tip the scales in their favour.
